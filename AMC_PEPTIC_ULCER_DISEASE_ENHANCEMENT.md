## Gastric vs Duodenal Ulcers: Critical Timing Distinction {#gastric-vs-duodenal-ulcers}

### [⭐⭐⭐ HIGH-YIELD FOR AMC] This timing distinction is frequently tested

**GASTRIC ULCERS:**
- **Pain timing:** IMMEDIATELY after eating
- **Malignancy risk:** CAN progress to gastric adenocarcinoma
- **Investigation:** Requires endoscopy with biopsy to rule out cancer
- **Demographics:** Often elderly or chronic NSAID users
- **Location:** Usually lesser curvature of stomach

**DUODENAL ULCERS:**
- **Pain timing:** 2-3 HOURS after eating
- **Malignancy risk:** Does NOT become malignant
- **Investigation:** Can manage empirically if no red flags
- **Demographics:** More common (4:1 ratio vs gastric), younger patients
- **Location:** First part of duodenum

### Clinical Pearl - Dr. Amir's Mnemonic

> **"G for Gastric = Goes with food (immediate pain)"**
> **"D for Duodenal = Delayed (2-3 hours)"**

### Why This Matters for AMC Exams

1. **Diagnostic Accuracy:** Correctly differentiating based on timing shows clinical reasoning
2. **Investigation Urgency:** Gastric ulcers require urgent endoscopy (malignancy risk)
3. **Patient Education:** Explains why some patients need endoscopy and others don't
4. **Safety-Netting:** Appropriate referral pathways depend on ulcer type

### History Taking Question (Critical)

**Always ask:** *"How long after eating does your pain start?"*

- **Immediate → Gastric ulcer**
- **2-3 hours later → Duodenal ulcer**
- **No relationship to eating → Consider alternative diagnosis (GORD, functional dyspepsia)**

## NSAID-Induced Peptic Ulcer Disease {#nsaid-induced-pud}

### [⭐⭐⭐ HIGH-YIELD FOR AMC] NSAIDs are the #1 modifiable risk factor

### Mechanism of Injury

**COX-1 Inhibition Pathway:**
1. NSAIDs inhibit cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) enzyme
2. Reduced prostaglandin E2 production
3. Decreased gastric mucus secretion
4. Decreased bicarbonate secretion
5. Reduced mucosal blood flow
6. **Result:** Loss of protective gastric mucosal barrier

**Direct Toxicity:**
- NSAIDs are weak acids that accumulate in gastric mucosa
- Direct cellular injury independent of systemic effects
- Increased gastric acid secretion
- Impaired ulcer healing

### High-Risk NSAIDs (Australian Market)

| Brand Name (Australian) | Generic Name | Risk Level | Notes |
|-------------------------|--------------|------------|-------|
| **Nurofen** | Ibuprofen | High | Most commonly used OTC NSAID |
| **Voltaren** | Diclofenac | Very High | Highest GI risk among NSAIDs |
| **Naprosyn** | Naproxen | High | Long half-life increases risk |
| **Aspirin** | Acetylsalicylic acid | Moderate-High | Even low-dose (100mg) for cardiac protection |
| **Celebrex** | Celecoxib | Lower | COX-2 selective, reduced GI risk |

### Risk Factors for NSAID-Induced PUD

**Major Risk Factors:**
- Age >60 years
- History of peptic ulcer disease or GI bleeding
- Concomitant use of:
  - Corticosteroids (prednisolone)
  - Anticoagulants (warfarin, DOACs)
  - Antiplatelet agents (clopidogrel, aspirin)
  - SSRIs (increase bleeding risk)
- High-dose or multiple NSAID use
- Duration >3 months

**Moderate Risk Factors:**
- H. pylori infection (synergistic effect with NSAIDs)
- Smoking
- Alcohol consumption
- Chronic kidney disease

### Management: The MOST IMPORTANT Step

**1. CEASE NSAID IMMEDIATELY**
- This is **non-negotiable** - ulcer will not heal with continued NSAID use
- Even PPI co-therapy cannot fully protect against continued NSAID-induced damage

**2. Switch to SAFER Alternative**

| Medication | Brand Name (Australian) | Safety Profile | Dosing |
|------------|------------------------|----------------|--------|
| **Paracetamol** | Panadol | ✅ Safe for gastric mucosa | 1g QID (max 4g/day) |
| Tramadol | Tramal | ✅ Safe (opioid, not NSAID) | 50-100mg QID PRN |
| Topical NSAIDs | Voltaren Emulgel | ✅ Minimal systemic absorption | Apply TDS-QID |

**3. If NSAID Absolutely Essential**
- Use **lowest effective dose** for **shortest duration**
- Co-prescribe PPI (omeprazole 20mg daily or pantoprazole 40mg daily)
- Consider COX-2 selective agents (celecoxib) - lower GI risk
- Regular monitoring for symptoms

### Patient Education Script (AMC Communication Skill)

> "The ibuprofen you're taking (Nurofen) is actually causing your stomach ulcer. NSAIDs like ibuprofen reduce the protective lining in your stomach, which allows acid to damage the stomach wall. We need to **stop the ibuprofen immediately** and switch to **paracetamol (Panadol)**, which is much safer for your stomach. Paracetamol works just as well for pain but doesn't have the same effect on your stomach lining."

### AMC Exam Pitfall

**❌ Common Mistake:** Candidate prescribes PPI but fails to address NSAID cessation
**✅ Correct Approach:** **"First and most important: stop the NSAIDs. Then start PPI therapy."**

## Australian OTC Medications {#australian-otc-medications}

### [⭐⭐ IMPORTANT FOR AMC] Patients use brand names, not generic names

### Why This Matters

AMC Clinical Exam uses **realistic patient scenarios** where patients say:
- ❌ NOT: "I'm taking calcium carbonate antacids"
- ✅ INSTEAD: "I've been taking Quickies for the pain"

**You must recognize Australian medication brands** to:
1. Understand what the patient is actually taking
2. Assess appropriateness of self-medication
3. Provide accurate medication counselling
4. Demonstrate cultural competency in Australian healthcare context

### Complete Australian OTC GI Medications Reference

| Brand Name | Generic/Type | Mechanism | PBS Code | When Used | Effectiveness for PUD |
|------------|--------------|-----------|----------|-----------|----------------------|
| **Quickies** | Calcium carbonate antacid | Neutralizes stomach acid | OTC | Fast relief of heartburn, indigestion | ✓ Temporary symptom relief only |
| **Gaviscon** | Alginate-antacid combination | Forms protective foam barrier on stomach | OTC | GORD, heartburn | ✓ Better for reflux than PUD |
| **Mylanta** | Aluminium/magnesium hydroxide antacid | Neutralizes acid, coats stomach | OTC | Heartburn, gastritis | ✓ Temporary relief |
| **Renn** ies** | Calcium carbonate + magnesium | Dual-action antacid | OTC | Indigestion, heartburn | ✓ Fast-acting relief |
| **Panadol** | Paracetamol 500mg | Analgesic (NOT anti-inflammatory) | PBS listed | Pain relief | ✅ SAFE - does not cause ulcers |
| **Nurofen** | Ibuprofen 200-400mg | NSAID - COX inhibitor | PBS listed | Pain, inflammation | ❌ CAUSES ulcers - MUST cease |
| **Somac** | Pantoprazole 20mg | Proton pump inhibitor (PPI) | 8240K | Acid suppression | ✅✅ Most effective for PUD |
| **Nexium** | Esomeprazole 20mg | Proton pump inhibitor (PPI) | 8139R | Acid suppression | ✅✅ Most effective for PUD |

### Clinical Scenarios - How Patients Present

**Scenario 1: Antacid Overuse**
> **Patient:** "I've been taking Quickies 5-6 times a day for the last month."
> **Clinical Significance:** Symptoms not controlled by antacids → needs investigation
> **Your Response:** "Quickies give temporary relief but don't treat the underlying ulcer. We need stronger medication (PPI) and to investigate the cause."

**Scenario 2: NSAID Misunderstanding**
> **Patient:** "I take Panadol for headaches and Nurofen for my back pain."
> **Clinical Significance:** Patient doesn't realize Nurofen is causing the ulcer
> **Your Response:** "Nurofen (ibuprofen) is actually contributing to your stomach ulcer. Let's switch everything to Panadol, which is safe for your stomach."

**Scenario 3: Self-Medication with PPIs**
> **Patient:** "I bought Somac from the chemist and it's helped a bit."
> **Clinical Significance:** Symptom improvement but no investigation of underlying cause
> **Your Response:** "Somac is helping by reducing acid, which is good. But we still need to test for H. pylori and make sure there's no underlying problem like an ulcer that needs specific treatment."

### PBS (Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme) Notes

**PPIs for Peptic Ulcer Disease:**
- **Initial 4-8 week trial:** Can be prescribed without authority
- **Ongoing/maintenance therapy:** Requires **Streamlined Authority (code 4497)**
- **Authority criteria:** Documented peptic ulcer disease or H. pylori eradication

**Antacids:**
- All OTC (over-the-counter) - not PBS listed
- Patients self-purchase from pharmacy or supermarket
- No prescription required

### AMC Exam Tips

1. **If patient says medication name you don't recognize:**
   - ASK: "Can you tell me what that medication is for?"
   - Usually context clues: "I take Quickies when my stomach hurts" → antacid

2. **Always clarify NSAID use:**
   - "Are you taking any pain killers like Nurofen, Voltaren, or similar?"
   - Many patients don't consider OTC NSAIDs as "real medications"

3. **Brand name vs generic:**
   - Use brand names when talking to patients: "Stop the Nurofen, switch to Panadol"
   - Use generic names in medical documentation: "Cease ibuprofen, commence paracetamol"

## Red Flag Assessment for Upper GI Pain {#red-flag-assessment}

### [⭐⭐⭐ HIGH-YIELD] Dr. Amir Emphasis: "I'm emphasizing a lot on gastric cancers"

### Why Red Flags Matter

**Gastric ulcers CAN become malignant** → Must aggressively screen for cancer features
**Missed gastric cancer = catastrophic outcome** → Zero tolerance for inadequate screening

### Complete Red Flag Checklist

| Red Flag | Significance | Action Required | Timeframe |
|----------|--------------|-----------------|-----------|
| **Hematemesis** (vomiting blood or coffee-ground material) | Upper GI bleeding - potentially life-threatening | Immediate hospital referral, NBM, IV access, urgent endoscopy | **Emergency - same day** |
| **Melena** (black, tarry, foul-smelling stools) | Upper GI bleeding - significant blood loss (>50-100mL) | Urgent endoscopy, FBC, consider admission | **Urgent - within 24 hours** |
| **Dysphagia** (difficulty swallowing) | Possible gastric cancer, oesophageal cancer, or stricture | Urgent endoscopy referral | **Within 2 weeks** |
| **Odynophagia** (painful swallowing) | Severe oesophagitis, cancer, or infection | Urgent endoscopy referral | **Within 2 weeks** |
| **Unintentional weight loss** (>5kg in 6 months or >10% body weight) | Red flag for malignancy | Urgent endoscopy referral | **Within 2 weeks** |
| **Loss of appetite** (anorexia) | Associated with gastric cancer, chronic illness | Urgent investigation | **Within 2 weeks** |
| **Persistent vomiting** | Gastric outlet obstruction, malignancy | Urgent assessment | **Within 1 week** |
| **Age >55 with new-onset dyspepsia** | Increased gastric cancer risk (age-related) | Direct endoscopy referral (NO empirical PPI trial) | **Within 2 weeks** |
| **Family history of gastric cancer** | Hereditary gastric cancer syndromes (rare) | Earlier endoscopy threshold | Case-dependent |
| **Night pain waking patient** | Specific for peptic ulcer (acid secretion peaks at night) | Supports PUD diagnosis, not a cancer red flag | Routine investigation |
| **Palpable abdominal mass** | Advanced malignancy | Urgent CT abdomen, gastroenterology referral | **Within 1 week** |

### Red Flag Questioning Script (AMC Communication)

**Systematic Approach - Dr. Amir Method:**

> **"I need to ask some important questions to make sure there's nothing more serious going on:"**

1. **Bleeding Questions:**
   - "Have you vomited any blood or material that looks like coffee grounds?"
   - "Have you noticed any dark, black, or tarry stools that smell bad?"
   - "Any bright red blood when you go to the toilet?"

2. **Malignancy Questions:**
   - "Have you lost any weight without trying?" (Quantify: "How much over what time?")
   - "How's your appetite been? Are you eating normally?"
   - "Any difficulty or pain when you swallow?"
   - "Does anyone in your family have a history of stomach or bowel cancer?"

3. **Severity Questions:**
   - "Is the pain waking you up at night?"
   - "Have you had any fever or feeling generally unwell?"
   - "Is the pain getting worse despite the antacids you're taking?"

### Clinical Decision Making Based on Red Flags

**IF RED FLAGS PRESENT:**
```
1. Do NOT start empirical PPI therapy without investigation
2. Direct referral for urgent endoscopy (within 2 weeks)
3. Arrange baseline investigations (FBC, iron studies, LFTs)
4. Safety-netting advice: "If symptoms worsen or you develop vomiting/bleeding, go to emergency"
```

**IF NO RED FLAGS:**
```
1. Empirical management appropriate:
   - Cease NSAIDs
   - Start PPI therapy
   - H. pylori testing
   - Review at 4-8 weeks
2. Endoscopy if symptoms persist despite treatment
```

### AMC Exam Pitfall

**❌ Common Mistake:** Rushed red flag screening - asks only 1-2 questions
**✅ Correct Approach:** **Systematic screening of ALL red flags** - demonstrates thoroughness and patient safety focus

### Dr. Amir Teaching Point

> "When I have an upper abdomen pain case, especially if there's a possibility of peptic ulcer disease, I'm **emphasizing a lot on gastric cancers**. You MUST ask about dark stools, vomiting blood, difficulty swallowing, and weight loss. These are non-negotiable questions."

## Dr. Amir's Differential-Driven Approach for Upper Abdominal Pain {#differential-driven-approach}

### [⭐⭐ IMPORTANT] This is MORE EFFICIENT than generic history-taking

### Traditional Approach (Less Efficient)

```
1. Take complete generic history (all systems, all questions)
2. Examine patient fully
3. Review findings
4. THEN think about diagnosis
❌ Problem: Time-consuming, unfocused, may miss key features
```

### Dr. Amir's Differential-Driven Approach (AMC-Preferred)

```
1. **LOCATION FIRST** → Immediate differential list
2. **CHARACTER** → Narrows differential
3. **TIMING & ASSOCIATIONS** → Further refinement
4. **RED FLAGS** → Immediate recognition
5. **TARGETED QUESTIONS** → Confirm/exclude specific diagnoses
✅ Advantage: Efficient, focused, clinically safe
```

### Step-by-Step Framework

#### **STEP 1: Location Defines Differential Groups**

**Left Upper Quadrant / Epigastric Pain → 3 Main Groups:**

1. **Peptic/Reflux Group**
   - Peptic ulcer disease (gastric or duodenal)
   - Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GORD)
   - Gastritis

2. **Pancreatic Group**
   - Pancreatitis (acute or chronic)
   - Pancreatic cancer

3. **Hepatobiliary Group**
   - Cholecystitis (inflamed gallbladder)
   - Cholangitis (bile duct infection)
   - Biliary colic (gallstone pain)
   - Hepatitis
   - Hepatocellular carcinoma

**Plus Must-Not-Miss:**
- Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) - can present as epigastric pain
- Lower lobe pneumonia - can cause upper abdominal pain

#### **STEP 2: Character Further Refines**

| Pain Character | Suggests | Excludes |
|----------------|----------|----------|
| **Dull ache** | Peptic ulcer, chronic inflammation | Perforation (would be severe/sharp) |
| **Burning** | GORD, gastritis | Biliary colic (colicky not burning) |
| **Severe/sharp** | Perforation, acute pancreatitis, ACS | Functional dyspepsia (milder) |
| **Colicky** (comes and goes in waves) | Biliary colic, renal colic | PUD (constant/intermittent but not waves) |

#### **STEP 3: Timing is Diagnostic**

| Timing Relationship | Diagnosis | Mechanism |
|---------------------|-----------|-----------|
| **Immediately after eating** | **Gastric ulcer** | Food stretches stomach → acid on ulcer → pain |
| **2-3 hours after eating** | **Duodenal ulcer** | Acid passes from stomach to duodenum → pain |
| **After fatty meals** | **Biliary colic** | Gallbladder contracts to digest fat → pain if stone present |
| **After alcohol binge** | **Pancreatitis** | Alcohol toxic to pancreas |
| **Worse lying flat** | **GORD** | Gravity allows acid reflux when supine |
| **Relieved by leaning forward** | **Pancreatitis** | Position reduces pancreatic stretch |

#### **STEP 4: Associated Features Clinch Diagnosis**

**FOR PEPTIC ULCER:**
- Relieved by antacids ✓
- NSAID use ✓
- Smoking/alcohol ✓
- No jaundice, no fever → excludes hepatobiliary/infection

**FOR GORD:**
- Heartburn (retrosternal burning) ✓
- Bitter taste in mouth ✓
- Waterbrash (sudden saliva increase) ✓
- Worse lying flat ✓

**FOR PANCREATITIS:**
- Pain radiates to back ✓
- Nausea and vomiting ✓
- Alcohol consumption ✓
- Pain relieved by leaning forward ✓

### Example Clinical Reasoning (Dr. Amir's Approach)

**Patient Presentation:** 32-year-old with dull ache in left upper quadrant, worse after eating, taking Nurofen regularly

**Dr. Amir's Thought Process (Verbalized for AMC):**

> **Step 1 - Location:** "Left upper quadrant pain → I'm thinking peptic ulcer, GORD, or pancreatitis as my main groups."
>
> **Step 2 - Character:** "Dull ache → suggests chronic inflammation like peptic ulcer, less likely acute perforation or severe pancreatitis."
>
> **Step 3 - Timing:** "Worse after eating → peptic ulcer most likely. I need to ask specifically: how SOON after eating? If immediate → gastric ulcer. If 2-3 hours later → duodenal ulcer."
>
> **Step 4 - Risk Factors:** "Taking Nurofen regularly → NSAIDs are major risk factor for peptic ulcer. This supports my hypothesis."
>
> **Step 5 - Associated Features:** "Relieved by antacids → acid-related pathology, consistent with PUD. No vomiting, no fever → excludes complicated ulcer or infection."
>
> **Step 6 - Red Flags:** "No melena, no hematemesis, no weight loss, no dysphagia → no features of gastric cancer. But I still need to ask these systematically."
>
> **Conclusion:** "Most likely diagnosis is **NSAID-induced peptic ulcer disease**. Need to cease Nurofen, start PPI, test for H. pylori."

### Comparison: Generic vs Differential-Driven

| Approach | Time Efficiency | Diagnostic Accuracy | AMC Examiner Impression |
|----------|-----------------|---------------------|------------------------|
| **Generic History** | Slow (may run out of time) | Good (if complete) | "Thorough but unfocused" |
| **Differential-Driven** | Fast (targeted questions) | Excellent (if systematic) | "Clinically reasoning, efficient, safe" |

### AMC Exam Application

**When presenting to examiner, use this structure:**

> "Based on the **location** (epigastric pain), **character** (dull ache), **timing** (worse after eating), and **risk factors** (NSAID use), my primary diagnosis is **peptic ulcer disease**, most likely NSAID-induced.
>
> The **timing** of pain - specifically how soon after eating - will help me distinguish gastric versus duodenal ulcer, which affects investigation urgency since gastric ulcers can become malignant.
>
> I've ruled out red flags for gastric cancer (no weight loss, no dysphagia, no bleeding).
>
> Alternative diagnoses I'm considering include GORD and gastritis, but the relationship to eating and NSAID use make peptic ulcer most likely.
>
> Must-not-miss diagnoses would be gastric cancer (ruled out by absence of red flags) and perforated ulcer (ruled out by examination - no peritonism)."

## Summary: Key Takeaways for AMC Clinical Exam

### Critical Teaching Points (Dr. Amir Emphasis)

1. **Gastric vs Duodenal Timing:**
   - Gastric: IMMEDIATE pain after eating
   - Duodenal: 2-3 HOURS after eating
   - Gastric CAN become malignant; Duodenal CANNOT

2. **NSAID Cessation is PARAMOUNT:**
   - Most important management step
   - Switch to Panadol (paracetamol) - safe alternative
   - Even with PPI, continued NSAID use prevents healing

3. **Red Flag Screening is NON-NEGOTIABLE:**
   - Hematemesis, melena, dysphagia, weight loss
   - Gastric cancers are a priority concern in upper GI pain
   - Age >55 with new dyspepsia → direct to endoscopy

4. **Australian Medication Knowledge:**
   - Quickies = antacid
   - Nurofen = ibuprofen (CAUSES ulcers)
   - Panadol = paracetamol (SAFE)
   - Gaviscon = antacid-alginate

5. **Differential-Driven Approach:**
   - Location → Character → Timing → Associations → Red Flags
   - More efficient than generic history-taking
   - Demonstrates clinical reasoning to examiners
