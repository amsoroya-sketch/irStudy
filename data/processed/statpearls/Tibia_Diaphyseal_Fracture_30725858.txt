# Tibia Diaphyseal Fracture

**Authors:** Ahmed Mabrouk, Alex Jahangir

**Source:** StatPearls - StatPearls Publishing

## Abstract

Tibial shaft or diaphyseal fractures account for a substantial proportion of long-bone injuries in adults, with an incidence of approximately 16.9 per 100,000 persons per year, peaking among male individuals aged 10 to 20 years. These fractures frequently result from high-energy mechanisms and are particularly susceptible to open injury due to the tibia’s subcutaneous anteromedial border. The lower leg is organized into 4 distinct fascial compartments, an anatomical configuration that predisposes patients to acute compartment syndrome, especially following high-energy or crush trauma. Timely recognition and decompression via fasciotomy are essential to prevent irreversible neurovascular compromise and soft tissue necrosis. Initial evaluation requires standard anteroposterior and lateral radiographs to confirm the diagnosis and assess alignment, fracture morphology, and joint involvement. Nonoperative management is appropriate for minimally displaced or stable fractures, whereas displaced, unstable, or open fractures generally require operative fixation to restore alignment, facilitate union, and reduce complications. Intramedullary nailing (IMN) represents the gold standard for most tibial shaft fractures due to its biomechanical stability and minimally invasive technique. Alternative interventions, including open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) or external fixation, are indicated in cases involving complex fracture patterns, soft tissue compromise, or periarticular extension.
