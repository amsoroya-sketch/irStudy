# Episiotomy

**Authors:** Kyle Barjon, Elsa S. Vadakekut, Heba Mahdy

**Source:** StatPearls - StatPearls Publishing

## Abstract

An episiotomy, a surgical incision made at the end of the second stage of labor to widen the vaginal opening, helps facilitate delivery. Ideally, an episiotomy relieves pressure on the perineum during difficult deliveries, resulting in an easily repairable incision when compared to uncontrolled vaginal trauma. The primary types of episiotomy are the midline (or median), which begins near the center of the perineum and extends straight downward, and the mediolateral, which starts similarly but angles 60 degrees to the side. Other less frequently used episiotomy incisions include the modified median, J-shaped, lateral, anterior, and radical.  After childbirth, performing a thorough perineal examination is necessary to assess trauma, and a rectal examination is vital to detect any anal sphincter injuries. In the United States, episiotomy was a widely used technique until 2006, when the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) made a recommendation against its routine use. However, episiotomy still has select indications and should be performed based on clinical judgment and maternal or fetal factors. Episiotomies, particularly the midline type, are linked with higher rates of obstetric anal sphincter injuries (OASIS). Please see StatPearls' companion resource, "Obstetric Perineal Lacerations," for more information.
