# Fibula Tissue Transfer(Archived)

**Authors:** Muhammad Taqi, Charles M. Llewellyn, Martin Estefan

**Source:** StatPearls - StatPearls Publishing

## Abstract

Autologous grafts have been fundamental to reconstructing bony defects for over a century; however, the first description of free fibular graft transfer was reported by Taylor et al. in 1975. More recently, the lateral approach to fibula harvest was popularized by Gilbert, followed by Chuang et al., who described free, vascularized fibula transfer with a skin paddle and functional soleus muscle for forearm reconstruction. Numerous options exist for reconstructing bony defects, including non-vascularized cancellous and cortical autografts, cadaveric bone allografts, endoprosthesis placement, bone transfer procedures, and vascularized bone grafts, such as those from the iliac crest, rib, scapula, and fibula. Free vascularized fibular grafting is widely utilized for reconstructing skeletal defects larger than 6 cm due to the fibula's length, straightness, strength, predictable vascular pedicle, and hypertrophic potential, which can enhance long-term bone remodeling. Nowadays, a fibular transfer is commonly utilized after tumor surgery, for pseudarthrosis rescue, mandibular defect reconstruction, and the management of chronic osteomyelitis. A vascularized epiphyseal transfer is used for reconstruction in skeletally immature patients with complex injuries to preserve the potential for ongoing longitudinal growth and provide a viable articular surface for effective joint function.
