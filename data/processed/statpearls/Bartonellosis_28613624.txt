# Bartonellosis

**Authors:** Beverly A. Mikes, Hassam Zulfiqar, Andrew Stevenson Joel Chandranesan

**Source:** StatPearls - StatPearls Publishing

## Abstract

Bartonella spp are intracellular, facultative, gram-negative bacteria that cause zoonotic infections in humans globally. Bartonella is an emerging and expanding genus, currently reported to have over 45 species, 12 of which are associated with human infections.. The species most commonly associated with bartonellosis in humans are B henselae, B quintana and B bacilliformis. B henselae causes cat-scratch disease, and other clinical syndromes, such as culture-negative endocarditis and bacillary peliosis hepatis. B quintana, a louse-borne infection that was historically the cause of trench fever, is a neglected cause of syndromes, such as acute febrile illness, chronic bacteremia, bacillary angiomatosis, and subacute endocarditis. Transmission of B quintana is by infected body lice, and risk factors include living in crowded, unhygienic conditions as well as experiencing homelessness.  Recent reports have documented transmission of B quintana through organ transplantation from deceased persons who experienced homelessness. B bacilliformis is endemic to the South American Andean valleys and causes Carrion disease. B bacilliformis is transmitted to humans via the bite of the sandfly Lutzomyia verrucarum.  Carrion disease presents in 2 clinical forms: The acute phase of Oroya fever is a severe, febrile, hemolytic illness with untreated mortality rates reaching up to 80% to 90%. The chronic phase, known as verruga peruana, typically appears weeks to months later as indolent and disfiguring angioproliferative skin lesions, which are rarely life-threatening. Unlike other Bartonella species, B bacilliformis is exclusively linked to these 2 manifestations. This review focuses on human infections caused by B henselae, B quintana, and B bacilliformis, the most common causes of emerging bartonellosis in humans, with significant epidemiological implications.
