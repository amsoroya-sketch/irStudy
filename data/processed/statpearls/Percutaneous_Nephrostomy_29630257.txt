# Percutaneous Nephrostomy

**Authors:** Michael Young, Stephen W. Leslie

**Source:** StatPearls - StatPearls Publishing

## Abstract

Percutaneous nephrostomy is a minimally invasive procedure used to drain the renal collecting system, typically in cases of urinary tract obstruction. This procedure involves puncturing the skin and the renal collecting system with a needle; generally, a catheter is left in place with its tip positioned in the renal collecting system at the conclusion of the procedure. Urologist Willard Goodwin described fluoroscopically guided percutaneous nephrostomy in 1955 as an alternative to surgical intervention for draining an obstructed renal collecting system. Percutaneous nephrostomy catheters, commonly referred to as "tubes," come in various types. All have multiple side holes at the distal end, allowing urine or other fluids (eg, pus) to drain from the renal pelvis and calyces.  The renal calyces, renal pelvis, ureter, bladder, and urethra form the urinary excretory tract. Accessing the renal collecting system is possible using an anatomically retrograde-oriented maneuver via the urethra, called transurethral ureteral access or retrograde ureteral access (RUA). RUA does not require creating a new hole in the body, let alone a hole through a highly vascular organ that can hemorrhage, leak urine, and fistulize. Thus, RUA is a preferable approach when feasible. Percutaneous nephrostomy is a type of antegrade renal access or antegrade ureteral access (AUA). Therefore, percutaneous nephrostomy becomes the procedure of choice when RUA is impossible, improbable, or has already failed. Factors that also favor RUA instead of AUA include: Minimal or no hydronephrosis. Stones that are small or close to the ureterovesical junction (UVJ). Uncorrected coagulopathy. A percutaneous access route that is obstructed or too long (eg, because of colon interposition or morbid obesity, respectively). Pregnancy, if radiation is needed for percutaneous nephrostomy. The RUA success rate diminishes when obstruction is caused by an object too large to pull through the UVJ or too near the ureteropelvic junction (UPJ). Compared to RUA, AUA can create a conduit for larger-caliber instruments to be inserted when needed, eg, for shattering and retrieving stone debris. AUA also sometimes offers a more favorable angle to insert instruments than RUA. When RUA is used to place a ureteral stent, the procedure may be called retrograde ureteral stent insertion (RUSI). Antegrade ureteral stent insertion (AUSI) after percutaneous nephrostomy can also be performed, as can placement of a nephroureteral stent (NUS), which is the placement of a single tube that combines the features of a percutaneous nephrostomy tube and a standard ureteral stent. Unlike arterial and venous stents, ureteral stents are made of plastic and are designed to be retrievable. Indications for ureteral stent placement can overlap (eg, stone treatment) or be separate from (eg, ureteral fistula) those for percutaneous nephrostomy tube insertion alone. Compared to having percutaneous nephrostomy tubes alone, some people do not tolerate having ureteral stents as well, which can interfere with ureteral peristalsis or cause painful bladder spasms. Both ureteral stents and percutaneous nephrostomy tubes require frequent replacement due to the buildup of mineral deposits, also known as encrustation. At institutions where radiologists perform percutaneous nephrostomy, the radiologist and urologist should collaborate to determine when RUA or AUA should be attempted first, or whether a rendezvous procedure (simultaneously attempting both approaches) should be used. In-depth discussion of AUSI, RUSI, NUS, percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL), and other procedures adjunct to percutaneous nephrostomy is beyond this activity's scope and can be found elsewhere.
