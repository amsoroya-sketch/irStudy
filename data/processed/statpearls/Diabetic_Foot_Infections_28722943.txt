# Diabetic Foot Infections

**Authors:** Heather M. Murphy-Lavoie, Adam Ramsey, Minhthao Nguyen, Elsa S. Vadakekut

**Source:** StatPearls - StatPearls Publishing

## Abstract

Approximately 29 million people have diabetes in the United States, and approximately 25% of people older than 65 have diabetes. The incidence of diabetes worldwide is projected to increase by 55% over the next 20 years, so this problem is only going to get worse. Approximately 75% of diabetic neuropathies are distal symmetric polyneuropathy (DSP). Once peripheral neuropathy develops, the annual incidence of ulcer formation increases from <1 % to >7%. The 3-year mortality for people with diabetes increases from 13% to 28% with an ulcer. The other major factor in diabetic foot infections is compromised blood flow. In the presence of local trauma and microvascular disease, diabetic foot infections may vary from a simple case of cellulitis to full-blown gangrene. Osteomyelitis occurs in 15% of ulcers, and 15% of those require amputation. Approximately 60% of patients undergoing lower extremity amputation have diabetic foot ulcers as the underlying cause. Following a lower extremity amputation, the 5-year mortality jumps to 60%. Diabetic foot ulcers are precursors to amputation and mortality, and therefore, every effort should be made to prevent them. Treating diabetic foot infections is difficult as the blood flow is compromised and the antibiotics usually cannot reach the diseased area. In many patients with diabetes, a foot infection will progress, and the time to resolution can be much more prolonged than for patients without diabetes.
