# Uterine Fibroid Embolization

**Authors:** Michael Young, Beverly A. Mikes

**Source:** StatPearls - StatPearls Publishing

## Abstract

Embolization is intentionally barricading an anatomic structure, such as an artery, vein, ureter, or other passage. Arterial embolization is performed to eliminate or reduce the blood supply to an area where it is no longer desirable, such as to induce tumor necrosis. Embolization of the uterus through the uterine artery is called uterine artery embolization (UAE). The main indication for UAE is to shrink or kill symptomatic uterine leiomyomata (eg, fibroids). Thus, the technique may also be called uterine fibroid embolization (UFE) when used for this purpose. However, a minority of fibroids are supplied by arteries other than the uterine artery, and UAE may be performed for indications other than fibroid embolization.  Results from a 2016 random survey of American women reached by cell phone, numbering 1443 women, found that 9% had been diagnosed with uterine fibroids. This rate was slightly higher than a 2009 survey that reached women online and concluded that 7% had fibroids. In the former survey, the primary treatment goal identified was relief of menorrhagia, alleviating pelvic tightness and pressure, and restoring energy levels. The women's mean age was 41, regardless of race, ethnicity, or income level; 11% were younger than age 35. Of the 1443 participants, 42% had already selected hysterectomy for treatment. They were much more likely to have had a healthcare professional recommend hysterectomy during the first discussion (60%) than women whose healthcare professional did not (16%). The leading reasons stated for choosing hysterectomy were recommendations from a healthcare professional (58%), pain (55%), and distress (47%). One in 3 women said that they would have been at least "somewhat interested" in a uterus-preserving option had they been told that was an option (Black women highest at 40%, White women lowest at 26%). Of participants who did not undergo hysterectomy, 71% tried pharmacological treatment. Thirty percent of participants (which the authors extrapolated to comprise just over 1 million US women yearly) underwent a uterus-preserving procedure, most often endometrial ablation or myomectomy.
