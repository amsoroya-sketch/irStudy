# Vertebral Osteomyelitis

**Authors:** Walter A. Hall, Adam Graeber, Nathan D. Cecava

**Source:** StatPearls - StatPearls Publishing

## Abstract

Vertebral osteomyelitis, also referred to as spinal osteomyelitis or spondylodiskitis, accounts for approximately 3% to 5% of all cases of osteomyelitis cases annually. This condition often presents with nonspecific clinical features, leading to diagnostic delays that can last several months. Although vertebral osteomyelitis is typically caused by a single pathogen, such as Staphylococcus aureus or Propionibacterium acnes, back pain is the most common symptom, while fever is often absent. Neurological impairment occurs in approximately one-third of patients with vertebral osteomyelitis. Therefore, recognizing high-risk patients is crucial for ensuring a timely diagnosis. Common risk factors include advanced age, immunosuppression, diabetes, intravenous (IV) drug use, and malnutrition. Enhanced clinical recognition is essential to reduce the morbidity and mortality associated with this condition. Vertebral osteomyelitis can develop following spinal trauma, surgery, or hematogenous spread from an adjacent site. If left untreated, it carries a high risk of morbidity, potentially resulting in spinal deformity, paraplegia, or death. Diagnosis involves assessing elevated inflammatory markers, imaging studies—particularly magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)—and blood cultures. In culture-negative cases, a computed tomography (CT)–guided biopsy may be required to identify the causative pathogen. Treatment involves at least 6 weeks of pathogen-targeted antibiotic therapy. Surgery is reserved for patients with neurologic deficits, spinal instability, or failure of medical treatment. Timely recognition and intervention are essential to reduce morbidity and improve patient outcomes.
