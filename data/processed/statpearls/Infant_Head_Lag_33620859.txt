# Infant Head Lag

**Authors:** Ihianle E. Osagie, Donald N. Givler

**Source:** StatPearls - StatPearls Publishing

## Abstract

Infants generally have a large head compared to the rest of their habitus, and the cervical group of paraspinal muscles is relatively weak at birth. Hence, the notable head lag is especially evident during the first few weeks of life. During this stage, caregivers routinely support the infant's head until neck control is achieved. Gross motor skill development in infants typically follows a cephalocaudal sequence, and the acquisition of postural control heralds it. Postural control is an infant's ability to stabilize the center of their body mass/trunk with a dynamic or static base. This innate maturational process of the neck and trunk muscles is expected to progress rapidly during infancy. When an infant is pulled by the hand from a lying to a sitting position, the expected response is to activate the head-righting reflex and maintain the head in line with his/her shoulders. Conversely, infant head lag is observed when the head seems to flop around or lag posteriorly behind the trunk during the pull-to-sit maneuver. Several articles have maintained that head lag should be absent by 3 months to 4 months of age, when infants typically develop increased control of their neck muscles. Once neck control is attained, infants actively participate in a normal pick-up routine by stiffening their neck muscles and tensing their trunk to reduce the lag between the chin and chest. Persistent head lag beyond age 4 months has been associated with poor neurodevelopmental outcomes. There is a higher incidence of head lag amongst preterm neonates and infants with cerebral palsy, and it is a likely predictor of developmental outcome. It may, however, not accurately predict neurobehavioral effects when used as an isolated metric. A possible explanation is that assessing a single isolated response is insufficient to accurately predict neurodevelopmental outcomes. Many studies have suggested that a combination of neonatal responses, such as reflexes, postural reactions, and tone, may accurately predict alterations in brain development. Studies have also shown that head lag, combined with other early childhood developmental alterations, may be intricately linked with a higher risk of autism in specific populations. Head lag was more commonly observed in siblings of children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) than in low-risk infants. Similarly, developmental problems relating to postural instability and axial hypotonicity have been described in infants with ASD.
