# Drug Trials

**Authors:** Sharoon David, Preeti Patel, Peggy Y. Kim

**Source:** StatPearls - StatPearls Publishing

## Abstract

Clinical trials are prospectively planned studies in humans that test medical interventions, such as drugs, vaccines, biologics, and medical devices, to evaluate safety, dose-response relationships, the efficacy of the intervention, and whether it produces clinical benefit. The drug trial is a type of clinical trial specifically designed to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of a new pharmaceutical or medicinal intervention in human subjects. The goal is to discover new or improved treatments for prevention and therapy, and to reassess existing medicines as new questions arise. Randomized controlled trials are studies where subjects are randomly assigned to receive a new drug or treatment (experimental group), standard treatment (comparison group), or no treatment at all (placebo group).  RCTs are considered the “gold standard” as they offer the best answer on the efficacy of a treatment or intervention. Robust trials also incorporate independent safety monitoring and adherence to good clinical practice guidelines. Blinding, randomization, adequate power, and a clinically relevant population of patients are considered the hallmarks of high-quality drug trials. This allows researchers to assess the benefit-risk balance logically. Adaptive trials are a prospectively planned approach. An integrated phase 2/3 study is one example that combines elements of phase 2 and phase 3, using interim analysis to make predefined, valid modifications. Instead of waiting for a phase 2 trial to conclude before making decisions on the next steps, a seamless phase 2/3 trial can facilitate mid-study choices, saving time, money, and resources. Trial methodology can vary depending on the design and the endpoints.  Biologic clinical trials have focused on the risk of immunogenicity, as therapeutic proteins can induce anti-drug antibodies that impact efficacy and safety. Both biologics and small-molecule drugs begin under an investigational new drug application. Most biologic trial application is submitted as a biologics license application (BLA) to the Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research (CBER). Traditional drug trials conclude with a new drug application to the Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER). Notably, that not all biologics fall under CBER; CDER reviews many monoclonal antibodies, cytokines, and growth factors, while vaccines, blood products, and certain cell and gene therapies remain with CBER. See the illustrative diagrams which provide a rapid overview of the clinical trial phases for both conventional drugs and biologics, Images. Drug Development Overview and Clinical Development and Regulatory Overview of Biologics.
