# Trinucleotide Repeat Disorders

**Authors:** Sharanya Ramakrishnan, Vikas Gupta

**Source:** StatPearls - StatPearls Publishing

## Abstract

Trinucleotide repeat disorders are a group of human diseases caused by the abnormal expansion of repetitive sequences. These conditions primarily affect the nervous system and occur during various stages of human development. Repetitive sequences, scattered in the microsatellite regions, usually account for about 30% of the human genome. In humans, the primary purpose of various lengths of repetitive DNA is to allow for evolutionary plasticity. However, when these repeats extend beyond the code for a viable physiological protein, the expression of this aberrant segment is suppressed. After a certain threshold number, this suppression is lost, and an aberrant protein is coded for, which gives rise to either a functional or a nonfunctional protein, thereby giving rise to a gain- or loss-of-function mutation. With every generation, the number of repeats increases drastically, and the age at which the patient presents is inversely related to the number of expansions. The severity, on the other hand, worsens with every generation due to a larger repeat sequence. This inheritance pattern of repeat-expansion diseases, called "anticipation," is evidence of the dynamic nature of these mutations. Myotonic dystrophy, Huntington disease, spinocerebellar ataxia (SCA), Friedreich ataxia, and Fragile X syndrome fall under the spectrum of trinucleotide repeat disorders. This activity provides insights into the various features of these 5 commonly studied trinucleotide repeat disorders.
