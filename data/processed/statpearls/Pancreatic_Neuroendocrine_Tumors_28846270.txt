# Pancreatic Neuroendocrine Tumors

**Authors:** Alexandra Helbing, Gopal Menon, Faiz Tuma

**Source:** StatPearls - StatPearls Publishing

## Abstract

Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (pNETs) arise from endocrine islet cells of the pancreas and occur sporadically or in the context of hereditary syndromes such as multiple endocrine neoplasia Type 1 (MEN1), von Hippel-Lindau disease, neurofibromatosis type 1, or tuberous sclerosis complex. They account for only 1% to 2% of pancreatic malignancies, although their incidence has increased over recent decades. These tumors are classified as functional when they secrete bioactive hormones (for example, insulin in insulinoma) or nonfunctional when hormone secretion is absent. Tumorigenesis involves activating mutations in genes, eg, MEN1, DAXX, ATRX, or components of the mTOR signaling pathway, that drive uncontrolled proliferation. Functional neoplasms produce characteristic clinical syndromes, whereas nonfunctional lesions often remain asymptomatic until they generate mass effect or metastasize, most commonly to the liver and regional lymph nodes. The tumors range from well-differentiated NETs to poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas. The diagnostic workup includes measuring serum hormone levels and chromogranin A, as well as cross-sectional imaging with contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and functional imaging using gallium-68 DOTATATE PET/CT. Management of localized disease is primarily surgical, ranging from enucleation for small lesions to formal pancreatectomy for larger or high-risk tumors. Treatment options in advanced or inoperable cases include somatostatin analogs, targeted agents such as everolimus or sunitinib, peptide receptor radionuclide therapy, and cytotoxic chemotherapy. Five-year survival for patients with low-grade localized neoplasms can approach 90%, whereas outcomes are markedly worse in high-grade or metastatic disease.
