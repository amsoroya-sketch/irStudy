# Vertebral Augmentation

**Authors:** Sunil Munakomi, Michael F. Stretanski, Joe M. Das

**Source:** StatPearls - StatPearls Publishing

## Abstract

Vertebral compression fractures (VCFs) represent the most common complication of osteoporosis, affecting approximately 1.4 million individuals globally each year. Among adults aged 70 and older, the global incidence of osteoporotic VCFs is nearly 20%, with postmenopausal women experiencing a 15% incidence rate. In the United States alone, an estimated 750,000 new cases are diagnosed annually, primarily in patients with underlying osteoporosis, contributing to an estimated $1.2 billion in healthcare expenditures. These fractures tend to undergo progressive compression, increasing the risk of retropulsion and subsequent neurological compromise. VCFs are also associated with a 5-fold increased risk of subsequent adjacent or distant fractures, as well as significantly elevated mortality rates within the first year. Conservative management often fails to prevent complications such as kyphotic deformity, prolonged immobility, and medical comorbidities. Propensity-matched analyses report 12-month mortality rates ranging from 2% to 42% among cases managed nonoperatively. Moreover, untreated kyphosis can worsen underlying osteoporosis, further compounding functional decline, healthcare costs, and social limitations. Vertebral augmentation (VA) has emerged as an effective treatment option for VCFs, capable of restoring vertebral body height, improving sagittal alignment, and providing rapid pain relief. By stabilizing the fracture and promoting early mobilization, VA reduces the risk of complications such as impaired pulmonary function, pressure ulcers, and venous thromboembolism, which are often associated with prolonged immobilization. This makes VA a critical therapeutic modality in the management of osteoporotic VCFs, addressing the biomechanical and systemic consequences of these fractures. While VCFs are the primary focus of vertebral augmentation, burst fractures also warrant consideration, as select cases may benefit from similar techniques. Burst fractures typically result from high-energy trauma such as motor vehicle collisions or falls, leading to multi-directional vertebral body fragmentation with possible retropulsion of bony fragments into the spinal canal. These injuries most frequently occur at the thoracolumbar junction (T11–L2), a biomechanical transition zone where rigid thoracic and mobile lumbar segments converge, creating a focal point for stress concentration and fracture risk.
