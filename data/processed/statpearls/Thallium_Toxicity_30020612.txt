# Thallium Toxicity

**Authors:** Tyler R. Kemnic, Meghan Coleman

**Source:** StatPearls - StatPearls Publishing

## Abstract

Thallium (Tl; atomic number 81) is a heavy metal associated with a broad spectrum of toxic effects in humans. Thallium sulfate was historically employed as an ant and rat poison. The occupational exposure limit for thallium on the skin is 0.1 mg/m³ over 8 hours, with concentrations of 15 mg/m³ or higher considered immediately hazardous to health. (Source: Occupational Safety and Health Administration, 2021) Thallium is readily absorbed via dermal and inhalational routes. This element's tasteless, odorless, and water-soluble properties have contributed to reports of both accidental and intentional poisoning. Historically, thallium salts were used therapeutically to treat dermatophytosis. The radioactive isotope thallium-201, typically administered as thallous chloride, was widely used for cardiac stress tests before being replaced by technetium-99m as the preferred tracer in nuclear medicine. Thallium-201 remains in use for the evaluation of coronary artery disease. The radioactive form exhibits toxicity more than 4,000 times lower than the elemental metal. Thallium scintigraphy assesses tissue perfusion by measuring thallium uptake. Normal coronary arteries dilate in response to exercise or vasodilatory drugs, potentially reducing myocardial perfusion in adjacent regions, a phenomenon termed "coronary steal syndrome." Thallium is a gray posttransition metal that does not occur in its free form in nature. Although chemically similar to tin, isolated thallium undergoes discoloration upon exposure to air. Exposure occurs primarily through dermal absorption, inhalation, or ingestion.
