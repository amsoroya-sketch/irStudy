Bloating in a 25-Year-Old Woman: History Structure, IBS and Coeliac Disease Versions

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / CLASS 4 MID GI CASE (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Case Stem

A 25-year-old lady presents to your general practice complaining of bloating — bloating alone. Task: history taking for six minutes (a newer case with a predominant assessment area of history taking).

Differential Diagnosis of Bloating

Working list for a young person with bloating: IBS, IBD, pancreatitis, obstruction, coeliac disease, ovarian cancer, lactose intolerance, pancreatic pseudocyst/pancreatic problems, gastroenteritis and infections such as giardiasis.

Top two red-flag groups (irrespective of age):

  • Malignancies — beyond bowel cancer and other GI cancers (pancreatic etc.), always leave a place for ovarian cancer: it rarely gives specific symptoms and classically presents with non-specific GI complaints (bloating, feeling full, constipation, diarrhoea), so it must stay on the list.
  • Obstruction — whether the bloating is acute or chronic: acute volvulus, small bowel obstruction (especially with previous surgeries — adhesions), and hernias (bowel enters the hernia, causing bloating and gas problems).

Remaining groups:

  • Diet-related: lactose intolerance, coeliac disease (both cause bloating), and gastroenteritis (acute and chronic forms) with increased gas production.
  • Motility problems of the GI tract (analogous to oesophageal motility disorders): diabetes (autonomic neuropathy) and medications affecting gut motility.
  • IBS at the very bottom — it is a diagnosis of exclusion; you never make it before ruling out the other conditions.
  • Optionally: constipation / faecal impaction.

History Map: Four Major Steps

  1. Introduction — initiate the station.
  2. Explore the complaint (bloating).
  3. Rule out differentials — red flags sit at the top of the list no matter how unlikely, in the exam and in real practice.
  4. If time allows in the six minutes, wrap up with a good closure.

Introduction and Opening

You enter, are ID-checked, and are asked to sanitise your hands (sanitiser on the desk). Introduce yourself and open with: "How can I help you today?" Respond to the patient's opening statement empathically — acknowledge any concern, reassure, then move into the history: "Thank you for coming in, Jenny. I understand this is concerning for you — let's go through it together. I'll ask you a few questions to figure out what's going on, and we'll make a proper plan together."

Exploring the Bloating

First, define what the patient means by 'bloating'. Patients use the word for different things:

  • excessive burping;
  • passing too much wind (possibly foul-smelling);
  • feeling distended in the abdomen;
  • reflux symptoms.

Ask: "Jenny, can you describe what you mean by bloating? Are you burping too much, passing more wind lately, or feeling distended in the stomach?"

Timing: How long has this been going on and when did it start? Is it on and off or continuous? Is it getting worse? Add diurnal variation: symptoms at night suggest something more serious than symptoms only in waking hours related to eating — "Is it worse at any specific time of day? Do you feel the bloating at night too, or only during the daytime?"

Alleviating and aggravating factors: the two generic questions, plus the key specific one — "Is it worse after eating? Does it happen after any specific food?"

General GI Questions

  • Nausea and vomiting.
  • Abdominal pain (replacing the bloating/wind questions, since bloating is the presenting complaint). Pain is relevant across the list: cancers and obstruction cause pain; coeliac disease and gastroenteritis cause cramps; IBD and IBS cause pain.
  • Constipation and diarrhoea.
  • Fever.

Ruling Out the Differentials

1. Malignancies — general cancer questions: weight loss; appetite changes; lumps and bumps; tiredness. Specific questions: blood in the stools (with dark stools if desired); change in bowel habit; family history of bowel cancer (changes your whole approach). For ovarian cancer, the relevant extra is period problems — usually left to the end of the history.

2. Obstruction — ask about its causes: cancer (already covered); adhesion bands — "Any previous surgeries?"; hernia — "Any lump or swelling in your groin?"

3. Diet-related:

  • Lactose intolerance: "Are your bloating symptoms triggered or made worse by eating dairy products?"
  • Coeliac disease: "Have you noticed greasy stools that are hard to flush, stick to the pan and float on water?" Plus: "Any family history of coeliac disease?"

4. Gastroenteritis/infections: diarrhoea and abdominal pain already asked; add recent travel (the travel box for infections, e.g. giardiasis).

5. Motility problems: "Are you taking any medications currently?" Past medical history made specific: "Any history of high blood sugar levels (diabetes)?" — neuropathy affecting bowel movement is the best example.

6. IBD (towards the bottom): extra-intestinal symptoms — joint pain, blurring of vision (eye symptoms), mouth ulcers — just to tick it off.

IBS and the Rome IV Criteria

With six minutes of history, do not start with IBS questions — it is a diagnosis of exclusion. Per Murtagh: if there is blood in the stools, older age, or a family history of cancer, do not diagnose IBS until those have been properly excluded.

Stress is commonly present in the background of IBS, but stress is not a diagnostic criterion. The diagnosis is made using the Rome IV criteria: recurrent abdominal pain (on average at least 1 day per week over the last 3 months, with onset at least 6 months ago) associated with at least two of the following three:

  1. pain related to defecation (typically the pain/bloating/discomfort improves when the patient opens the bowels);
  2. change in stool frequency;
  3. change in stool form (classically alternating constipation and diarrhoea).

Key IBS questions:

  • "Do your stools alternate between being constipated and being loose/diarrhoea?" or "Are your stools sometimes hard and sometimes loose?"
  • "Does opening your bowels help with the bloating (or the pain)?"
  • Stress screen — a mini psychosocial (HEADSS-style) assessment: "Who do you live with? How are things at home — any stresses? Do you study or work — any stresses there?"

Period History

Most patients presenting with this bloating case are women — ask a period history at the end: it covers the possibility of pregnancy as a cause of the symptoms, and ovarian cancer can cause period problems.

Exam Version 1: IBS

Recalled findings: the patient feels bloated, and it is better when she goes to the toilet. Assuming a completed history shows no red flags (no weight loss, no blood in the stools), symptoms better with defecation, alternating stool form, and some stress — the diagnosis is irritable bowel syndrome.

(In one pilot exam the tasks were: history for five minutes, then PEFE, then diagnosis and differentials — the five-minute history was unusual against the usual four-minute structure.)

Exam Version 2: Coeliac Disease

Later-recalled findings: the patient cannot eat pizza and bread ("I've realised when I have pizza and bread it gets worse") — this should immediately spark the diagnosis. There are smelly stools that are hard to flush (steatorrhoea characteristics), and a past history of iron deficiency anaemia — attributed to heavy periods to confuse you — for which she takes iron supplements.

Diagnosis: coeliac disease. The anaemia is the malabsorption clue — coeliac cases like to drop a past history of anaemia. And note the elegant extra differential: iron supplements themselves cause GI symptoms — constipation, bloating and cramps — so the iron tablets belong on the differential list too.

Explaining Coeliac Disease to the Patient

"Most likely you have a condition called coeliac disease. Your bowel reacts abnormally to gluten, a protein found in wheat, oats, barley and rye. Whenever you eat food containing gluten, your bowel becomes inflamed — causing bloating, cramps, and the stool changes you describe." (See corrections: coeliac disease is an immune-mediated condition, not an allergy.)

Reasoning:

  1. The relationship with gluten-containing foods (pizza and bread contain gluten).
  2. Stools hard to flush = extra fat = malabsorption — fats are not absorbed, making stools fatty and greasy so they stick to the pan and float.
  3. Low iron — iron deficiency occurs in coeliac disease through malabsorption.
  4. Additional differential: side effects of the iron tablets (constipation, bloating, cramps).
  5. Other conditions considered: bowel cancers, obstruction, lactose intolerance, inflammatory bowel disease, IBS.

Explaining IBS to the Patient

"Most likely you have a condition called irritable bowel syndrome. It is a stress-related problem that affects the movement (motility) of your bowels, causing bloating and cramps that typically get better when you open your bowels." Stress affects the gut via the nervous system — the same way stress contributes to gastritis and reflux symptoms.

Reasoning (point towards Rome IV): symptoms improve with opening the bowels; change in stool form (alternating); background stress if found. Second limb of the reasoning: "I made sure to ask about and rule out more important conditions — bowel cancer, coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease — and they do not appear probable in your case, which is why I've settled on irritable bowel syndrome" (IBS = diagnosis of exclusion).

Then give the differential list with reasoning: "A rare but significant condition I considered was bowel cancer — but you have no blood in the stools and no weight loss. I considered lactose intolerance (sensitivity to the sugar in dairy products), inflammatory bowel disease (inflammation in the bowels), obstruction (blockage from previous surgery or hernias), and motility problems from medications or conditions like diabetes." The longer and better-grouped the list, the better the marks.

Physical Examination (PEFE) Structure

Headlines: general appearance; vital signs; core examination: abdominal examination; office tests.

  • General appearance — cancer-related findings: pallor, cachexia, lymphadenopathy.
  • Rashes: IBD — erythema nodosum; coeliac disease — dermatitis herpetiformis: "herpetiform" means it looks like herpes (a vesicular rash), but unlike herpes (which is painful) it is intensely itchy, typically on the elbows and legs. An itchy vesicular rash in a patient (including a child) with chronic anaemia or chronic diarrhoea should make you think of coeliac disease.
  • Vital signs: as usual, with emphasis on temperature.
  • Abdominal examination (complete): inspection; palpation for tenderness and masses; percussion; auscultation; inguinal examination; rectal examination.
  • Office tests: blood sugar level.
  • Pelvic/bimanual examination: debatable — reasonable only if genuinely concerned about ovarian cancer; it can feel incongruous in an uncomplicated bloating presentation but is relevant at the end of the day.

Expected findings: in the IBS version everything is normal (that is why you can make the diagnosis). In the coeliac version, the abdomen is unremarkable and the only pointer may be the dermatitis herpetiformis rash.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The coeliac explanation says 'your bowel is sensitive or allergic to gluten', and the lactose intolerance differential is explained as being 'sensitive to a protein or sugar in dairy products'.
Coeliac disease is an immune-mediated (autoimmune) enteropathy triggered by gluten in genetically susceptible people (HLA-DQ2/DQ8) — it is not an allergy, and this matters clinically because it is lifelong, requires strict gluten exclusion, and carries complications (osteoporosis, other autoimmune disease, small increased lymphoma risk) that 'allergy' framing understates. Lactose intolerance is a lactase enzyme deficiency causing sugar malabsorption — also not an allergy. Additionally, note that oats are not intrinsically gluten-containing; the concern is contamination and avenin sensitivity in a minority — Coeliac Australia advises specialist guidance on oats.
✏️ The Rome IV criteria are paraphrased as 'chronic abdominal pain for six months plus two of three: related to defecation, better when opening the bowels, change in stool form' — merging Rome III and Rome IV wording and omitting the frequency criterion.
Rome IV (2016) requires recurrent abdominal pain on average at least 1 day per week in the last 3 months, with onset at least 6 months before diagnosis, associated with at least TWO of: (1) pain related to defecation, (2) change in stool FREQUENCY, (3) change in stool FORM. 'Improvement with defecation' was the older Rome III wording — Rome IV broadened it to 'related to defecation' (pain may worsen or improve), and change in stool frequency is a distinct criterion from change in form. Corrected in the structured sections.
✏️ Critical omission in the coeliac pathway: no mention that the patient must remain on a gluten-containing diet during diagnostic work-up.
In Australian practice (eTG, Coeliac Australia, GESA), coeliac serology (anti-tTG IgA with total IgA level) and confirmatory duodenal biopsy are only reliable while the patient is consuming gluten. Patients must NOT commence a gluten-free diet before testing; if already gluten-free, a gluten challenge (or HLA-DQ2/DQ8 testing to help exclude the diagnosis) is required. This should be stated whenever coeliac disease is explained to a patient.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add first-line investigations for the bloating work-up to bridge history and PEFE: FBC and iron studies (iron deficiency in coeliac disease or occult GI blood loss), coeliac serology (anti-tTG IgA plus total IgA, on a gluten-containing diet), CRP (IBD screen), stool microscopy/PCR for Giardia if traveller, faecal calprotectin to discriminate IBD from IBS in patients under 50, and a pregnancy test (beta-hCG) in any woman of reproductive age — reinforcing the case's own period-history teaching.
⭐ Add IBS management content for the two-minute explanation: reassurance and explanation of the gut-brain axis, dietary strategies including a dietitian-supervised trial of a low-FODMAP diet (developed at Monash University — a strong Australian reference), regular exercise, psychological therapies for stress, and symptom-targeted agents; safety-net with red-flag advice (bleeding, weight loss, nocturnal symptoms warrant review).
⭐ Add explicit IBS alarm features as a boxed list (consistent with the Murtagh caution the case cites): age over 50 at symptom onset, rectal bleeding, unintentional weight loss, nocturnal diarrhoea waking the patient, fever, anaemia, and family history of bowel cancer, coeliac disease or IBD — any of these mandates investigation before an IBS label.
⭐ Add a note on giardiasis as the exemplar chronic infective cause of bloating flagged in the differential list: prolonged bloating, flatulence and loose greasy stools after freshwater/overseas travel; diagnose on stool PCR/microscopy; treat per eTG (e.g. metronidazole or tinidazole) — this makes the 'travel box' question concrete.
