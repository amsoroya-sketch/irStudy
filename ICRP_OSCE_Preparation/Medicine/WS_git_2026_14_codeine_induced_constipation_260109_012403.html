Codeine-Induced Constipation — Young Woman with Lower Abdominal Pain (2025 Case)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / 2.Abdominal pain case (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Station Stem and Tasks

2025 case. Your next patient is a 20-year-old lady who has presented complaining of abdominal pain. She has a history of recent back pain (the lead point).

  • Task 1: Take history for 4 minutes.
  • Task 2: Ask physical examination findings from the examiner.
  • Task 3: Explain diagnosis and differentials to the patient.

History Taking

  • Haemodynamic stability
  • Introduction and open-ended question

Explore the pain (SIQORAA):

  • Site: "Can you reconfirm where the pain is?" — "Lower side of the stomach." "Which side?" — "The entire lower stomach."
  • Intensity: "On a scale of 1–10, how would you describe your pain over the last few weeks?" — "4/10."
  • Quality: "Is it a sharp ache or a vague dull ache?"
  • Onset/time: "When did it start?" — "A week ago" (so not hyperacute). "On and off or constant? Getting worse?"
  • Radiation: "Does the pain radiate anywhere?"
  • Alleviating/aggravating factors: "Anything that makes it better or worse?" — "The pain gets worse with eating." "Any specific food?" — "No." (Odd combination: lower abdominal pain worse on eating.) "Any position that changes it?"

Second Exploration: The Lead Point — Recent Back Pain

The back pain has resolved, but it must be explored:

  • "Did you see a doctor? What did they diagnose?" — "Yes, he told me it's just regular back pain."
  • "What treatment did you take?" — the significant answer: "The doctor gave me Panadol and codeine" (in Australia the combination brand is Panadeine Forte — paracetamol + codeine).
  • "Overall, are you feeling better?" — "Yes."

Differential Groups

For lower abdominal pain, screen the usual groups after the general GI questions:

  • GI group
  • KUB group
  • Pelvic group — hernia questions, plus pelvic examination in a woman (or scrotal examination in a man)

In this case the patient volunteers that she is on Panadol and codeine; the rest of the history is usually normal — no red flags, no pelvic problems.

Physical Examination From the Examiner (PEFE)

  • General appearance and vital signs (BP, pulse, respiratory rate, temperature).
  • Abdominal inspection: symmetric movement with respiration; Cullen/Grey Turner signs; distension.
  • Superficial and deep palpation: "Any tenderness or masses?" — faecal loading/stool palpable in the left lower quadrant (sometimes reported as "a mass" — this should still point to the diagnosis). Ask the key-point questions: rigidity, guarding, rebound tenderness, other masses (see expert review regarding the recorded answers).
  • Percussion: any tenderness. Auscultation: bowel sounds present?
  • Inguinal area examination.
  • DRE: examiner — "You can feel faeces and hard stool on your finger."
  • Pelvic examination (with patient consent and a chaperone): inspection for rashes, lumps, discharge or bleeding; speculum — cervix and vaginal wall structurally normal, no bleeding or discharge; bimanual — cervical motion tenderness, uterine size/border/tenderness, adnexal tenderness or mass.
  • Office tests: urine dipstick — normal; urine pregnancy test — reported positive in the source notes (see expert review).

Diagnosis and Differentials

Two diagnostic labels are considered: codeine-induced constipation versus faecal impaction/obstruction. Because this patient is passing wind and not vomiting, it is not an obstruction. "Codeine-induced constipation" is the easier terminology to explain.

Diagnosis to the patient: "Most likely the cause of your pain is constipation, caused by the painkiller codeine you are taking. Codeine has a side effect of causing constipation, and I believe the constipation is causing your pain."

Reasons: it is a known side effect of the medication, and on examination a mass was felt in the lower tummy which is stool.

Differentials:

  • Faecal impaction → bowel obstruction: "the same constipation getting so hard it starts blocking the bowel — but with your symptoms it isn't obstructed yet."
  • Other groups: GI differentials, KUB differentials, pelvic differentials.

Variant: Same Case, Different Findings → Bowel Obstruction

If the findings differ, the diagnosis changes. With:

  • Codeine use and constipation
  • Not passing wind
  • Vomiting non-stop (pain for a week, vomiting started yesterday)
  • Normal periods, no vaginal discharge or bleeding

→ the diagnosis becomes bowel obstruction.

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: history-taking-expert (Australian clinical supervisor) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The PEFE section records rigidity (+), guarding (+) and rebound tenderness (+) alongside a diagnosis of simple codeine-induced constipation. Positive peritonism signs are incompatible with benign constipation and would mandate urgent surgical escalation, not reassurance.
These are almost certainly OCR/transcription artefacts — in the intended station, rigidity, guarding and rebound tenderness are NEGATIVE, with the positive findings being palpable faecal loading in the left lower quadrant and hard faeces on DRE. Teaching point for candidates: if peritonism signs were genuinely positive, the correct action is immediate senior/surgical review, not a diagnosis of constipation.
✏️ The office tests record 'urine pregnancy test => Positive' with no further comment, while the case proceeds to a diagnosis of codeine-induced constipation.
A positive pregnancy test in a 20-year-old with abdominal pain changes the entire station (ectopic pregnancy must be excluded; codeine and many laxatives require pregnancy-safety review). The intended finding in the constipation station is a NEGATIVE pregnancy test; the positive result appears to be a transcription error. If a pregnancy test is ever positive in such a case, address it explicitly and re-frame the differential.
✏️ The notes name the combination product as 'Panadol & Codeine {The brand name is Australia is - Panadeine Forte}' without regulatory context.
Panadeine Forte is paracetamol 500 mg + codeine 30 mg. Since 1 February 2018 ALL codeine-containing analgesics are prescription-only (Schedule 4) in Australia (TGA up-scheduling). Current eTG/RACGP guidance discourages codeine for uncomplicated low back pain; if used, co-prescribe advice on constipation prevention and limit duration.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add a bowel-habit question set that the current history skips despite constipation being the diagnosis: when was the last bowel motion, stool frequency and consistency (Bristol chart), straining, sensation of incomplete emptying, passing wind, and any overflow diarrhoea — these are the discriminators between simple constipation, impaction and obstruction.
⭐ Add management for the closure (frequently asked as an extension): cease/wean the codeine and substitute non-opioid analgesia (paracetamol +/- NSAID) for the resolved back pain; first-line laxatives per eTG — an osmotic laxative (e.g. macrogol) +/- a stimulant (e.g. senna); increase fluid, fibre and activity; review if not opening bowels within 48–72 hours or if vomiting/distension develops (safety-netting for obstruction).
⭐ Add opioid-induced constipation as a named entity in the metadata/teaching text: constipation is the one opioid adverse effect that does NOT develop tolerance, which is why prophylactic laxatives should accompany any ongoing opioid prescription — a high-yield AMC counselling point.
⭐ Add the obstruction red-flag triad to the variant section for quick recall: absolute constipation (no faeces or flatus) + vomiting + distension/colicky pain → ED referral, nil by mouth, IV fluids, abdominal imaging.
