Sudden Collapse and Unwell (Anaphylaxis)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med workshop 2 - 2026 / CLASS 5 UNWELL (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: general_practice

Case Overview

A super-acute 'unwell' case: a patient becomes suddenly unwell and is too unstable to talk, so the history is taken from a relative (raising confidentiality and consent issues). Vital signs in the stem already indicate an unstable patient. The diagnosis to explain to the relative is anaphylaxis. The station tests the ability to formulate a differential for sudden collapse and to recognise and manage anaphylaxis.

Differential for Sudden Collapse

  • Cardiovascular: myocardial infarction, arrhythmia
  • Neurological: stroke, intracranial haemorrhage, sudden head injury
  • Respiratory: pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax
  • Infectious: meningococcaemia, sepsis, meningitis
  • Allergic: anaphylaxis
  • Vascular: ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm
  • Also: hypoglycaemia, drug/alcohol intoxication or withdrawal

Talking to the Relative - Consent

Introduce yourself and confirm the patient has consented to the relative discussing her condition. In an emergency, confidentiality rules are applied differently to allow urgent care. Then use an open-ended question: 'Tell me what has happened.'

Exploring the Super-Acute 'Unwell'

  • Clarify 'unwell': did she lose consciousness, faint, become drowsy/unresponsive, or was she in severe pain (MI/AAA)?
  • Timing: exact onset (super-acute), constant vs intermittent, worsening
  • Trigger first (before aggravating/relieving factors): what was she doing just before - new food, medication, insect bite?
  • Ask three critical questions early: past medical history, current medications (she is on insulin), and allergies

System Differential Screen

Cardiovascular: preceding chest pain, palpitations, dizziness.

Neurological: loss of consciousness, limb weakness, numbness, slurred speech, gait/balance problems.

Respiratory: cough, shortness of breath, noisy breathing, recent long travel (PE), choking episode (obstructed airway) - relative reports she choked on food then collapsed.

Infection: fever/chills, sick contacts, travel.

Anaphylaxis Features

Anaphylaxis is a multi-system allergic reaction affecting skin, respiratory, cardiovascular and GI systems.

  • Skin: urticaria (angry, raised, itchy rash appearing rapidly), lip/tongue swelling (angioedema)
  • Respiratory: noisy breathing/stridor, wheeze, shortness of breath
  • GI (often forgotten): nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain
  • Trigger: new food (nuts, seafood, eggs), insect sting/bite, drugs (penicillin)
  • Background: personal/family history of anaphylaxis and atopy (asthma, eczema, hay fever)

Consider hypoglycaemia as a differential (missed meals; she is on insulin).

Examination

From the examiner - general appearance (level of consciousness, rash, audible noisy breathing/stridor and wheeze); vital signs including temperature and oxygen saturation. Quick neurological, cardiovascular, respiratory and abdominal examination (bruising - Cullen/Grey Turner sign, pulsatile mass for AAA). Anaphylaxis-specific: lip/tongue swelling, stridor, rash, visible insect-bite marks. Office tests: blood sugar level and urine dipstick. Findings: insect bite on the neck and wheeze - confirming anaphylaxis.

Diagnosis and Management

Explain to the relative: a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis), most likely triggered by an insect bite. Reasons: rapid onset, wheeze/noisy breathing, and the insect-bite mark on the neck.

Management (anaphylaxis is a clinical diagnosis - treat immediately):

  • Remove the trigger (e.g. sting) if present
  • Lay the patient flat (do not let them stand or walk); sitting is acceptable if breathing difficulty
  • Intramuscular adrenaline 0.01 mL/kg of 1:1000 (max 0.5 mL), anterolateral thigh - the priority intervention
  • EpiPen technique: 'blue to the sky, orange to the thigh'; hold against the outer thigh (through clothing) and press. Adult EpiPen (yellow) 300 microg; EpiPen Jr (green) 150 microg
  • Call triple zero (000)
  • Nebulised adrenaline is an option for persistent stridor/wheeze, but IM adrenaline comes first

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The definition states that 'acute onset of hypertension or bronchospasm' (and a 'suddenly unwell, hypertensive patient') should be diagnosed as anaphylaxis.
This is an error (likely OCR/transcription). Anaphylaxis is characterised by acute onset of <strong>hypotension</strong> (or bronchospasm/upper-airway obstruction), not hypertension. The ASCIA definition includes acute onset hypotension or bronchospasm or upper airway obstruction even without typical skin features.
✏️ Positioning advice ('semi-sitting is also fine') is given without qualification.
Per ASCIA, the patient should be laid flat (and legs elevated if hypotensive); sitting is only appropriate if breathing is difficult, and the patient must NOT stand or walk - sudden posture change to upright has been associated with fatal outcomes ('empty ventricle' syndrome).

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add repeat dosing and adjuncts: repeat IM adrenaline every 5 minutes if there is no improvement; give high-flow oxygen, obtain large-bore IV access and give an IV fluid bolus for hypotension; state clearly that adrenaline is first-line and that antihistamines and corticosteroids are NOT first-line and do not treat airway or cardiovascular compromise.
⭐ Add post-event care: observe for a minimum of 4 hours for a biphasic reaction, and on discharge provide an adrenaline auto-injector, a personalised ASCIA Action Plan for Anaphylaxis, a medical-alert recommendation, and referral to a clinical immunology/allergy service.
⭐ Clarify the confidentiality point with a concrete standard: in a life-threatening emergency where the patient cannot consent, necessary information may be gathered and shared to provide urgent care under the emergency provisions of the Australian Privacy Principles/duty of care.
⭐ Given the patient is on insulin, explicitly document an early bedside blood glucose to exclude hypoglycaemia as a rapidly reversible mimic of collapse.
