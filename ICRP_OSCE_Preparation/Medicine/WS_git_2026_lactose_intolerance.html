Lactose Intolerance: Chronic Abdominal Pain in a 20-Year-Old Man

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / CLASS 4 MID GI CASE (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Case Stem

A 20-year-old man presents to your general practice complaining of chronic abdominal pain. Your task: history taking for six minutes.

This case now sits within the bloating/young-patient chronic abdominal pain cluster (previously taught alongside the other two young-patient cases — IBD and coeliac disease).

History Structure (Headlines)

  • Introduction.
  • Explore the pain.
  • General GI questions, adapted to what you find.
  • Rule out the differentials.
  • If time allows, a good closure.

Case Findings

  • Six months of abdominal pain, periumbilical ("around my belly button"), on and off.
  • Alternating stool forms — sometimes constipation, sometimes diarrhoea.
  • No stress identified.
  • Every time he takes dairy products he gets cramping and bloating.
  • Family history of migraine.

How these findings emerge through the structure:

  • Exploring the pain gives you the site (periumbilical), duration (six months) and quality (expect dull pain).
  • Alleviating/aggravating factors: generic questions are negative, but the targeted question — "Is it related to food or eating? Any specific food?" — reveals: "I notice it when I have dairy products."
  • General GI questions: bloating — "yes, especially after I eat dairy products"; constipation or diarrhoea — "both — sometimes constipation, sometimes diarrhoea."

Diagnostic Reasoning: Lactose Intolerance vs IBS vs Abdominal Migraine

The picture is chronic, vague, on-and-off crampy abdominal pain with alternating bowel habit — superficially IBS-like. But there is a specific, reproducible dairy trigger (cramps and bloating after dairy). Because IBS is a diagnosis of exclusion, a positive identifiable trigger takes priority: the top differential is lactose intolerance, with IBS retained lower on the list unless other IBS features dominate.

Abdominal migraine: some call this case "the abdominal migraine" because of the family history of migraine. Formal criteria for abdominal migraine exist, but the final criterion requires that the symptoms are not better explained by another disorder — you must rule out other conditions first. It might only be considered where the patient does not fulfil IBS criteria and has a supportive personal/family history of migraine or headaches. In this station it is best not to raise abdominal migraine at all — the dairy trigger explains the presentation.

Explaining Lactose Intolerance to the Patient

Explain simply: "You have a condition called lactose intolerance. Your bowel cannot properly handle a sugar found in dairy products called lactose. Whenever you eat dairy products — milk, yoghurt, cheese — you get symptoms: bloating, abdominal pain, diarrhoea, and sometimes nausea."

Note: the workshop transcript used the phrase "sensitive or allergic to a sugar"; see corrections — lactose intolerance is an enzyme (lactase) deficiency causing malabsorption, not an allergy, and this distinction should be preserved even in lay explanations.

Reasoning to give the patient: the pain is triggered by eating dairy products; you also have bloating, cramps and diarrhoea with the dairy exposure — the full symptom pattern fits.

At the bottom of the differential list, add IBS.

The Young-Patient Chronic Abdominal Pain Cluster

AMC has used three young-patient chronic abdominal pain cases: IBD, coeliac disease, and lactose intolerance. Put IBS beside them — there is a very old, famous AMC IBS case.

IBS diagnostic criteria — Rome IV: IBS is diagnosed using the Rome IV criteria, as a diagnosis of exclusion. Importantly, stress is NOT an essential criterion. Most IBS patients do have a background stressor, and it is worth screening for, but the diagnosis rests on the Rome IV criteria, not on the presence of stress — stress is too hard to define to be a diagnostic criterion. Criteria exist precisely because IBS is a condition that needs formal criteria to be diagnosed reliably.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The patient explanation states 'your bowel is sensitive or allergic to a sugar in dairy products called lactose'.
Lactose intolerance is NOT an allergy. It is a deficiency of the enzyme lactase in the small bowel brush border, causing lactose malabsorption; unabsorbed lactose is fermented by colonic bacteria producing gas, bloating, cramps and osmotic diarrhoea. This must be distinguished from cow's milk protein allergy, which is immune-mediated. Even in lay explanations, say 'your bowel lacks the enzyme that breaks down the milk sugar lactose' — telling a patient they are 'allergic' can lead to unnecessary strict avoidance and confusion (most lactose-intolerant patients tolerate small amounts of lactose, hard cheeses and yoghurt).
✏️ The transcript refers to the IBS criteria as 'Rome4', 'wrongful criteria' and 'rongful criteria' (transcription artifacts), and does not state the actual criteria content.
The correct name is the Rome IV criteria (2016): recurrent abdominal pain on average at least 1 day per week in the last 3 months, with symptom onset at least 6 months before diagnosis, associated with at least two of: (1) pain related to defecation, (2) a change in stool frequency, (3) a change in stool form. Corrected in the structured sections.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add confirmation and management steps expected in Australian practice (eTG gastrointestinal): a 2-4 week trial of dietary lactose exclusion with symptom re-challenge is the usual first-line diagnostic approach in general practice; a lactose hydrogen breath test can confirm the diagnosis if uncertainty remains. Management is dietary modification (reduce, not necessarily eliminate, lactose), lactose-free dairy alternatives, lactase enzyme supplements, and ensuring adequate calcium and vitamin D intake — an important counselling point in a 20-year-old.
⭐ Add secondary lactase deficiency as a teaching point: transient lactose intolerance commonly follows infectious gastroenteritis (especially in children) and can accompany coeliac disease or Crohn disease due to villous damage — so a positive dairy trigger does not fully exclude the other cluster diagnoses, and coeliac serology (anti-tTG IgA with total IgA, while on a gluten-containing diet) is reasonable in a young patient with chronic abdominal pain and bloating before closing the case.
⭐ Add an explicit red-flag screen to the history structure for chronic abdominal pain in a young adult (weight loss, nocturnal symptoms waking from sleep, blood in stools, anaemia symptoms, family history of bowel cancer or IBD) — these are the features that must be negative before accepting a functional/dietary diagnosis, and examiners expect them to be asked even when the trigger seems obvious.
⭐ Add epidemiology metadata useful for the exam discussion: primary (adult-type) lactase non-persistence is genetically determined, typically presents in adolescence/early adulthood, and is much more prevalent in people of Asian, African, Middle Eastern and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander backgrounds than in Northern European populations — relevant to the multicultural Australian patient mix.
