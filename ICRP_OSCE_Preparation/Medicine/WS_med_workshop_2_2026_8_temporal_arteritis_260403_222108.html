Temporal Arteritis (Giant Cell Arteritis) - History and Management

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med workshop 2 - 2026 / CLASS 1 HEADACHE (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: neurology

Overview

An older, less-common headache case regarded as the most challenging because around 90% of candidates find no clear positive findings by the end of the history. Key differentials in a patient over 50 with headache: brain tumour and temporal arteritis.

Opening and Pain Characteristics (SIQORAA)

Confirm haemodynamic stability, introduce yourself and use an open-ended question. The patient reports a 6-month, worsening headache with worry — a chronic, worsening headache in an older patient. Respond with acknowledgement, empathy and reassurance.

  • Site: right side, always the same side (a fixed unilateral headache is concerning).
  • Intensity: 6-7/10.
  • Quality: dull ache.
  • Onset: 6 months, on and off, gradually worsening.
  • Radiation: none.
  • Alleviating/aggravating: nothing noted.
  • Affect on life: assess as it is chronic.

Brain Tumour Red Flags

Night waking; early-morning headache and vomiting; worse on coughing/sneezing; neurological deficit (weakness, numbness, pins and needles, blurred vision); weight loss; personal/family history of cancer.

Temporal Arteritis Screen and Polymyalgia Rheumatica

Temporal arteritis (older patient, unilateral headache): tender cord over the temple; blurred vision (the most important symptom, signalling threatened vision); headache worse on chewing (jaw claudication) and combing; and polymyalgia rheumatica — shoulder and hip pain/stiffness (positive: 6 months of shoulder aches).

Probe the shoulder pain (key characteristics, not full SIQORAA): stiffness/difficulty moving the shoulders; worse in the morning; worse after rest — an inflammatory pattern typical of PMR.

Rule Out Other Differentials

Migraine: nausea/vomiting, photophobia/phonophobia, aura (skip triggers). Tension headache: home/work stress (patient often retired). Trigeminal neuralgia: pain triggered by facial movement. Infections: rash (shingles), sore neck (cervicogenic), dental hygiene/toothache, post-nasal drip (sinusitis). ICH/SAH: trauma, falls, blood thinners (an elderly patient with recurrent falls on warfarin is high-risk for chronic subdural haemorrhage). Plus cervicogenic, cluster, glaucoma, medication-overuse headache and BIH.

Closure, Diagnosis and Differential

Closure with SADMA, past and family history. Diagnosis: "I'm concerned about temporal arteritis — a blood vessel at the side of your head becomes inflamed by your own immune system." Reasons: fixed unilateral headache, age over 50, and shoulder pain consistent with polymyalgia rheumatica. Differentials: brain tumour (no red flags), migraine (no nausea/photophobia), tension headache (no stress), infections (dental, sinusitis), chronic subdural haemorrhage if falls, cluster, glaucoma, medication overuse and BIH.

Management

Request ESR today but do NOT wait for it — start oral prednis(ol)one immediately and continue until results return and the patient is handed to the specialist for tapering. If visual loss has already developed, send the patient to the ED for IV steroids. ESR gives initial support; temporal artery biopsy provides confirmation. The feared complication is irreversible vision loss, which prompt steroids aim to prevent.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The note describes PMR as 'essentially the same as Joint cell Arteritis' (an OCR mangling of 'giant cell arteritis').
Temporal arteritis is giant cell arteritis (GCA). PMR and GCA are related conditions on the same spectrum but are not identical: GCA is large-vessel arteritis threatening vision, while PMR is an inflammatory condition causing proximal (shoulder/hip) girdle pain and stiffness. Roughly 15-20% of PMR patients have GCA and vice versa.
✏️ The starting prednisolone dose is given loosely as '50 mg once daily' for all presentations.
Per Therapeutic Guidelines, uncomplicated GCA (no visual symptoms) is treated with prednis(ol)one about 1 mg/kg up to 60 mg daily (commonly 40-60 mg); GCA WITH visual loss or amaurosis requires urgent IV methylprednisolone pulse therapy. Start steroids immediately on clinical suspicion - do not wait for ESR/CRP or biopsy - and arrange temporal artery biopsy within about 1-2 weeks. Add CRP (more reliable than ESR alone) and gastro/bone protection given high-dose long-course steroids.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add CRP (not just ESR) and note temporal/axillary artery ultrasound (halo sign) as an increasingly used first-line investigation in Australia, plus platelet count which is often elevated.
⭐ Include steroid-course safety counselling: PPI/bone protection (calcium, vitamin D, bisphosphonate consideration), blood glucose monitoring, steroid card, and do-not-stop-abruptly advice - high-yield AMC safety points.
⭐ Emphasise the sight-saving red flag explicitly: any visual symptom (amaurosis fugax, diplopia, visual loss) is an ophthalmic emergency requiring same-day IV steroids and ophthalmology - critical for scoring.
⭐ For Young District Hospital (rural), note limited ophthalmology/rheumatology access, use of telehealth, and low threshold for transfer if visual symptoms develop.
