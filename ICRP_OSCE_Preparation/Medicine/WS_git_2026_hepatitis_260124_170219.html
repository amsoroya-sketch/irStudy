Hepatitis and Jaundice: Differential Diagnosis, Travel History and LFT Interpretation

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / class 5 liver cases (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Reviewing Jaundice: A Diagnostic Strategy

Hepatitis is inflammation of the liver; it is often better approached via the presenting complaint of jaundice (yellow discolouration of skin).

Probability diagnoses: viral hepatitis A/B/C; gallstones in the common bile duct (cholecystitis); alcoholic hepatitis/cirrhosis.

Serious disorders not to be missed: malignancy (pancreas, biliary tract, liver, metastases) causing obstructive jaundice; severe infection (sepsis, cholangitis, hepatitis, HIV); rarities (Wilson disease, acute fatty liver of pregnancy).

Pitfalls: gallstones, autoimmune hepatitis, haemochromatosis, viral infections (EBV, CMV); cardiac failure causing hepatic congestion (look for jaundice on general appearance in heart failure).

Drug-induced jaundice: antibiotics (Augmentin, flucloxacillin, erythromycin). Haemolysis: G6PD deficiency, spherocytosis (pre-hepatic jaundice).

Red flags in jaundice: unexplained weight loss, progressive painless jaundice (painless favours malignancy over hepatitis), oedema, cerebral dysfunction (hepatic encephalopathy).

Case A - 40-year-old Traveller with RUQ Pain and Jaundice

Task: take history, explain diagnosis and differentials.

Opening: confirm haemodynamic stability (BP, PR, RR, temperature); introduce; open question - RUQ pain, recently returned from a South-East Asian country. Offer analgesia. Explore pain (SIQORAA): site, intensity, quality, onset (continuous, not worsening), radiation (shoulder/back), aggravating/relieving factors.

Travel history (ABCDEFG): Activities/destination/duration; Bushwalking/insect bites; Contact with sick people/animals (zoonoses); Drugs; Exposure to blood (tattoo, piercing, transfusion, procedures); Food (street food - positive); GP pre-travel vaccination and malaria prophylaxis. Sexual history can be asked here.

Case A - Differentials Screen, Risk Factors and Diagnosis

General GI: nausea/vomiting, bloating, wind, diarrhoea/constipation, fever (none).

Liver and GB red flags: cancer questions (weight loss, appetite, lumps, tiredness, family history) - all negative. Hepatitis: jaundice (positive), itch, dark urine/pale stool. Gallbladder: fatty-food pain, previous surgery/strictures.

Hepatitis risk factors (ABP FIT NO SEX): Alcohol/Antibiotics, Blood, Piercing, Food, IV drugs, Tattoo/Travel, Needle-stick/Occupation, Sexual history (safe sex, sex overseas).

Further screen: pancreas (alcohol, trauma, hyperlipidaemia); PUD (spicy food, medications, coffee/tea); GORD (heartburn); ACS/pneumonia (chest pain, SOB, cough).

Findings: RUQ pain, jaundice, no fever (not cholangitis), street food; note: patient has had a cholecystectomy.

Diagnosis: acute hepatitis, most likely hepatitis A (faeco-oral) from street food. Differentials: hepatitis B/C (blood/sexual), malignancy (liver/GB/pancreas), gallstone disease, antibiotic-induced hepatitis, pancreatitis, PUD/GORD, ACS/lower-lobe pneumonia, travel-related infection (malaria, dengue, EBV, CMV, chikungunya).

Cholangitis Contrast and LFT Patterns

Why skip full hepatitis risk factors in the cholangitis case? When the triad RUQ pain + jaundice + fever is present, the context points to cholangitis (stones/tumours), and the shorter (4-minute) station prioritises ruling out differentials over an exhaustive liver questionnaire.

LFT patterns:

  • Hepatocellular (drained ALT/AST/GGT): AST/ALT/GGT markedly raised, small ALP rise.
  • Cholestatic: ALP markedly raised, small AST/ALT/GGT rise.
  • Isolated hyperbilirubinaemia: raised bilirubin, normal AST/ALT/ALP.

Worked examples referenced in the notes include a mild ALP rise with bilirubin 40 (mixed/cholestatic) and a hepatocellular pattern with modestly raised AST/ALT and elevated triglycerides/cholesterol (suggesting metabolic/fatty liver).

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The diagnosis of hepatitis A is reached without stating confirmatory serology or notification.
Confirm with anti-HAV IgM and a full acute viral panel (HBsAg, anti-HBc IgM, anti-HCV/HCV RNA); check INR and albumin for synthetic function. Hepatitis A is NOTIFIABLE in NSW - notify public health and offer post-exposure vaccine/immunoglobulin to contacts.
✏️ Haemochromatosis is listed only as a pitfall without the Australian context.
Haemochromatosis (HFE-related) is the commonest inherited liver disorder in Australians of Northern European descent; where chronic hepatocellular LFT derangement is unexplained, add iron studies (transferrin saturation, ferritin) and HFE genotyping to the work-up.
