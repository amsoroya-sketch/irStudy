Unwell / Fever in a Returned Traveller (Acute Hepatitis A)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med workshop 2 - 2026 / CLASS 5 UNWELL (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: general_practice

Case Overview

A patient presents feeling 'unwell' and requests a repeat medical certificate. The 'unwell' presentation is deliberately vague and must be worked up systematically. Structured differential clusters for the undifferentiated unwell patient include: infection (viral, bacterial, travel-related), malignancy (leukaemia, lymphoma), endocrine (diabetes, DKA, hypothyroidism), hepatic (hepatitis), haematological (anaemia), psychological (depression) and cardiopulmonary (pulmonary embolism, cardiac and respiratory disease).

Final provisional diagnosis: acute Hepatitis A secondary to contaminated food exposure during overseas travel (Jakarta, Indonesia).

Opening, Consent and the Certificate Request

Introduce yourself and clarify the request. The patient asks only for another medical certificate; the safe response is to agree to provide it while explaining that a few questions are needed first to make sure they are safe and well.

  • 'I am happy to provide another medical certificate, that will not be a problem.'
  • 'Would it be okay if I ask a few questions first, just to make sure you are safe and well?'

Exploring 'Unwell' and the Fever

Ask the patient to define 'unwell' (energy, sleepiness, fever). This patient reports feeling feverish, shifting the differential cluster towards infection until proven otherwise.

  • Confirm fever: measured temperature? (patient has not checked)
  • Associated: chills; treatment tried (paracetamol, not helping)
  • Timing: several weeks; pattern (constant vs intermittent); progression (worsening)
  • Aggravating and relieving factors

Travel and Exposure History (ABCDE)

Any returned traveller is a special group. Screen for malaria, dengue, hepatitis, TB, HIV, typhoid, Ross River fever, chikungunya, CMV, leptospirosis and meningococcal disease.

  • Destination and duration: Jakarta, Indonesia, ~1 week
  • A Activities (attending a conference does not exclude exposure)
  • B Bushwalking (positive)
  • C Contact with sick people (no) and animals (no)
  • D Drug use overseas (no)
  • E Exposure: blood products, tattoos, piercings (no); street food (yes); unbottled water (no); sexual contact overseas (no)
  • Pre-travel GP visit and vaccinations

Two positive risk factors identified: bushwalking and street food.

Targeted Differential Questions

Bushwalking cluster (tick/insect-borne: malaria, dengue, Ross River): joint pain, body aches, headache, rash.

Street food cluster (gastroenteritis or hepatitis): abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, blood in stool; and hepatitis features - yellow eyes (positive), dark tea-coloured urine (positive), pale stools (positive), epigastric discomfort (positive).

Complete the screen rather than jumping straight to a liver work-up. Also cover STI screen, sore throat / flu-like symptoms, and a red-flag malignancy screen (weight loss, appetite loss, lumps, lymph nodes, night sweats, family history of cancer).

Physical Examination (from Examiner)

General appearance: jaundice present (key finding); no cachexia, rash or pallor. Vital signs essentially normal, no fever at time of exam. No lymphadenopathy. Normal CVS and respiratory exam.

Abdominal examination (core): scratch marks present (pruritus of cholestasis), no ascites or distension; superficial and deep tenderness in the right upper quadrant; no guarding, rigidity or rebound; tender hepatomegaly, no splenomegaly; no shifting dullness; normal bowel sounds. Urine dipstick: bilirubin positive.

Positive findings: jaundice, scratch marks, RUQ tenderness, tender hepatomegaly, bilirubinuria.

Investigations and Explanation

FBC, LFTs. Results: AST 200, ALT 250, GGT elevated - a hepatocellular pattern. Hepatitis B and C serology negative.

Confirmatory test: Hepatitis A IgM serology (positive IgM = acute Hepatitis A).

Explain in plain language that the liver is inflamed and some liver cells are being damaged, most likely from a virus (Hepatitis A) acquired through contaminated food or water during travel.

Differential Diagnosis

  • Travel-related infections: gastroenteritis, dengue, Ross River fever, malaria, tick-borne illness, Q fever, atypical pneumonia, TB, HIV, syphilis, typhoid
  • Other viral hepatitis: Hepatitis B, C (serology negative), and Hepatitis E (also faeco-oral, endemic in the region)
  • Malignancy: leukaemia, lymphoma, hepatocellular/pancreatic cancer
  • Other: drug-induced hepatitis, autoimmune hepatitis

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The case interprets an elevated white cell count as confirming 'an infection somewhere in the body' when explaining results.
In acute viral hepatitis the WCC is typically normal or low with a relative/atypical lymphocytosis; a markedly elevated neutrophil count should instead prompt consideration of bacterial co-infection or an alternative diagnosis. Avoid teaching that a raised WCC confirms the hepatitis diagnosis.
✏️ Differential list omits Hepatitis E despite a returned traveller from Indonesia with a faeco-oral exposure (street food).
Hepatitis E is faeco-orally transmitted, common in developing countries and a recognised cause of acute traveller's hepatitis; it should be listed alongside Hepatitis A and requested on serology, particularly as it can be severe in pregnancy.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Apply SOCRATES to the epigastric/RUQ discomfort rather than noting it in passing - site, character and radiation help distinguish hepatitis from biliary colic/cholangitis.
⭐ Add a management and public-health section: Hepatitis A is a nationally notifiable disease in Australia (notify the local public health unit), management is largely supportive (rest, hydration, avoid alcohol and hepatotoxic drugs, review medications), and household/close contacts should be offered Hepatitis A vaccine or normal human immunoglobulin per the current Australian Immunisation Handbook.
⭐ State the standard acute hepatitis serology panel explicitly (Hep A IgM, HBsAg/anti-HBc IgM, anti-HCV with HCV RNA, Hep E IgM) plus EBV/CMV serology given the traveller context.
⭐ Add safety-netting and infection-control advice: hand hygiene and food-handling precautions, exclusion from food-handling work while infectious, and advice to return if drowsy, confused or bleeding (signs of fulminant hepatitis).
