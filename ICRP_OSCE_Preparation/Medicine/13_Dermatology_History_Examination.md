## SECTION 1: DERMATOLOGY HISTORY (4 minutes)

### Opening Statement (30 seconds)

**Word-for-word script:**

"Good morning/afternoon, I'm Dr [Your Name], one of the doctors here at Young District Hospital. Before we start, can I please confirm your name and date of birth? Thank you. I understand you've come in today with concerns about a skin problem. I'd like to ask you some questions about this to understand what's happening, and then with your permission, I'll examine the affected area. The whole consultation should take about 8 minutes. Is that okay with you?"

**[Pause for patient consent - essential for dermatology examinations]**

"Can I also confirm - do you have any concerns about me examining your skin today? Some areas may require removing clothing, and I can arrange a chaperone if you'd prefer."

---

### Focused History - SKIN RASH Framework

**Purpose:** Systematic approach to differentiate the Top 15 dermatological presentations (eczema, psoriasis, cellulitis, melanoma, viral exanthem, drug eruption, tinea, scabies, shingles, acne, rosacea, contact dermatitis, urticaria, impetigo, meningococcal rash)

#### **S - SITE (Location & Distribution)**

"Where did the rash/lesion first appear?"
- **Why ask:** Helps narrow differentials immediately
  - Face → Rosacea, acne, SLE, seborrhoeic dermatitis
  - Flexures (elbows, knees) → Eczema, psoriasis
  - Trunk → Drug eruption, viral exanthem, pityriasis rosea
  - Dermatomal → Shingles (herpes zoster)
  - Hands/feet → Contact dermatitis, fungal, hand-foot-mouth disease
  - Interdigital → Scabies (web spaces), tinea pedis

"Has it spread? Where to? How quickly?"
- Rapid spread (hours) → Meningococcal (EMERGENCY), urticaria, cellulitis
- Slow spread (days-weeks) → Psoriasis, eczema, tinea
- Centrifugal (from trunk outward) → Viral exanthem

**Differential Clue:**
- **Single site, not spreading** → Localized: melanoma, BCC, SCC, dermatofibroma
- **Multiple sites symmetrically** → Systemic: drug eruption, viral, psoriasis
- **Dermatomal distribution** → Shingles (⭐⭐⭐ - very common in AMC exams)

---

#### **K - KIND/CHARACTER (Morphology)**

"Can you describe what it looks like?"

**Use patient-friendly terms, then translate to medical:**

**Primary Lesions:**
- **"Flat red patches"** → Macules (viral exanthem, drug eruption)
- **"Raised bumps"** → Papules (psoriasis, lichen planus)
- **"Fluid-filled blisters"** → Vesicles <5mm (shingles, eczema, contact dermatitis)
- **"Larger blisters"** → Bullae >5mm (pemphigus, bullous pemphigoid, Stevens-Johnson)
- **"Pus-filled spots"** → Pustules (acne, impetigo, folliculitis)
- **"Scaly patches"** → Plaques (psoriasis, eczema, tinea)
- **"Lumps under skin"** → Nodules (melanoma, lipoma, cyst)

**Secondary Changes:**
- **"Crusting/weeping"** → Impetigo, infected eczema
- **"Scaling"** → Psoriasis, tinea, eczema
- **"Scratch marks"** → Excoriation (eczema, scabies - very itchy)
- **"Thickened skin"** → Lichenification (chronic eczema)
- **"Darker patches"** → Hyperpigmentation (post-inflammatory)
- **"Lighter patches"** → Hypopigmentation (vitiligo, pityriasis alba)

**CRITICAL QUESTION:**
"Does the rash blanch when you press on it?" / "If I gave you a glass to press on it, does it go white?"
- **Blanches** → Most rashes (vasodilation - benign)
- **Non-blanching** → EMERGENCY - Meningococcal septicaemia, vasculitis, thrombocytopenia

---

#### **I - ITCH (Pruritus)**

"Does it itch? How severe - mild, moderate, or really bothering you?"

**Differential by itch severity:**

**VERY ITCHY (patient can't sleep):**
1. **Scabies** (⭐⭐) - Worse at night, interdigital, family contacts
2. **Eczema** (⭐⭐⭐) - Flexural, dry skin, childhood onset
3. **Urticaria** (⭐⭐) - Migratory wheals, each lesion <24 hours
4. **Contact dermatitis** (⭐⭐) - New exposure, localized to contact site

**MODERATELY ITCHY:**
5. **Psoriasis** (⭐⭐⭐) - Can be itchy, but less than eczema
6. **Tinea** (⭐⭐) - Ring-shaped, expanding border
7. **Pityriasis rosea** - "Herald patch" then Christmas tree pattern

**MINIMAL/NO ITCH:**
8. **Acne** (⭐⭐) - Tender, not itchy
9. **Rosacea** (⭐) - Facial flushing, burning sensation
10. **Melanoma** (⭐⭐⭐) - Usually asymptomatic (may bleed or itch if advanced)
11. **Drug eruption** (⭐⭐⭐) - Variable, can be very itchy or not

**"Is the itch worse at any particular time?"**
- **Night-time worse** → Scabies (diagnostic)
- **After hot shower** → Eczema, urticaria
- **After exercise** → Cholinergic urticaria

---

#### **N - NEW Exposures/Triggers**

**This is the MOST IMPORTANT question for narrowing differentials**

**"Have you started any new medications in the last 2-4 weeks?"**
- **Drug eruption** (⭐⭐⭐ - very common)
  - Common culprits (Australia): Antibiotics (penicillin, sulfonamides), anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, phenytoin), allopurinol, NSAIDs
  - Timeline: Usually 7-14 days after starting drug
  - EMERGENCY signs: Mucosal involvement (mouth, eyes) → Stevens-Johnson syndrome

**"Any new soaps, detergents, creams, perfumes, jewellery?"**
- **Contact dermatitis** (⭐⭐)
  - Nickel (belt buckles, jewellery), fragrance, preservatives
  - Occupational: hairdressers (dyes), builders (cement), healthcare workers (latex, chlorhexidine)

**"Any new foods you've eaten?"**
- **Urticaria** (⭐⭐) - IgE-mediated
  - Nuts, shellfish, eggs
  - Ask about angioedema (lip/tongue swelling) - anaphylaxis risk

**"Have you been anywhere different - bushwalking, camping, beach?"**
- **Tinea** (⭐⭐) - Gyms, swimming pools (shared surfaces)
- **Jellyfish stings** - Australian beaches (box jellyfish in Far North Queensland - life-threatening)
- **Tick bites** - Rural NSW (can cause allergic reactions, Lyme-like illness)

**"Anyone else at home or close contacts with similar rash?"**
- **Scabies** (⭐⭐) - Highly contagious
- **Viral exanthem** (⭐⭐) - Measles, rubella, chickenpox (though rare post-vaccination)
- **Impetigo** (⭐⭐) - School children, contact sports

**"Any recent illnesses - cold, flu, sore throat?"**
- **Viral exanthem** - Rash appears 3-5 days after fever
- **Guttate psoriasis** - 2 weeks after strep throat

---

### Associated Symptoms (1 minute)

**Constitutional:**
"Have you had any fever, chills, sweats?"
- **Fever + rash** → Meningococcal (EMERGENCY), cellulitis, viral exanthem, drug reaction (DRESS)
- **No fever** → Eczema, psoriasis, melanoma (localized process)

**Systemic:**
"Any joint pain, muscle aches, headache?"
- **Arthralgia + rash** → Psoriatic arthritis, SLE, viral (rubella, parvovirus B19)

**Respiratory:**
"Any difficulty breathing, wheezing, throat tightness?"
- **ANAPHYLAXIS** (EMERGENCY) - Urticaria + angioedema + respiratory compromise

**Mucosal involvement (CRITICAL - indicates severe disease):**
"Any rash or sores in your mouth, eyes, or genital area?"
- **Stevens-Johnson syndrome** (⭐⭐⭐ EMERGENCY) - Drug-induced, mucosal erosions, target lesions
- **Erythema multiforme** - Post-HSV, target lesions, palms/soles
- **Behçet's disease** - Recurrent oral/genital ulcers

---

### Past Medical History (30 seconds)

**"Do you have any history of skin conditions - eczema, psoriasis, skin cancers?"**
- **Atopic triad**: Eczema, asthma, hay fever (allergic march)
- **Psoriasis**: Often chronic relapsing, family history
- **Previous skin cancers**: High risk for new lesions (Australia has highest rates globally)

**"Any other medical conditions - diabetes, kidney disease, immune problems?"**
- **Immunosuppressed** (HIV, chemotherapy, transplant) → Higher risk cellulitis, fungal, viral (shingles)
- **Diabetes** → Diabetic dermopathy, candida, slow healing

---

### Medications & Allergies (30 seconds)

**Current medications:**
"What medications are you currently taking - including over-the-counter, vitamins, herbal?"
- **New medications** → Drug eruption
- **Immunosuppressants** → Infection risk (cellulitis, fungal)

**Allergies:**
"Have you had any allergic reactions to medications before? What happened?"
- Document reaction type: True allergy (anaphylaxis, angioedema) vs intolerance (nausea, rash)
- **Penicillin allergy** → Very common in Australia, affects antibiotic choice for cellulitis

---

### Social History (30 seconds)

**Sun exposure (CRITICAL in Australia):**
"How much sun exposure do you get? Do you work outdoors?"
- **Australia has highest UV index globally** - outdoor workers (farmers, builders) at very high risk
- "Do you use sunscreen regularly? What SPF?"
- Previous sunburns, especially blistering burns in childhood → Melanoma risk

**Occupation:**
"What do you do for work?"
- **Occupational exposures**: Healthcare (latex), hairdresser (chemicals), builder (cement), farmer (sun exposure)

**Smoking/Alcohol:**
- **Smoking**: Psoriasis worse, impairs wound healing
- **Alcohol**: Rosacea trigger, psoriasis worse

**Travel:**
"Have you travelled recently - overseas or within Australia?"
- **Tropical infections**: Dengue (returning from Queensland/NT), leishmaniasis (overseas)
- **Different climate**: Eczema worse in dry climates (central Australia)

---

### Family History (30 seconds)

"Any family history of skin conditions - psoriasis, eczema, melanoma?"
- **Psoriasis**: Strong genetic component (30% if one parent) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.395)
- **Atopic eczema**: Hereditary (allergic march)
- **Melanoma**: Family history is major risk factor
- **Skin cancer syndromes**: Familial atypical mole melanoma (FAMMM) syndrome

## SECTION 2: SKIN EXAMINATION (4 minutes)

### Preparation (30 seconds)

**Position:**
- **Full skin examination**: Patient standing in underwear, adequate lighting
- **Localized lesion**: Expose affected area + surrounding skin for distribution

**Chaperone:**
"For examinations that require removing clothing, I'd like to have a chaperone present. Is that okay?"
- **ALWAYS offer chaperone** for examinations of breasts, genitals, buttocks

**Consent:**
"I'm going to examine your skin now. I'll need to look at the rash/lesion closely and may touch it gently. Is that alright?"

---

### The 5 Ps Framework Applied to Dermatology

#### **1. POSITION (30 seconds)**

"Please could you stand up / sit on the examination couch with good lighting."

**Adequate exposure:**
- **Full body examination** (melanoma screening, widespread rash): Patient in underwear
- **Localized rash**: Expose affected area PLUS adjacent areas to assess distribution

**Good lighting:** Natural light best (windows), or bright overhead light

---

#### **2. GENERAL INSPECTION (30 seconds)**

**From the end of the bed/standing back:**

**General appearance:**
- Does patient look unwell? (Septic - meningococcal, cellulitis)
- Comfortable or distressed? (Severe itch - eczema, scabies)
- Signs of scratching? (Excoriations)

**Distribution pattern (CRITICAL for differentials):**

**PATTERNS:**

1. **Symmetrical (bilateral, mirror-image):**
   - **Eczema** (⭐⭐⭐): Flexures (antecubital, popliteal)
   - **Psoriasis** (⭐⭐⭐): Extensor surfaces (elbows, knees), scalp, sacrum
   - **Drug eruption** (⭐⭐⭐): Trunk and limbs, symmetrical
   - **Viral exanthem**: Centrifugal (trunk → limbs)

2. **Asymmetrical (one-sided):**
   - **Shingles** (⭐⭐⭐): Dermatomal (unilateral, doesn't cross midline)
   - **Cellulitis** (⭐⭐⭐): Unilateral leg (often), localized
   - **Tinea corporis**: Asymmetric ring lesions

3. **Localized (single lesion/area):**
   - **Melanoma** (⭐⭐⭐): Solitary pigmented lesion
   - **Basal cell carcinoma**: Face (sun-exposed)
   - **Squamous cell carcinoma**: Ears, scalp, hands

4. **Flexural (skin folds):**
   - **Eczema**: Antecubital fossae, popliteal fossae
   - **Candida**: Groin, under breasts, axillae (warm, moist)
   - **Inverse psoriasis**: Groin, axillae (less scaly than classic psoriasis)

5. **Extensor surfaces:**
   - **Psoriasis**: Elbows, knees (classic)

6. **Dermatomal:**
   - **Shingles**: Unilateral, follows dermatome (T5-L2 most common)

7. **Photo-distributed (sun-exposed areas):**
   - **Rosacea**: Face (cheeks, nose)
   - **SLE**: Malar rash (butterfly, spares nasolabial folds)
   - **Polymorphic light eruption**: Arms, V of neck, back of hands

8. **Interdigital (between fingers/toes):**
   - **Scabies**: Web spaces, wrists, ankles
   - **Tinea pedis**: Between toes

---

#### **3. PALPATION (1 minute)**

**"I'm going to gently feel the rash/lesion now."**

**Temperature:**
- **Warm/hot** → Infection (cellulitis, abscess)
- **Normal** → Most rashes (eczema, psoriasis, drug eruption)

**Tenderness:**
- **Tender** → Cellulitis (⭐⭐⭐), abscess, shingles (before rash appears)
- **Non-tender** → Eczema, psoriasis, most rashes

**Texture:**
- **Smooth** → Macules (flat), urticaria (wheals)
- **Rough/scaly** → Psoriasis (silvery scale), eczema (dry scale), tinea (fine scale)
- **Firm/hard** → Melanoma (invasive), SCC, BCC
- **Soft** → Lipoma, seborrhoeic keratosis

**Blanching test (CRITICAL):**
**"I'm going to press on the rash with this glass slide."**

**Technique:** Use glass slide or clear plastic ruler, press firmly

- **Blanches (goes white)** → Vasodilation (benign - most rashes)
- **Non-blanching (stays red/purple)** → **EMERGENCY**
  - Meningococcal septicaemia (⭐⭐⭐ - DON'T MISS)
  - Vasculitis (Henoch-Schönlein purpura)
  - Thrombocytopenia (petechiae)

**Dermatographism (skin writing):**
Stroke skin firmly with blunt object (pen cap)
- **Positive** (wheal forms) → Urticaria, dermatographism

---

#### **4. PERCUSSION**

**Not routinely used in dermatology**

Exception: Neurological signs in shingles (sensory loss in dermatome)

---

#### **5. AUSCULTATION**

**Not routinely used in dermatology**

Exception: Vascular lesions (bruit in arteriovenous malformation - rare)

### LESION DESCRIPTION Framework: "ABCDEFG"

**For ANY discrete lesion (especially suspicious pigmented lesions), describe systematically:**

#### **A - ASYMMETRY**

"Is the lesion symmetrical or asymmetrical?"

- **Symmetrical** → Benign (seborrhoeic keratosis, naevus)
- **Asymmetrical** → **Melanoma** (⭐⭐⭐ - one half doesn't match other half)

**Technique:** Draw imaginary line through middle - do both halves match?

---

#### **B - BORDER**

"Is the border regular (smooth, well-defined) or irregular (jagged, notched, blurred)?"

- **Regular, well-defined** → Benign (naevus, BCC initially)
- **Irregular, jagged, notched** → **Melanoma** (⭐⭐⭐)

---

#### **C - COLOR**

"Is the color uniform (one color) or varied (multiple colors)?"

**Colors:**
- **Brown/black** → Melanocytic (naevus, melanoma)
- **Red** → Vascular (haemangioma, BCC with telangiectasia)
- **Pink/pearly** → BCC (classic)
- **Skin-colored** → SCC, dermatofibroma

**Uniform (one color):**
- Benign naevi usually uniform brown

**Varied (multiple colors - red, white, blue, black):**
- **Melanoma** (⭐⭐⭐ - multiple colors within same lesion is HIGHLY suspicious)

---

#### **D - DIAMETER**

"Measure the lesion - is it >6mm?"

**Technique:** Use ruler (in OSCE, state "I would measure with a ruler")

- **<6mm** → Usually benign (but small melanomas exist)
- **>6mm** → **Increased suspicion for melanoma** (⭐⭐⭐)

**Rule:** "Larger than a pencil eraser" (6mm)

---

#### **E - EVOLUTION**

"Has the lesion changed over time - size, shape, color, symptoms?"

**Ask patient:**
- "How long has this been there?"
- "Has it changed in size, color, or appearance?"
- "Any new symptoms - bleeding, itching, pain?"

**CHANGING lesion** → **MELANOMA** (⭐⭐⭐ - most important single feature)
- New lesion in adult >40 years
- Existing mole changing over weeks-months

**Stable for years** → Benign naevus, seborrhoeic keratosis

---

#### **F - FEEL (Texture)**

"What does it feel like - smooth, rough, scaly, hard?"

**Smooth:**
- Naevus (mole), dermatofibroma, lipoma

**Rough/scaly:**
- **Solar keratosis** (actinic keratosis - pre-malignant)
- **SCC** (⭐⭐⭐ - scaly, indurated, doesn't heal)
- Psoriasis (silvery scale)

**Hard/firm:**
- **Melanoma** (invasive)
- **SCC**
- **BCC** (less firm)

**Soft:**
- Lipoma, skin tag

---

#### **G - GENERAL DISTRIBUTION**

"Is it a single lesion or multiple lesions?"

**Solitary:**
- Melanoma (⭐⭐⭐ - "ugly duckling sign" - one different from others)
- BCC, SCC

**Multiple similar lesions:**
- Naevi (moles) - most people have 10-40
- Seborrhoeic keratoses (elderly)

**Dermatomal:**
- Shingles (clustered vesicles in dermatome)

---

### Specific Regional Examinations

#### **SCALP (if relevant)**

"I'm going to part your hair to examine your scalp."

**Look for:**
- **Psoriasis**: Silvery plaques at hairline, behind ears
- **Seborrhoeic dermatitis**: Greasy yellow scales (dandruff)
- **Melanoma**: Scalp is common site (especially in men)
- **Head lice**: Nits (eggs) on hair shafts

---

#### **NAILS (ALWAYS examine in psoriasis/eczema)**

**Psoriasis nail changes (50% of psoriasis patients):** (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.903)
- **Pitting**: Small depressions
- **Onycholysis**: Nail separation from nail bed
- **Oil drop sign**: Yellow-brown discoloration
- **Subungual hyperkeratosis**: Thickening under nail

**Eczema:**
- **Transverse ridging**: Beau's lines
- **Pitting**: Less common than psoriasis

**Fungal (onychomycosis):**
- **Yellow discoloration**, thickening, crumbling

**Melanoma:**
- **Longitudinal melanonychia**: Dark streak in nail (subungual melanoma - rare but serious)

---

#### **MUCOUS MEMBRANES (if relevant - severe rashes)**

**"Can I look inside your mouth?"**

**Indicates severe disease if involved:**
- **Stevens-Johnson syndrome** (⭐⭐⭐ EMERGENCY): Erosions, blistering of lips, oral mucosa, eyes (conjunctivitis)
- **Erythema multiforme**: Target lesions on palms/soles, oral ulcers (post-HSV)
- **Lichen planus**: White lacy pattern (Wickham's striae) on buccal mucosa

---

#### **LYMPH NODES**

**Palpate regional lymph nodes draining affected area:**

**Enlarged, tender:**
- **Cellulitis** (⭐⭐⭐): Inguinal nodes (leg cellulitis), axillary (arm)
- **Impetigo**: Regional lymphadenopathy

**Enlarged, hard, non-tender:**
- **Melanoma metastases** (⭐⭐⭐): Sentinel node involvement

**Technique:**
- Leg cellulitis → Palpate inguinal nodes
- Arm cellulitis → Palpate axillary nodes
- Scalp lesion → Palpate occipital, post-auricular, cervical nodes

---

### Closing the Examination (30 seconds)

**"Thank you, that's completed the examination. You can get dressed now / I'll step outside while you dress."**

**"To summarize, I've examined your [describe area], and I can see [brief description]. Based on your history and examination, I think this is likely [top differential]. I'd like to arrange some tests / treatment. Do you have any questions?"**

## SECTION 3: TOP 15 DERMATOLOGY PRESENTATIONS (AMC Clinical, p.385)

### 1. ECZEMA (Atopic Dermatitis) ⭐⭐⭐

**Frequency:** Appears in 15-20% of AMC Clinical exams

**Clinical Features:**
- **Distribution**: Flexures (antecubital, popliteal fossae), neck, wrists, ankles
- **Morphology**: Erythematous papules/plaques, dry scaly skin, excoriations (scratch marks)
- **Symptoms**: VERY itchy (worse at night), chronic relapsing
- **Age**: Usually starts in childhood (60% before age 1), can persist into adulthood (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.3)
- **Atopic triad**: Eczema + asthma + allergic rhinitis (hay fever)

**History clues:**
- "Itchy rash since childhood, worse in winter (dry skin)"
- "In the creases of elbows and knees"
- "Scratches at night, can't sleep"
- Family history of eczema/asthma/hay fever

**Examination findings:**
- **Acute**: Weeping, crusting, vesicles
- **Chronic**: Lichenification (thickened skin from chronic scratching), hyperpigmentation
- **Distribution**: Flexural (adults), face/extensor (infants)
- **Secondary infection**: Honey-colored crusting (staph aureus), grouped vesicles (eczema herpeticum - emergency)

**Differentials:**
1. **Contact dermatitis**: Localized to exposure site, no atopic history
2. **Psoriasis**: Extensor surfaces, well-demarcated plaques, less itchy
3. **Scabies**: Interdigital, very itchy, contacts affected

**Investigations (Australian context):**
- **Usually clinical diagnosis** - no tests needed
- **Skin swab** if infected (honey crusting) - free with Medicare
- **Allergy testing** (RAST/skin prick) if food allergies suspected (paediatric eczema) - specialist referral
- **Patch testing** if contact dermatitis suspected - dermatology referral (free in public system)

**Management (eTG 2024):**

**1. Education (cornerstone):**
- "Eczema is a chronic skin condition caused by a barrier defect - your skin doesn't hold moisture well"
- "It's not contagious, not due to poor hygiene"
- "Avoid triggers: harsh soaps, hot showers, wool, stress"

**2. Emollients (MOST IMPORTANT):**
- **"Moisturize, moisturize, moisturize"** - apply liberally 3-4 times daily
- **Australian PBS-subsidized options:**
  - **QV Cream** (very popular in Australia)
  - **Cetaphil Moisturizing Cream**
  - **Aqueous cream** (cheap, but can irritate - less recommended now) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.3)
  - **Sorbolene with 10% glycerine** (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.3)
- Apply within 3 minutes of bathing ("soak and seal")
- Use 250-500g per week for adult

**3. Topical corticosteroids:**
- **Mild** (face, flexures, children): **Hydrocortisone 1%** (OTC in Australia) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.73)
- **Moderate** (body, adults): **Betamethasone valerate 0.05%** (Betnovate) - PBS (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.762)
- **Potent** (severe flares, thick plaques): **Mometasone furoate 0.1%** (Elocon) - PBS (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.434)
- **Regimen**: Apply ONCE daily to active lesions for 5-7 days, then reduce frequency
- **Apply AFTER emollient** (wait 30 minutes)
- **Fingertip unit (FTU)**: Amount from fingertip to first crease = 0.5g (covers 2 adult palms)

**Side effects (counsel patient):**
- Skin thinning (prolonged use, especially face)
- Striae (stretch marks)
- Telangiectasia (visible blood vessels)
- **To minimize**: Use lowest potency effective, pulse therapy (weekends only), avoid face/groin

**4. Topical calcineurin inhibitors (steroid-sparing):**
- **Tacrolimus 0.03% ointment** (Protopic) - PBS for >2 years (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.3)
- **Pimecrolimus 1% cream** (Elidel) - PBS (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.3)
- Use for sensitive areas (face, neck, flexures) - no skin thinning
- Burning sensation common initially (warn patient)

**5. Antihistamines (for itch):**
- **Sedating** (night-time): **Promethazine** (Phenergan) 25mg nocte - PBS
- **Non-sedating** (day): **Cetirizine** 10mg daily, **fexofenadine** 180mg daily - OTC (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)
- Helps sleep, reduces scratching

**6. Secondary infection:**
- **Honey crusting** → Staph aureus
  - **Topical**: **Mupirocin 2% ointment** (Bactroban) TDS for 5 days - PBS <!-- NEEDS CITATION -->
  - **Oral** (extensive): **Flucloxacillin** 500mg QID for 5 days (if penicillin allergy: **Cephalexin** 500mg QID) - PBS (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)
- **Eczema herpeticum** (grouped vesicles, punched-out erosions) → HSV - EMERGENCY
  - Admit, IV **aciclovir**

**7. Severe refractory eczema:**
- Dermatology referral
- Phototherapy (UVB)
- Systemic immunosuppressants: Cyclosporin, azathioprine, methotrexate
- Biologics: **Dupilumab** (Dupixent) - PBS for severe atopic dermatitis (March 2024) - monoclonal antibody (anti-IL-4/IL-13)

**Safety-netting:**
"Please return or see your GP if:
- The eczema is getting worse despite treatment
- You develop honey-colored crusting (infection)
- You develop painful blisters or sores (possible herpes infection - needs urgent treatment)
- Fever or feeling unwell"

**Follow-up:**
- GP review in 2 weeks to assess response
- Long-term management plan (chronic condition)

### 2. PSORIASIS ⭐⭐⭐

**Frequency:** Appears in 15-20% of AMC Clinical exams

**Clinical Features:**
- **Distribution**: Extensor surfaces (elbows, knees), scalp, sacrum, nails
- **Morphology**: Well-demarcated erythematous plaques with silvery-white scale
- **Symptoms**: Mildly itchy (less than eczema), chronic relapsing
- **Age**: Bimodal peaks (15-25 years, 50-60 years)
- **Auspitz sign**: Pinpoint bleeding when scale removed (diagnostic but don't perform routinely)

**History clues:**
- "Scaly patches on elbows and knees for years"
- "Comes and goes, worse with stress"
- "Not very itchy"
- Family history (30% if one parent has psoriasis) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.903)
- Triggers: Stress, strep throat (guttate psoriasis), medications (beta-blockers, lithium, antimalarials), alcohol, smoking

**Examination findings:**
- **Plaque psoriasis** (90%): Chronic, thick, well-demarcated plaques with silvery scale (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.1031)
- **Guttate psoriasis**: Small drop-like lesions (post-strep throat), sudden onset
- **Flexural (inverse) psoriasis**: Smooth red plaques in groin, axillae (no scale due to moisture)
- **Nail changes** (50%): Pitting, onycholysis, oil drop sign (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.903)
- **Scalp**: Thick plaques at hairline, behind ears
- **Koebner phenomenon**: New lesions at sites of trauma (scratches, scars)

**Differentials:**
1. **Eczema**: Flexural, more itchy, ill-defined borders
2. **Tinea corporis**: Ring-shaped, expanding border, KOH positive
3. **Seborrhoeic dermatitis**: Greasy yellow scale (not silvery), scalp/face

**Investigations (Australian context):**
- **Clinical diagnosis** - usually no tests needed
- **Skin biopsy** if uncertain (dermatology) - shows parakeratosis, acanthosis
- **Throat swab** if guttate psoriasis (strep throat trigger)
- **Screen for psoriatic arthritis** (10-30% develop): Joint pain, morning stiffness, dactylitis (sausage digits) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.438)

**Management (eTG 2024):**

**1. Education:**
- "Psoriasis is a chronic immune-mediated skin condition"
- "Not contagious, not due to poor hygiene"
- "Triggers: stress, infections, some medications, alcohol, smoking"
- "Can improve with treatment but usually chronic relapsing"

**2. Topical therapies (first-line):** (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.7)

**Topical corticosteroids:**
- **Potent**: **Betamethasone dipropionate 0.05%** (Diprosone) - PBS (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.7)
- **Very potent**: **Mometasone furoate 0.1%** (Elocon) - PBS (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.726)
- Apply ONCE daily, reduce to alternate days when controlled
- **Avoid prolonged use** (skin thinning) - pulse therapy (weekends only)

**Vitamin D analogues (steroid-sparing):**
- **Calcipotriol 0.005% ointment** (Daivonex) - PBS (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.3)
- Apply BD (max 100g/week)
- Works slower than steroids (4-6 weeks)
- Can combine with steroids: **Calcipotriol/betamethasone** (Daivobet, Enstilar foam) - PBS

**Coal tar:**
- **Coal tar 2-5%** preparations (OTC) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.227)
- Cheap, effective, but messy and smelly (poor compliance)
- Apply at night, cover with old clothes

**Salicylic acid (keratolytic):**
- **Salicylic acid 5-10%** (OTC) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.684)
- Removes thick scale (descaling)
- Use before other topicals to improve penetration

**3. Scalp psoriasis:**
- **Betamethasone valerate 0.1% scalp application** (Bettamousse) - PBS (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.5)
- **Coal tar shampoo** (OTC): Pinetarsol, Polytar
- Descale first with **salicylic acid in coconut oil** (overnight, then shampoo)

**4. Phototherapy (moderate-severe):**
- **Narrowband UVB** - 3x/week for 8-12 weeks
- Dermatology referral, hospital-based
- Effective for extensive psoriasis (>10% BSA) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.3)

**5. Systemic therapies (severe, >10% BSA, failed topicals):** (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.3)
- **Methotrexate** 10-25mg weekly (oral/SC) + folic acid 5mg next day - PBS
  - Monitor: FBC, LFTs, UEC (hepatotoxic, bone marrow suppression)
  - Avoid alcohol
  - Contraception essential (teratogenic)
- **Acitretin** (retinoid) 25-50mg daily - PBS (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)
  - Severe dryness, teratogenic (2 years contraception post-cessation)
- **Cyclosporin** 2.5-5mg/kg/day - PBS
  - Renal toxicity, hypertension (monitor BP, creatinine)

**6. Biologics (severe, failed conventional systemic):**
- **TNF-alpha inhibitors**: **Adalimumab** (Humira), **Etanercept** (Enbrel) - PBS (Authority)
- **IL-17 inhibitors**: **Secukinumab** (Cosentyx), **Ixekizumab** (Taltz) - PBS (Authority)
- **IL-12/23 inhibitor**: **Ustekinumab** (Stelara) - PBS (Authority)
- Expensive (~$20,000/year), PBS requires Authority (dermatologist)

**7. Psoriatic arthritis (co-management with rheumatology):**
- NSAIDs, DMARDs (methotrexate), biologics

**Lifestyle:**
- **Weight loss** (obesity worsens psoriasis)
- **Reduce alcohol** (trigger)
- **Quit smoking** (trigger)
- **Stress management**

**Safety-netting:**
"Please return or see your GP if:
- Joint pain or swelling develops (psoriatic arthritis)
- Psoriasis covering >10% of body (need stronger treatment) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.581)
- Worsening despite treatment"

**Follow-up:**
- GP review 4-6 weeks
- Dermatology referral if severe or not responding

### 3. CELLULITIS ⭐⭐⭐

**Frequency:** Appears in 20-25% of AMC Clinical exams (common medical admission)

**Clinical Features:**
- **Definition**: Acute bacterial infection of dermis and subcutaneous tissue
- **Common sites**: Lower leg (70%), arm, face (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.1101)
- **Causative organisms**:
  - **Streptococcus pyogenes** (Group A Strep) - most common
  - **Staphylococcus aureus** - especially if purulent/abscess
  - **Pasteurella multocida** - cat/dog bites
- **Portal of entry**: Tinea pedis (toe web intertrigo - most common), trauma, insect bite, ulcer, IV drug use

**History clues:**
- "Red, hot, swollen leg for 2 days, getting worse"
- "Painful to walk on"
- "Fever and chills"
- Risk factors: Diabetes, peripheral vascular disease, lymphoedema, obesity, immunosuppression
- Portal of entry: "I have athlete's foot between my toes" (tinea pedis - VERY common in Australia)

**Examination findings:**
- **Acute inflammation**: Erythema (red), calor (heat), tumor (swelling), dolor (pain)
- **Unilateral** leg involvement (usually)
- **Poorly demarcated** borders (unlike erysipelas)
- **Warmth** on palpation
- **Tender**
- **Regional lymphadenopathy** (inguinal for leg cellulitis)
- **Systemic**: Fever (>38°C), tachycardia
- **Mark border** with pen (to monitor progression)

**Differentials (CRITICAL - cellulitis is often over-diagnosed):**

1. **Deep vein thrombosis (DVT)** (⭐⭐⭐ - DON'T MISS)
   - Similar presentation: Red, swollen, painful leg
   - **Differences**: DVT usually calf tenderness, Homan's sign (unreliable), recent immobility/surgery/long flight
   - **Work-up**: Wells score, D-dimer, Doppler ultrasound
   - **TREAT cellulitis first if uncertain** (antibiotics safe), then investigate DVT

2. **Venous stasis dermatitis (varicose eczema)**
   - Chronic, bilateral, brown pigmentation (hemosiderin), varicose veins
   - Not acutely tender or hot
   - Treat with compression stockings, emollients

3. **Lipodermatosclerosis**
   - Chronic venous insufficiency → fibrosis of subcutaneous fat
   - "Inverted champagne bottle" appearance of leg
   - Chronic, not acute

4. **Erysipelas** (superficial cellulitis)
   - Strep pyogenes
   - **Well-demarcated**, raised border (vs poorly demarcated in cellulitis)
   - Face common (butterfly distribution)

5. **Necrotizing fasciitis** (⭐⭐⭐ EMERGENCY - DON'T MISS)
   - "Flesh-eating disease" - surgical emergency
   - **Red flags**: Pain out of proportion, rapidly progressive, skin necrosis (purple/black), crepitus (gas in tissues), systemic toxicity (shock)
   - **LRINEC score** >6 → high risk
   - **Management**: URGENT surgical debridement + IV antibiotics + ICU

**Investigations (Australian context):**

**Blood tests (if systemically unwell or admission):**
- **FBC**: Leucocytosis (WCC >11)
- **CRP**: Elevated (monitors response to treatment)
- **Blood cultures** (if fever >38°C) - before antibiotics

**Imaging:**
- **Not routinely required** for uncomplicated cellulitis
- **Doppler ultrasound** if DVT suspected
- **X-ray** if gas gangrene suspected (crepitus), foreign body, osteomyelitis
- **MRI** if necrotizing fasciitis suspected (deep tissue involvement)

**Microbiology:**
- **Skin swab** of purulent material (if abscess) - free with Medicare
- **Blood cultures** if septic

**Management (eTG 2024):**

**Severity assessment (determines oral vs IV antibiotics, admission):**

**Mild (outpatient oral antibiotics):**
- Localized cellulitis, no systemic signs, patient well
- No comorbidities (diabetes, immunosuppression)

**Moderate (consider admission):**
- Larger area, systemic signs (fever, tachycardia)
- Comorbidities (diabetes, PVD)

**Severe (ADMIT):**
- Septic (fever >38.5°C, hypotension, tachycardia >100)
- Rapidly progressive
- Facial cellulitis (risk of cavernous sinus thrombosis)
- Immunosuppressed

**Antibiotic choice (eTG 2024):**

**ORAL (mild cellulitis, outpatient):**

**First-line:** (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.7)
- **Cephalexin** 500mg QID (or 1g BD) for 5-10 days - PBS (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)
  OR
- **Dicloxacillin** 500mg QID for 5-10 days - PBS (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)

**If penicillin allergy:**
- **Cefalexin** 500mg QID (cross-reactivity low if non-anaphylactic PCN allergy) (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)
  OR
- **Trimethoprim+sulfamethoxazole** (Bactrim) 160/800mg BD - PBS (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)
  OR
- **Doxycycline** 100mg BD - PBS (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)

**If MRSA suspected** (IVDU, previous MRSA, purulent discharge):
- Add **Trimethoprim+sulfamethoxazole** (Bactrim) 160/800mg BD (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)

**IV (severe cellulitis, admission):**

**First-line:** (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.7)
- **Flucloxacillin** 2g IV Q6H (QID)
  PLUS
- **Benzylpenicillin** 1.2g IV Q6H
  (Combination covers Strep pyogenes + Staph aureus)

**If penicillin allergy:**
- **Vancomycin** 25-30mg/kg loading, then 15-20mg/kg IV BD (monitor levels)

**If necrotizing fasciitis suspected:**
- **Piperacillin-tazobactam** 4.5g IV Q8H
  PLUS
- **Clindamycin** 600mg IV Q8H (toxin suppression)
  PLUS
- **URGENT surgical review** (debridement)

**Adjunctive management:**

1. **Elevate affected limb** (reduces swelling)
2. **Analgesia**: Paracetamol 1g QID, ibuprofen 400mg TDS (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)
3. **Mark border** with pen (date and time) - track progression
4. **Treat portal of entry**:
   - **Tinea pedis** (athlete's foot - VERY common cause): **Clotrimazole 1% cream** BD for 4 weeks (OTC) <!-- NEEDS CITATION -->
   - **Venous ulcer**: Compression bandaging
5. **Prophylaxis** (recurrent cellulitis >2 episodes/year):
   - **Benzathine penicillin** 1.2 million units IM monthly (Bicillin LA) - PBS
   - Treat tinea pedis aggressively

**Admission criteria (Young District Hospital):**
- Severe cellulitis (septic, hypotensive)
- Facial cellulitis
- Failed outpatient treatment (worsening after 48 hours oral antibiotics)
- Comorbidities (diabetes, immunosuppression, PVD)
- Social issues (can't manage oral meds, no follow-up)

**Safety-netting (CRITICAL for outpatient management):**

"Please call 000 or return to ED immediately if:
- The redness is spreading rapidly (mark the border, take a photo)
- You develop fever >38.5°C or feel very unwell
- You develop blistering or black areas on the skin (skin dying)
- You feel confused or dizzy (sepsis)

Please see your GP in 48 hours if:
- No improvement after 2 days of antibiotics (we may need to change antibiotics or admit you to hospital)
- To monitor progress"

**Follow-up:**
- GP review 48-72 hours (assess response, ensure border not progressing)
- If not improving → admission for IV antibiotics
- Dermatology referral if recurrent (>2 episodes/year) - prophylaxis, investigate lymphoedema

### 4. MELANOMA (Suspicious Pigmented Lesion) ⭐⭐⭐

**Frequency:** Appears in 20-25% of AMC Clinical exams

**Why High-Yield:** Australia has the HIGHEST melanoma incidence globally (1 in 17 Australians by age 85). Essential for safe GP practice.

**Clinical Features:**
- **Definition**: Malignant tumor of melanocytes
- **Risk factors** (Australia-specific):
  - **Sun exposure**: Outdoor work (farmers, builders), childhood sunburns
  - **Fair skin**: Fitzpatrick types I-II (burn easily, tan poorly)
  - **High UV index**: Australia has highest UV globally
  - **Family history**: FAMMM syndrome (familial atypical mole melanoma)
  - **Multiple naevi** (>50 moles)
  - **Previous melanoma**: 10% lifetime risk of second melanoma (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.1044)
  - **Immunosuppression**: Organ transplant (50-100x risk)

**History clues:**
- "I've had this mole for years, but it's changed recently - darker, bigger"
- "A new spot appeared a few months ago and keeps growing"
- "It bleeds when I scratch it"
- "I'm an outdoor worker" (farmer, builder - high sun exposure in Australia)
- **"Ugly duckling sign"**: Patient points out one lesion that looks different from others

**ABCDEFG Examination Framework (apply systematically):**

**A - Asymmetry:**
- Melanoma: One half doesn't match other half
- Benign naevi: Symmetrical

**B - Border:**
- Melanoma: Irregular, notched, jagged, blurred
- Benign naevi: Smooth, well-defined

**C - Color:**
- Melanoma: **Multiple colors** within same lesion (black, brown, red, white, blue) - HIGHLY suspicious
- Benign naevi: Uniform brown

**D - Diameter:**
- Melanoma: Usually >6mm (larger than pencil eraser)
- Benign naevi: <6mm
- **BUT small melanomas exist** - don't dismiss <6mm if other features present

**E - Evolution:**
- Melanoma: **CHANGING** over weeks-months (size, shape, color, symptoms)
- **MOST IMPORTANT FEATURE** - new lesion in adult >40, or changing existing mole
- Benign naevi: Stable for years

**F - Feel:**
- Melanoma: Firm, may be nodular (invasive)
- Benign naevi: Soft, flat or slightly raised

**G - General distribution:**
- Melanoma: Usually solitary lesion (ugly duckling)
- Check for satellite lesions (in-transit metastases - poor prognosis)

**Additional features:**
- **Bleeding**: Melanoma may bleed spontaneously or with minor trauma
- **Itching**: Can occur in melanoma
- **Ulceration**: Advanced melanoma

**Subtypes of melanoma:**

1. **Superficial spreading melanoma** (70%): (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.1044)
   - Most common
   - Flat, irregular, multiple colors
   - Trunk, legs

2. **Nodular melanoma** (15%): (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.1044)
   - Most aggressive
   - Rapidly growing nodule (weeks-months)
   - Uniform dark color (black/blue)
   - Trunk, head/neck
   - **Vertical growth phase immediately** (no horizontal phase) - poor prognosis

3. **Lentigo maligna melanoma** (10%): (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.1044)
   - Elderly, sun-damaged skin (face)
   - Large (>3cm), irregular, slow-growing (years)
   - Arises from lentigo maligna (in-situ melanoma)

4. **Acral lentiginous melanoma** (5%): (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.1044)
   - Palms, soles, subungual (under nail)
   - **More common in darker skin** (Asians, Africans - where other types rare)
   - Longitudinal melanonychia (dark streak in nail)
   - Poor prognosis (often diagnosed late)

**Examination:**
- **Full skin examination** (melanoma patients at high risk of second melanoma)
- **Describe lesion** using ABCDEFG
- **Palpate regional lymph nodes**:
  - Leg lesion → Inguinal nodes
  - Arm lesion → Axillary nodes
  - Head/neck → Cervical nodes
  - Hard, non-tender lymph node → Metastasis

**Differentials:**

1. **Benign naevus (mole)**:
   - Symmetrical, regular border, uniform color, <6mm, stable
   - Very common (most people have 10-40)

2. **Seborrhoeic keratosis** ("senile wart"):
   - Elderly, "stuck-on" appearance, warty surface
   - Brown/black, well-demarcated
   - **Benign** - no malignant potential

3. **Solar lentigo** ("age spot", "liver spot"):
   - Sun-exposed areas (hands, face)
   - Flat, uniform brown macule
   - Benign

4. **Dermatofibroma**:
   - Firm nodule, "dimple sign" (dimples when squeezed)
   - Legs common
   - Benign

5. **Basal cell carcinoma** (BCC):
   - Pearly papule/nodule, telangiectasia, central ulceration ("rodent ulcer")
   - **Most common skin cancer** (but not melanoma)
   - Locally invasive, almost never metastasizes

6. **Squamous cell carcinoma** (SCC):
   - Scaly, indurated nodule, doesn't heal
   - Sun-exposed areas
   - Can metastasize (5-10%) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.3)

**Investigations (Australian context):**

**Dermoscopy (dermatoscopy):**
- Non-invasive, in-clinic
- Magnifies skin lesion (10x), polarized light
- Identifies melanoma-specific features (asymmetric pigment network, blue-white veil, atypical vascular patterns)
- **Sensitivity ~90%** when used by trained clinician (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.783)
- **GP training**: RACGP offers dermoscopy courses

**Excisional biopsy (GOLD STANDARD):**
- **Complete excision** with 2mm margins (narrow margin to allow histology, wider if confirmed melanoma)
- **Elliptical excision** (oriented along skin tension lines for cosmesis)
- Send to pathology: **Breslow thickness** (depth in mm - most important prognostic factor)

**Staging (if melanoma confirmed):**

**Breslow thickness:**
- **<1mm** (thin): Excellent prognosis (98% 5-year survival) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.3)
- **1-2mm**: Good prognosis (90-95% 5-year survival) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.1010)
- **2-4mm**: Intermediate prognosis (70-80% 5-year survival) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.646)
- **>4mm** (thick): Poor prognosis (50-60% 5-year survival) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.905)

**Sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB):**
- If Breslow >1mm or ulcerated
- Identifies microscopic nodal metastases
- Nuclear medicine scan + surgical excision of sentinel node

**Imaging (if metastatic disease suspected):**
- **CT chest/abdo/pelvis**
- **PET scan**
- **Brain MRI** (melanoma has high brain metastasis rate)

**Management (Australian context - eTG 2024):**

**1. Suspicious lesion (in primary care):**

**URGENT REFERRAL to dermatology/skin cancer clinic:**
- **2-week rule**: Suspected melanoma should be seen by specialist within 2 weeks

**OR**

**Excision by GP (if trained and confident):**
- Complete excision with 2mm margins
- Send to pathology
- If melanoma confirmed → refer to dermatology/surgical oncology

**2. Confirmed melanoma:**

**Wide local excision (WLE):**
- **Margin depends on Breslow thickness:**
  - **In-situ (lentigo maligna)**: 5mm margin
  - **<1mm**: 10mm margin
  - **1-2mm**: 10-20mm margin
  - **>2mm**: 20mm margin
- Performed by dermatologist or surgical oncologist

**Sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB):**
- If Breslow >1mm or ulcerated
- If positive → Complete lymph node dissection (controversial now - may observe)

**3. Adjuvant therapy (if high-risk):**

**Immunotherapy (stage III-IV):**
- **Pembrolizumab** (Keytruda - anti-PD-1) - PBS for stage III/IV melanoma
- **Nivolumab** (Opdivo - anti-PD-1) - PBS
- Dramatically improved survival in metastatic melanoma (from months to years)

**Targeted therapy (if BRAF V600E mutation - 50% of melanomas):** (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.359)
- **Dabrafenib + Trametinib** (BRAF + MEK inhibitors) - PBS

**4. Surveillance:**

**High-risk patients (multiple naevi, previous melanoma, family history):**
- **3-monthly skin checks** for first 2 years
- **6-monthly** for next 3 years
- **Annual** lifelong
- Full body photography (mole mapping) - specialist clinics

**General population (Australia):**
- **Annual skin check** by GP or dermatologist (Australia-specific recommendation due to high incidence)

**Prevention (CRITICAL in Australia - highest UV globally):**

**Sun protection:**
- **Slip** (on sun-protective clothing)
- **Slop** (on SPF 50+ sunscreen - reapply every 2 hours)
- **Slap** (on wide-brimmed hat)
- **Seek** (shade, especially 10am-3pm)
- **Slide** (on sunglasses)

**Australia-specific:**
- **UV index very high** (8-14 in summer, even higher in tropics)
- **"No hat, no play"** policy in schools
- **SunSmart app** (free, shows daily UV index)

**Safety-netting:**

"Please return urgently if:
- Any existing mole changes in size, shape, color, or starts bleeding
- A new dark spot appears
- Any lesion that doesn't heal within 4 weeks"

**Follow-up:**
- Post-excision: Remove sutures in 7-14 days (face 5-7 days, trunk/limbs 10-14 days)
- Histology results in 1-2 weeks
- If melanoma confirmed → Urgent dermatology/oncology referral

### 5. VIRAL EXANTHEM (Measles, Rubella) ⭐⭐

**Frequency:** Appears in 10-15% of AMC Clinical exams

**Clinical Features:**
- **Definition**: Widespread rash caused by viral infection
- **Common viruses**: Measles, rubella, roseola, parvovirus B19, enterovirus (Coxsackie), EBV

**MEASLES (Rubeola)** - ⭐⭐⭐ DON'T MISS (notifiable disease in Australia)

**History:**
- **Immunization status**: Non-immunized or single dose MMR (need 2 doses)
- **Recent travel**: Overseas (measles outbreaks in Europe, Asia)
- **Contacts**: Recent exposure to measles case
- **Timeline**: Rash appears ~14 days post-exposure

**Prodrome (3-5 days before rash):**
- **3 Cs**: Cough, Coryza (runny nose), Conjunctivitis
- **High fever** (>39°C)
- **Malaise**

**Rash:**
- **Day 0-1**: Koplik spots (pathognomonic) - white spots on buccal mucosa (opposite 2nd molars) - appear 2 days before rash
- **Day 1-3**: Maculopapular rash starts on **face** (hairline, behind ears), spreads **centrifugally** (head → trunk → limbs)
- **Day 4-7**: Rash becomes confluent, brownish, then fades with fine desquamation

**Examination:**
- **3 Cs**: Red eyes (conjunctivitis), runny nose, cough
- **Koplik spots** (if early) - diagnostic
- **Maculopapular rash**: Face → trunk → limbs (centrifugal spread)
- **Blanches** on pressure

**Complications (why measles is serious):**
- **Otitis media** (7-9%) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.726)
- **Pneumonia** (6%) - viral or secondary bacterial (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.315)
- **Diarrhea** (8%) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.461)
- **Encephalitis** (0.1%) - can cause permanent brain damage (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.3)
- **Subacute sclerosing panencephalitis (SSPE)**: Rare, fatal, years after measles (persistent CNS infection)
- **Immunosuppression**: Measles causes transient immunosuppression (weeks-months) → secondary infections

**Investigations:**
- **Clinical diagnosis** (if typical presentation)
- **NOTIFIABLE DISEASE** in Australia → Notify public health unit immediately
- **Serology**: Measles IgM (positive from day 3 of rash)
- **Throat swab** / **urine PCR** for measles virus
- **Measles viral culture** (if available)

**Management:**
- **Supportive care**: Paracetamol for fever, fluids, rest
- **Isolate**: Airborne precautions (measles is highly contagious - R0 = 12-18, one of most contagious diseases)
- **Notify public health** (mandatory in Australia)
- **Contact tracing**: Identify non-immune contacts, offer MMR within 72 hours (post-exposure prophylaxis)
- **Vitamin A supplementation** (WHO recommendation, especially in malnourished children - reduces mortality)

**Prevention:**
- **MMR vaccine**: 2 doses (12 months, 18 months) - free on National Immunisation Programmes (NIP)
- **Catch-up**: Anyone born >1966 should have 2 documented MMR doses

**RUBELLA (German Measles)** - ⭐⭐⭐ DON'T MISS (teratogenic)

**History:**
- **Immunization status**: Non-immunized
- **Pregnancy status**: **CRITICAL** - rubella in pregnancy causes congenital rubella syndrome (CRS)
- **Recent travel/contacts**

**Prodrome:**
- **Mild** (unlike measles): Low-grade fever, malaise, headache
- **Lymphadenopathy**: Post-auricular, suboccipital, cervical (appears before rash, lasts weeks)

**Rash:**
- **Pink maculopapular rash**: Face → trunk → limbs (centrifugal)
- **Fades in 3 days** ("three-day measles")
- **Fine, less confluent** than measles
- May have **Forchheimer spots** (petechiae on soft palate)

**Examination:**
- **Lymphadenopathy**: Post-auricular, suboccipital (diagnostic clue)
- **Fine pink rash**: Face → body
- **Arthralgia** (adults, especially women) - knees, wrists, fingers

**Congenital Rubella Syndrome (CRS):**
If pregnant woman infected in **first trimester** (especially <12 weeks):
- **Cardiac defects**: PDA, pulmonary stenosis
- **Eye defects**: Cataracts, glaucoma
- **Hearing loss**: Sensorineural deafness
- **Microcephaly**, intellectual disability
- **Risk**: 80-90% if infected <12 weeks gestation (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.765)

**Investigations:**
- **NOTIFIABLE DISEASE** in Australia
- **Serology**: Rubella IgM (confirms acute infection)
- **If pregnant**: URGENT obstetric referral

**Management:**
- **Supportive care**
- **Isolate** until 7 days after rash onset
- **Notify public health**
- **Pregnancy**: URGENT obstetric referral, discuss termination if <12 weeks (high CRS risk)

**Prevention:**
- **MMR vaccine**: As per schedule
- **Preconception screening**: All women planning pregnancy should check rubella immunity (free in Australia)

### 6. DRUG ERUPTION (Drug-Induced Rash) ⭐⭐⭐

**Frequency:** Appears in 15-20% of AMC Clinical exams

**Clinical Features:**
- **Common culprits** (Australia):
  - **Antibiotics**: Penicillin, amoxicillin, sulfonamides (Bactrim), cephalosporins
  - **Anticonvulsants**: Carbamazepine, phenytoin, lamotrigine
  - **Allopurinol** (very common in Australia for gout)
  - **NSAIDs**: Ibuprofen, diclofenac
  - **Sulfa drugs**: Sulfasalazine (IBD), sulfonylureas (diabetes)

**History clues:**
- **"Started new medication 7-14 days ago, now rash all over"**
- **Timeline**: Usually 7-14 days after starting drug (can be sooner if re-exposed)
- **Worse with continued use**

**Types of drug eruptions:**

**1. Morbilliform (Maculopapular) Drug Eruption** (most common 95%): (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.891)
- **Widespread symmetrical** maculopapular rash
- **Trunk and limbs** (spares palms/soles usually)
- **Itchy**
- **Blanches**
- **No mucosal involvement**
- **Mild systemic symptoms** (low-grade fever)
- **Benign** - resolves 1-2 weeks after stopping drug

**2. Urticarial Drug Eruption:**
- **Wheals** (raised, itchy, transient - each <24 hours)
- **IgE-mediated** (true allergy)
- Risk of **anaphylaxis** if continued

**3. Fixed Drug Eruption:**
- **Recurrent** in same site each time drug taken
- **Round/oval erythematous patch** → becomes hyperpigmented
- **Common sites**: Genitals, lips, hands
- **Common drugs**: NSAIDs, sulfonamides, tetracyclines

**4. DRESS Syndrome** (Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms) - ⭐⭐⭐ SEVERE:
- **Delayed onset**: 2-8 weeks after starting drug
- **Widespread rash** (morbilliform, may become erythrodermic)
- **Facial oedema** (characteristic)
- **High fever** (>38.5°C)
- **Lymphadenopathy**
- **Eosinophilia** (blood test)
- **Organ involvement**: Hepatitis (elevated LFTs - most common), nephritis, pneumonitis, myocarditis
- **Common drugs**: Allopurinol, anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, phenytoin), sulfonamides
- **Mortality**: 10% (organ failure) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.950)

**5. Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) / Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN)** - ⭐⭐⭐ EMERGENCY:
- **Severe mucocutaneous reaction**
- **Prodrome**: Flu-like illness (fever, malaise) 1-3 days
- **Rash**: **Target lesions** (central clearing), then **blistering and epidermal detachment**
- **MUCOSAL INVOLVEMENT** (diagnostic): Mouth (painful erosions), eyes (conjunctivitis, can → blindness), genitals
- **Positive Nikolsky sign**: Lateral pressure on skin causes epidermis to slide off (sheet detachment)
- **Classification by BSA involved:**
  - **SJS**: <10% BSA (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.585)
  - **SJS-TEN overlap**: 10-30% BSA (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.989)
  - **TEN**: >30% BSA (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.3)
- **Common drugs**: Allopurinol, anticonvulsants, sulfonamides, NSAIDs
- **Mortality**: SJS 5%, TEN 30% (sepsis, fluid loss like burns) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.950)

**Examination:**
- **Morbilliform eruption**: Widespread maculopapular rash, trunk and limbs
- **Check mucous membranes**: Mouth, eyes, genitals (if involved → SJS/TEN)
- **Lymphadenopathy**: DRESS syndrome
- **Nikolsky sign**: If positive → SJS/TEN (EMERGENCY)

**Differentials:**
1. **Viral exanthem**: But no new medication
2. **Guttate psoriasis**: Post-strep throat, smaller lesions
3. **Pityriasis rosea**: Herald patch, Christmas tree pattern

**Investigations:**
- **Clinical diagnosis**: History of new drug + rash = drug eruption (until proven otherwise)
- **Eosinophilia**: DRESS syndrome (eosinophils >1.5 x 10^9/L)
- **LFTs, UEC**: Check organ involvement (DRESS)
- **Skin biopsy**: If uncertain (shows interface dermatitis)

**Management:**

**Morbilliform drug eruption (benign):**
1. **STOP the drug** immediately
2. **Antihistamines** for itch: Cetirizine 10mg daily (OTC) (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)
3. **Topical corticosteroids**: Hydrocortisone 1% (mild) for itch <!-- NEEDS CITATION -->
4. **Resolves** 1-2 weeks after stopping drug
5. **Avoid drug** in future (document allergy)

**DRESS syndrome (ADMIT):**
1. **STOP drug** immediately
2. **ADMIT to hospital** (monitor organ function)
3. **Systemic corticosteroids**: Prednisolone 1mg/kg/day (oral) or IV if severe
4. **Monitor**: LFTs (hepatitis), UEC (nephritis), FBC (eosinophilia)
5. **Supportive care**: Fluids, electrolytes
6. **Long taper** of steroids (6-8 weeks) - can relapse if stopped too quickly

**SJS/TEN (EMERGENCY - ADMIT ICU/Burns Unit):**
1. **STOP drug** immediately
2. **ADMIT ICU or Burns Unit** (treat like severe burns)
3. **Supportive care**:
   - **Fluid resuscitation** (massive fluid loss through denuded skin)
   - **Wound care**: Sterile dressings, infection prevention
   - **Analgesia**: Severe pain (morphine)
   - **Nutrition**: Nasogastric feeding
   - **Eye care**: Ophthalmology consult (prevent synechiae, blindness)
4. **Immunotherapy** (controversial):
   - **IVIg (intravenous immunoglobulin)**: 2-3 g/kg over 3 days - may reduce mortality
   - **Cyclosporin**: Alternative
   - **Corticosteroids**: Controversial (some studies show increased mortality, others benefit)
5. **Complications**: Sepsis (most common cause of death), ARDS, multi-organ failure
6. **Mortality**: TEN 30% (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.3)

**Prevention:**
- **Document allergy**: Patient should wear MedicAlert bracelet
- **Pharmacogenomics** (if available):
  - **HLA-B*5801** testing before allopurinol (Asian ancestry) - high risk DRESS/SJS
  - **HLA-B*1502** testing before carbamazepine (Asian ancestry) - high risk SJS/TEN
  - Australia offers HLA-B*5801 testing in some centres (public hospitals)

**Safety-netting:**
"Please call 000 or return to ED immediately if:
- You develop blisters in your mouth or eyes
- Large areas of skin start peeling off
- You develop high fever (>38.5°C)
- You have trouble swallowing or breathing"

### 7. FUNGAL INFECTION - TINEA (Dermatophyte) ⭐⭐

**Frequency:** Appears in 10-15% of AMC Clinical exams

**Clinical Features:**
- **Common types**:
  - **Tinea pedis** (athlete's foot) - most common
  - **Tinea cruris** (jock itch) - groin
  - **Tinea corporis** (ringworm) - body
  - **Tinea capitis** (scalp) - children
  - **Onychomycosis** (nail fungus)

**TINEA PEDIS (Athlete's Foot):**

**History:**
- **"Itchy, scaly skin between my toes"**
- **Risk factors**: Communal showers (gyms, swimming pools), occlusive footwear, hyperhidrosis (sweaty feet)
- **Very common in Australia** (warm climate, beach culture)

**Examination:**
- **Interdigital** (between toes, especially 4th/5th web spaces): Maceration, scaling, fissuring
- **Moccasin type**: Scaling on soles, hyperkeratosis
- **Vesicular type**: Vesicles on soles/sides of feet

**TINEA CRURIS (Jock Itch):**

**History:**
- **"Itchy rash in groin"**
- **Males** > females
- **Risk factors**: Obesity, tight clothing, warm climate, hyperhidrosis

**Examination:**
- **Inguinal folds**: Well-demarcated erythematous plaques with **advancing scaly border**
- **Spares scrotum** (unlike candida which involves scrotum)
- **Bilateral** usually

**TINEA CORPORIS (Ringworm):**

**History:**
- **"Ring-shaped rash on body"**
- **Contacts**: Pets (cats, dogs), farm animals, other people
- **Zoophilic** (from animals): More inflammatory

**Examination:**
- **Annular (ring-shaped)** lesions
- **Advancing scaly border**, central clearing
- **Expanding peripherally**

**TINEA CAPITIS (Scalp Ringworm):**

**History:**
- **Children** usually
- **Scaly patches on scalp, hair loss**
- **Contacts**: Siblings, school

**Examination:**
- **Scaly patches** on scalp
- **Alopecia** (hair loss in patches)
- **Broken hairs** ("black dot" appearance)
- **Kerion**: Severe inflammatory response (boggy, pustular mass) - can cause permanent scarring alopecia

**Differentials:**
1. **Candida** (yeast, not dermatophyte):
   - **Intertrigo**: Groin, under breasts, axillae (skin folds)
   - **Satellite lesions** (small pustules around main rash)
   - **Involves scrotum** (unlike tinea cruris)
2. **Eczema**: Less well-demarcated, no expanding border
3. **Psoriasis**: Silvery scale, extensor surfaces
4. **Erythrasma** (bacterial - Corynebacterium minutissimum):
   - Groin, axillae
   - **Coral-red fluorescence** under Wood's lamp (diagnostic)

**Investigations:**
- **Clinical diagnosis** usually sufficient
- **KOH (potassium hydroxide) preparation**:
  - Scrape scale from advancing border
  - Add KOH → dissolves keratinocytes, leaves fungal hyphae
  - Microscopy: Septate branching hyphae = dermatophyte
  - **Free in GP clinic** (if have microscope)
- **Fungal culture**: If KOH negative but high suspicion, or if not responding to treatment (identify species, antifungal sensitivity)
- **Wood's lamp** (UV light): Some tinea capitis fluoresce green (Microsporum species) - but most don't (Trichophyton - most common)

**Management (eTG 2024):**

**TOPICAL (first-line for localized tinea pedis, cruris, corporis):** (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.493)

**Azoles:**
- **Clotrimazole 1% cream** (Canesten) BD for 4 weeks - OTC <!-- NEEDS CITATION -->
- **Miconazole 2% cream** (Daktarin) BD for 4 weeks - OTC <!-- NEEDS CITATION -->
- **Apply to affected area + 2cm beyond border**

**Allylamines:**
- **Terbinafine 1% cream** (Lamisil) BD for 2 weeks - OTC (shorter duration than azoles) <!-- NEEDS CITATION -->

**Duration:**
- **Tinea pedis**: 4 weeks
- **Tinea cruris**: 2-4 weeks
- **Tinea corporis**: 2-4 weeks

**Adjunctive:**
- **Keep area dry**: Powder (Desenex, antifungal powder), cotton socks (change daily)
- **Avoid occlusive footwear**: Thongs/sandals when possible
- **Treat tinea pedis aggressively** (prevents recurrent cellulitis - very common in Australia)

**ORAL (extensive tinea, tinea capitis, onychomycosis, failed topical):**

**Terbinafine** (first-line): (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.896)
- **250mg daily** - PBS (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)
- **Duration:**
  - Tinea corporis/cruris: 2-4 weeks
  - Tinea capitis: 4 weeks
  - Onychomycosis (fingernails): 6 weeks
  - Onychomycosis (toenails): 12 weeks
- **Monitoring**: LFTs at baseline and 4-6 weeks (hepatotoxic - rare)

**Itraconazole** (alternative):
- **200mg daily** - PBS (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)
- **Pulse therapy for onychomycosis**: 200mg BD for 1 week/month, repeat for 2 pulses (fingernails) or 3 pulses (toenails) (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)

**Griseofulvin** (children, tinea capitis):
- **10mg/kg/day** for 6-8 weeks
- Take with fatty meal (increases absorption)

**Tinea Capitis (always needs oral - topical doesn't penetrate hair):**
- **Terbinafine 250mg daily** for 4 weeks (adults) (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)
- **Griseofulvin 10mg/kg/day** for 6-8 weeks (children)
- **Selenium sulfide shampoo** (Selsun) twice weekly (reduces spore shedding)

**Prevention:**
- **Dry feet thoroughly** after showering (especially between toes)
- **Cotton socks**, change daily
- **Thongs in communal showers**
- **Treat all family members** if tinea capitis (to prevent re-infection)
- **Treat pets** if zoophilic tinea

**Safety-netting:**
"Please return to GP if:
- No improvement after 2 weeks of treatment
- Spreading despite treatment
- You develop fever or increased pain (secondary bacterial infection)"

### 8. SCABIES ⭐⭐

**Frequency:** Appears in 10% of AMC Clinical exams

**Clinical Features:**
- **Causative organism**: *Sarcoptes scabiei* (mite)
- **Transmission**: Close skin-to-skin contact (family members, sexual partners), fomites (bedding, clothes)
- **Incubation**: 4-6 weeks (first infestation), 1-3 days (re-infestation)

**History clues:**
- **"VERY itchy rash, worse at night, can't sleep"** (pathognomonic)
- **"Other family members have similar symptoms"** (highly contagious)
- **Distribution**: Interdigital web spaces, wrists, ankles, axillae, belt line, nipples (women), genitals (men)
- **Spares head and neck** (adults) - but involves scalp in infants

**Examination:**
- **Burrows** (pathognomonic): Linear, wavy, 5-10mm long, grey-brown track (mite burrowing in stratum corneum)
  - **Common sites**: Interdigital web spaces, wrists (flexor aspect), ankles
- **Papules**: Small red papules
- **Excoriations**: Scratch marks (patient can't stop scratching)
- **Secondary infection**: Impetiginization (honey crusting from Staph aureus)
- **Nodules**: Red-brown nodules on genitals, axillae (hypersensitivity reaction - persist for months after treatment)

**Special types:**

**Crusted (Norwegian) scabies:**
- **Immunocompromised** patients (HIV, transplant, elderly in nursing homes)
- **Thick crusts** (thousands-millions of mites, vs 10-15 in ordinary scabies)
- **Highly contagious** (institutional outbreaks)
- **Less itchy** (impaired immune response)

**Differentials:**
1. **Eczema**: But not worse at night, no burrows, no contacts
2. **Contact dermatitis**: Localized to exposure
3. **Dermatitis herpetiformis**: Associated with coeliac disease, grouped vesicles
4. **Pediculosis (lice)**: Itchy, but see lice/nits

**Investigations:**
- **Clinical diagnosis** (itchy rash worse at night + contacts + distribution = scabies)
- **Dermoscopy**: Can visualize mite at end of burrow ("delta sign" - triangular mite)
- **Skin scraping + microscopy**: Scrape burrow, add KOH, microscopy shows mite, eggs, fecal pellets (scybala)
  - **Sensitivity low** (30-50%) - difficult to find mite (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.866)
  - **Negative scraping doesn't exclude scabies** - treat empirically if high suspicion

**Management (eTG 2024):**

**1. Topical scabicides (first-line):** <!-- NEEDS CITATION -->

**Permethrin 5% cream** (Lyclear): (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.882)
- **First-line** (most effective) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.7)
- **Application**: Apply to **entire body** from neck down (include under nails, between toes, genitals)
  - Infants: Include scalp and face (avoid eyes, mouth)
- **Leave on overnight** (8-12 hours), then wash off
- **Repeat in 7 days** (to kill newly hatched mites)
- **PBS-subsidized**

**Benzyl benzoate 25% emulsion** (alternative): (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.3)
- **Cheaper** but less effective, more irritant
- Apply to entire body, leave overnight, wash off
- Repeat on days 1, 2, 8

**Ivermectin oral** (alternative, especially crusted scabies):
- **200 microgram/kg** single dose, repeat in 7-14 days
- **PBS** (Authority for crusted scabies)
- **NOT first-line** (less effective than permethrin) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.7)
- Use for: Crusted scabies, institutional outbreaks, failed topical treatment

**2. Symptomatic treatment:**

**Itch:**
- **Antihistamines**: Promethazine 25mg nocte (sedating - helps sleep) - PBS
- **Topical corticosteroids**: Hydrocortisone 1% (mild) for residual itch/eczema <!-- NEEDS CITATION -->
- **Warn patient**: Itch can persist 2-4 weeks after successful treatment (hypersensitivity reaction to dead mites) - **doesn't mean treatment failed**

**Secondary infection:**
- **Topical**: Mupirocin 2% TDS for 5 days <!-- NEEDS CITATION -->
- **Oral**: Flucloxacillin 500mg QID for 5 days (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)

**3. Environmental decontamination:**
- **Wash all clothes, bedding, towels** in hot water (>60°C), tumble dry
- **Items that can't be washed**: Seal in plastic bag for 72 hours (mites die off host in 2-3 days)
- **Vacuum floors, furniture**

**4. Contact tracing (CRITICAL):**
- **Treat all household members and close contacts simultaneously** (even if asymptomatic)
- **Sexual partners**
- **Childcare/school**: Inform, check other children

**Crusted scabies (special management):**
- **Admit** (highly contagious, needs isolation)
- **Ivermectin oral**: Days 1, 2, 8, 9, 15 (multiple doses)
- **Permethrin 5% cream**: Daily for 7 days, then twice weekly <!-- NEEDS CITATION -->
- **Keratolytic**: Salicylic acid to remove crusts
- **Aggressive environmental decontamination**

**Return to school/work:**
- **After first treatment** (no longer contagious after 24 hours of permethrin)

**Safety-netting:**
"The itch may continue for 2-4 weeks after treatment - this is normal (allergic reaction to dead mites), doesn't mean treatment failed.

Please return if:
- New burrows appear after 2 weeks (treatment failure - may need repeat or different treatment)
- Severe worsening (possible secondary infection)
- Family members develop symptoms (need treatment)"

### 9. SHINGLES (Herpes Zoster) ⭐⭐⭐

**Frequency:** Appears in 15-20% of AMC Clinical exams (very common presentation)

**Clinical Features:**
- **Causative organism**: Varicella-zoster virus (VZV) reactivation
- **Pathophysiology**: Primary infection = chickenpox (varicella) → virus lies dormant in dorsal root ganglia → reactivates years later (shingles)
- **Risk factors**: Age >50, immunosuppression (HIV, cancer, transplant, steroids), stress

**History clues:**
- **"Painful rash on one side of body, in a band"** (dermatomal distribution - pathognomonic)
- **"Pain came first (2-3 days), then rash appeared"** (prodromal neuralgia)
- **Pain description**: Burning, stabbing, electric-shock-like
- **Immunization history**: Chickenpox as child? (If no chickenpox, can't have shingles)

**Timeline:**
- **Day 0-3 (prodrome)**: Dermatomal pain, tingling, hyperaesthesia (increased sensitivity to touch) - **before rash**
- **Day 3-5**: Erythema, then **vesicles** on erythematous base (grouped, dermatomal)
- **Day 5-10**: Vesicles → pustules → crusting
- **Day 10-21**: Crusts fall off, heals

**Examination:**

**Distribution (CRITICAL):**
- **DERMATOMAL** (follows sensory dermatome)
- **UNILATERAL** (doesn't cross midline) - pathognomonic
- **Common dermatomes**:
  - **Thoracic** (T5-L2): Most common (50%) - "band around chest/abdomen" (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.1025)
  - **Trigeminal** (V1-V3): 10-25% - **OPHTHALMIC ZOSTER (V1) = EMERGENCY** (see below) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.300)
  - **Cervical**: Neck, shoulder
  - **Lumbar/sacral**: Buttock, leg

**Morphology:**
- **Grouped vesicles on erythematous base** (classic - "dew drops on a rose petal")
- **Unilateral**, doesn't cross midline
- **Different stages**: Vesicles → pustules → crusts (all stages present)

**Special presentations:**

**1. Ophthalmic zoster (V1 dermatome)** - ⭐⭐⭐ EMERGENCY:
- **Forehead, upper eyelid, tip of nose** (nasociliary branch - **Hutchinson's sign** - if tip of nose involved → 76% have eye involvement) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.860)
- **Complications**: Keratitis, uveitis, glaucoma, blindness
- **URGENT ophthalmology referral** (same day)

**2. Ramsay Hunt syndrome (geniculate ganglion - facial nerve)**:
- **Vesicles in ear canal + facial palsy** (ipsilateral)
- **Hearing loss**, vertigo
- **Worse prognosis** than Bell's palsy

**3. Disseminated zoster**:
- **>20 vesicles outside primary dermatome**
- Indicates **immunosuppression** (HIV, cancer)
- Risk of visceral involvement (pneumonitis, hepatitis, encephalitis)

**Differentials:**
1. **Herpes simplex (HSV)**: Grouped vesicles, but **recurrent same site** (lips - cold sore, genitals), **not dermatomal**
2. **Contact dermatitis**: Vesicles, but **not dermatomal**, exposure history
3. **Cellulitis**: Erythema, painful, but **no vesicles**

**Investigations:**
- **Clinical diagnosis** (dermatomal vesicular rash = shingles)
- **PCR of vesicle fluid**: If uncertain (immunocompromised, atypical presentation) - confirms VZV
- **Tzanck smear**: Scrape vesicle base, stain → multinucleated giant cells (but doesn't differentiate VZV from HSV)

**Complications:**

**1. Post-herpetic neuralgia (PHN)** (most common):
- **Persistent pain >3 months** after rash healed
- **Incidence**: 10-15% overall, **50% in >60 years** (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.70)
- **Pain**: Severe, burning, stabbing, interferes with sleep, quality of life
- **Prevention**: Early antivirals (<72 hours) reduce PHN risk by 50% (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.768)

**2. Ophthalmic complications (V1 zoster):**
- Keratitis, uveitis, glaucoma → blindness

**3. Bacterial superinfection:**
- Staph/Strep → cellulitis, impetigo

**4. Ramsay Hunt syndrome:**
- Facial palsy, hearing loss

**5. Visceral dissemination (immunocompromised):**
- Pneumonitis, hepatitis, encephalitis

**Management (eTG 2024):**

**1. Antivirals (MOST IMPORTANT - reduce duration, severity, PHN risk):**

**Indications for antivirals:**
- **ALL patients if <72 hours** from rash onset
- **>72 hours**: Still give if new vesicles forming, ophthalmic zoster, immunocompromised, severe pain
- **Start ASAP** - efficacy decreases after 72 hours

**First-line:** (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.7)
- **Valaciclovir** 1000mg TDS for 7 days - PBS (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)
  OR
- **Famciclovir** 500mg TDS for 7 days - PBS (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)

**Alternative:**
- **Aciclovir** 800mg 5 times daily for 7 days - PBS (cheaper, but inconvenient dosing)

**Immunocompromised/severe:**
- **Aciclovir IV** 10mg/kg TDS (admit) (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)

**2. Analgesia (PHN prevention):**

**Simple analgesia:**
- **Paracetamol** 1g QID - OTC (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)
- **NSAIDs**: Ibuprofen 400mg TDS - OTC (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)

**Neuropathic pain (if simple analgesia insufficient):**
- **Gabapentin** 300mg nocte, titrate up to 300mg TDS (max 3600mg/day) - PBS (Authority) (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)
  OR
- **Pregabalin** 75mg BD, titrate up to 300mg BD - PBS (Authority) (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)
  OR
- **Amitriptyline** 10-25mg nocte, titrate up to 75mg - PBS (off-label for pain)

**Topical (acute):**
- **Calamine lotion** (soothing) - OTC
- **Lignocaine 5% patches** (Versatis) - PBS (Authority) - for PHN (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.3)

**Opioids (if severe):**
- **Oxycodone** 5mg Q4-6H PRN - PBS (S8 - controlled drug)

**3. Supportive care:**
- **Keep rash clean and dry**
- **Cover rash** (prevents spread - contagious to non-immune via contact with vesicle fluid)
- **Avoid scratching**
- **Cool compresses**

**4. Ophthalmic zoster (URGENT):**
- **Start antivirals immediately** (don't wait for ophthalmology)
- **URGENT same-day ophthalmology referral**
- **Topical antivirals**: Aciclovir eye ointment 3% 5 times daily <!-- NEEDS CITATION -->
- **Mydriatics**: Cyclopentolate (prevent synechiae)

**5. Post-herpetic neuralgia (PHN) - if pain persists >3 months:**
- **Gabapentin** or **pregabalin** (first-line) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.7)
- **Amitriptyline** 25-75mg nocte
- **Lignocaine 5% patches** (Versatis) - PBS (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.1010)
- **Capsaicin 0.075% cream** (depletes substance P) - OTC (burning initially, warn patient) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.7)
- **Pain clinic referral** if refractory

**Prevention:**

**Shingles vaccine (Shingrix - recombinant):**
- **Recommended for all adults ≥50 years** (Australia) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.3)
- **2 doses** (0, 2-6 months)
- **90% effective** at preventing shingles (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.505)
- **Reduces PHN** by 90% (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.3)
- **NOT free** (~$560 for 2 doses) - patient pays (not on NIP)
- **IS free for immunocompromised** ≥18 years (NIP from Feb 2023)

**Zostavax (live attenuated - old vaccine):**
- **No longer recommended** (replaced by Shingrix) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.57)
- 50% effective (lower than Shingrix) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.505)

**Contagiousness:**
- **Contagious to non-immune** (those who haven't had chickenpox) - contact with vesicle fluid causes chickenpox (not shingles)
- **Not airborne** (unlike chickenpox)
- **Cover rash**, avoid contact with pregnant women (non-immune), newborns, immunocompromised
- **Can return to work** if rash covered

**Safety-netting:**

"Please return to ED urgently if:
- Rash involves your eye or tip of nose (risk of blindness)
- You develop severe headache, confusion, or weakness (brain involvement - rare)
- Rash spreads widely beyond original area (disseminated zoster)

Please see your GP if:
- Pain persists after rash heals (post-herpetic neuralgia - we have treatments)
- No improvement after 3 days of antivirals"

**Follow-up:**
- GP review in 1 week (assess response to antivirals, monitor for PHN)
- If PHN develops → consider pain clinic referral

### 10-15. ADDITIONAL COMMON PRESENTATIONS (Brief Summary)

**10. ACNE VULGARIS ⭐⭐**
- Comedones (blackheads/whiteheads), papules, pustules, nodules
- Face, chest, back
- Management: Benzoyl peroxide, topical retinoids (adapalene), oral antibiotics (doxycycline), isotretinoin (severe)

**11. ROSACEA ⭐**
- Facial flushing, telangiectasia, papules/pustules
- No comedones (vs acne)
- Triggers: Alcohol, spicy food, sun, stress
- Management: Metronidazole gel, doxycycline

**12. CONTACT DERMATITIS ⭐⭐**
- Localized to contact site
- Acute: Vesicles, weeping
- Chronic: Lichenification
- Management: Identify trigger, avoid, topical corticosteroids

**13. URTICARIA (Hives) ⭐⭐**
- Wheals (raised, itchy, each <24 hours)
- Triggers: Food, drugs, infection
- Management: Antihistamines, avoid triggers
- Chronic urticaria (>6 weeks): Dermatology referral

**14. IMPETIGO ⭐⭐**
- Honey-colored crusting
- Contagious (school-aged children)
- Staph aureus / Strep pyogenes
- Management: Topical mupirocin, oral flucloxacillin if extensive

**15. MENINGOCOCCAL RASH (Emergency) ⭐⭐⭐**
- NON-BLANCHING purpuric rash
- Fever, headache, neck stiffness, photophobia
- Rapidly progressive (hours)
- **EMERGENCY**: IM/IV benzylpenicillin STAT, urgent transfer to hospital
- Notifiable disease

## COMMON IMG MISTAKES ❌

**1. ❌ Using "diaper rash" instead of "nappy rash"**
- **Australian term**: Nappy rash (infants)
- American term "diaper" immediately flags you as foreign graduate

**2. ❌ Missing meningococcal septicaemia in non-blanching rash**
- **CRITICAL**: Always perform glass test/blanching test
- Non-blanching purpura + fever = meningococcal until proven otherwise
- **DON'T MISS** - fatal within hours if untreated

**3. ❌ Not asking about new medications (drug eruption)**
- Drug eruptions very common (especially antibiotics, allopurinol, anticonvulsants)
- **Always ask**: "Any new medications in last 2-4 weeks?"
- Timeline: Usually 7-14 days after starting drug

**4. ❌ Poor lesion description - not using systematic framework**
- **MUST use ABCDEFG** for pigmented lesions (melanoma screening)
- Examiners expect systematic approach (not "looks suspicious")
- Practice describing lesions using framework

**5. ❌ Not examining whole body for melanoma**
- Melanoma can occur anywhere (including scalp, soles, genitals)
- **Full skin examination** = patient in underwear, check all areas including back, scalp
- Don't just focus on lesion patient points out

**6. ❌ Using non-Australian drug names**
- **WRONG**: Acetaminophen, lidocaine, epinephrine, albuterol
- **CORRECT**: Paracetamol, lignocaine, adrenaline, salbutamol
- Using American drug names = instant fail in some examiners' eyes

**7. ❌ Not safety-netting for melanoma ("return if changes")**
- **MUST tell patient**: "Return if this lesion changes in size, color, shape, or starts bleeding"
- Melanoma is #1 medicolegal risk in Australian dermatology

**8. ❌ Forgetting Australian sun exposure context**
- Australia has **highest UV index globally** (8-14 in summer)
- **MUST ask**: "Do you work outdoors? Do you use sunscreen?"
- Sun protection advice is EXPECTED in every Australian dermatology consult

**9. ❌ Not recognizing tinea pedis as cellulitis portal of entry**
- **Tinea pedis (athlete's foot) = most common cause recurrent cellulitis** in Australia
- If cellulitis → **MUST examine between toes** for tinea
- **MUST treat tinea** to prevent recurrent cellulitis

**10. ❌ Confusing eczema distribution (flexural) with psoriasis (extensor)**
- **Eczema**: FLEXURES (antecubital, popliteal)
- **Psoriasis**: EXTENSORS (elbows, knees)
- Getting this wrong = instant loss of examiner confidence

**11. ❌ Not offering chaperone for intimate skin examinations**
- **MUST offer chaperone** for: Breast examination, genital examination, buttock examination
- "I'd like to have a nurse present as a chaperone. Is that okay?"
- Medicolegal requirement in Australia

**12. ❌ Not documenting penicillin allergy reaction type**
- **MUST ask**: "What happened when you took penicillin?" (rash vs anaphylaxis)
- True allergy (anaphylaxis, angioedema) vs intolerance (rash, nausea)
- Affects antibiotic choice for cellulitis

## INVESTIGATIONS (Australian Context)

### Bedside (GP Clinic)

**1. Wood's Lamp (UV Light):**
- **Erythrasma**: Coral-red fluorescence
- **Some tinea capitis**: Green fluorescence (Microsporum - rare)
- **Porphyria**: Pink-red urine fluorescence
- **Free** (if GP owns lamp)

**2. Dermoscopy:**
- **Melanoma screening**: Identify features (asymmetric pigment network, blue-white veil)
- **Scabies**: Visualize mite ("delta sign")
- **Free** (if GP owns dermatoscope - ~$500-2000)
- **RACGP dermoscopy courses** available

**3. KOH Preparation:**
- **Tinea**: Scrape scale, add KOH, microscopy → septate hyphae
- **Free** (if GP has microscope)

### Pathology (Bulk-Billed with Medicare)

**4. Skin Swab:**
- **Bacterial culture**: Cellulitis, impetigo, infected eczema
- **Viral PCR**: Shingles (VZV), HSV
- **Free with Medicare** (~$30 without)

**5. Fungal Culture:**
- **If KOH negative** but high suspicion for tinea
- **Identify species** and antifungal sensitivity
- **Free with Medicare**

**6. Blood Tests:**
- **FBC**: Eosinophilia (DRESS syndrome), leucocytosis (cellulitis)
- **CRP**: Infection, inflammation
- **LFTs**: DRESS syndrome (hepatitis), methotrexate monitoring
- **UEC**: DRESS (nephritis), cyclosporin monitoring
- **Glucose**: Diabetes (risk factor for cellulitis, candida)
- **Free with Medicare**

### Specialist Investigations (Referral Required)

**7. Skin Biopsy:**
- **Punch biopsy** (3-4mm) - most common
- **Excisional biopsy** - melanoma (complete excision with margins)
- **Shave biopsy** - superficial lesions
- **Dermatology/GP proceduralist**
- **Partial Medicare rebate** (~$200-500 out-of-pocket)

**8. Patch Testing (Contact Dermatitis):**
- Identify allergens (nickel, fragrance, preservatives, etc.)
- **Dermatology referral**
- **Free in public system**, ~$300-500 private

**9. RAST/Skin Prick Testing (Allergies):**
- IgE-mediated allergies (food, environmental)
- **Immunology/allergy specialist referral**
- **Partial Medicare rebate**

**10. Sentinel Lymph Node Biopsy (Melanoma):**
- If Breslow thickness >1mm
- **Nuclear medicine scan** + **surgical excision**
- **Free in public system**

## SAFETY-NETTING (Dermatology-Specific Red Flags)

**General Safety-Netting Statement:**

"Please call 000 or return to ED immediately if you develop:
- **Non-blanching rash** (purple spots that don't go white when pressed with a glass) with fever
- **Rapid spread** of redness (over hours, not days)
- **Blistering in mouth, eyes, or genitals**
- **Facial swelling** or **difficulty breathing** (allergic reaction)
- **Severe pain** out of proportion to rash appearance
- **Black or dead-looking skin**
- **High fever** (>38.5°C) with rash
- **Confusion** or **severe headache** with rash"

**Lesion-Specific Safety-Netting:**

**Melanoma/Suspicious Lesion:**
"Please return urgently if this spot:
- Changes in size, shape, or color
- Starts bleeding or becomes painful
- Develops a new dark area
- Any new spots appear that look different from your other moles"

**Cellulitis:**
"Please return to ED if:
- The redness is spreading (I've marked the border - take a photo to compare)
- You develop fever or feel very unwell
- The area becomes more painful or you develop blisters
- No improvement after 48 hours of antibiotics"

**Shingles:**
"Please return urgently if:
- The rash involves your eye or the tip of your nose (risk of vision loss)
- You develop severe headache or confusion
- The rash spreads widely beyond the original area"

**Eczema/Atopic Dermatitis:**
"Please return if:
- You develop honey-colored crusting (infection)
- Painful blisters or sores appear (possible herpes infection)
- The eczema is getting worse despite treatment"

## FOLLOW-UP & REFERRAL

**When to Refer to Dermatology:**

**URGENT (within 2 weeks):**
- **Suspected melanoma** (ABCDEFG features)
- **Ophthalmic zoster** (shingles involving eye)
- **Stevens-Johnson syndrome / TEN**
- **Severe DRESS syndrome**
- **Rapidly progressive skin condition**

**SEMI-URGENT (within 4-8 weeks):**
- **Severe psoriasis** (>10% BSA, failed topicals) (Talley & O'Connor's Clinical Examination, 8th ed, p.891)
- **Severe atopic eczema** (not controlled with topicals)
- **Recurrent cellulitis** (>2 episodes/year - needs prophylaxis)
- **Blistering disorder** (pemphigus, pemphigoid)
- **Suspected skin cancer** (BCC, SCC)

**ROUTINE:**
- **Acne** (failed topicals, consider isotretinoin)
- **Chronic urticaria** (>6 weeks)
- **Hair loss** (alopecia areata, scarring alopecia)
- **Nail disorders** (severe onychomycosis, nail dystrophy)

**Dermatology Wait Times (NSW Public System - 2024):**
- Urgent: 2-4 weeks
- Semi-urgent: 3-6 months
- Routine: 6-12 months
- **Private**: 2-6 weeks (out-of-pocket ~$200-400 initial consult)

## AUSTRALIAN-SPECIFIC DERMATOLOGY CONTEXT

### Sun Protection (Australia Has Highest UV Globally)

**SunSmart Campaign (Cancer Council Australia):**
1. **Slip** on sun-protective clothing (long sleeves, collared shirt)
2. **Slop** on SPF 50+ broad-spectrum sunscreen (reapply every 2 hours)
3. **Slap** on wide-brimmed hat (≥7.5cm brim all around)
4. **Seek** shade, especially 10am-3pm (UV index highest)
5. **Slide** on sunglasses (UV protection)

**UV Index (Australia):**
- **Summer**: 10-14 (extreme) - even on cloudy days
- **Winter**: 3-8 (moderate-high)
- **SunSmart app**: Free, shows daily UV index by location

**Occupational Sun Exposure (High-Risk Groups in Australia):**
- Farmers, outdoor laborers, builders, lifeguards, sports coaches
- **WorkCover NSW**: Employers must provide sun protection (hats, sunscreen, shade)

### Melanoma Screening Programmes

**National Skin Cancer Awareness:**
- **Free skin checks**: Some GPs offer free skin screening days (Check Club)
- **SPOT** campaign: "Spot the difference" (ugly duckling sign)
- **MoleMap** (private): Full body photography, dermoscopy (expensive ~$300-500)

**Medicare Skin Cancer Rebate:**
- **MBS Item 30071**: Skin cancer check by GP (bulk-billed)
- **MBS Item 31200-31210**: Skin lesion excision (partial rebate)

### Indigenous Australian Dermatology

**Lower Skin Cancer Rates (Higher Melanin):**
- But still occurs - **acral lentiginous melanoma** more common (palms, soles, subungual)

**Higher Rates of:**
- **Scabies**: Especially remote communities (overcrowding)
- **Crusted scabies**: Immunosuppression, diabetes
- **Impetigo**: Post-scabies, post-insect bites
- **Rheumatic fever**: Post-streptococcal (impetigo, pharyngitis)

**Scabies in Remote Communities:**
- **"Healthy Skin" programmes**: Mass treatment campaigns (ivermectin to whole community)
- Reduces scabies → reduces impetigo → reduces rheumatic fever (major health initiative)

**Top 5 Dermatology Presentations (AMC Clinical, p.385):**
1. **Eczema** (⭐⭐⭐): Flexural, itchy, emollients + topical steroids
2. **Psoriasis** (⭐⭐⭐): Extensor, plaques, silvery scale
3. **Cellulitis** (⭐⭐⭐): Red, hot, tender leg - oral cephalexin or IV flucloxacillin

5. **Shingles** (⭐⭐⭐): Dermatomal, unilateral vesicles, antivirals <72 hours

**Must-Know Frameworks:**
- **SKIN rash**: Site, Kind, Itch, New exposures
- **ABCDEFG lesion description**: Asymmetry, Border, Color, Diameter, Evolution, Feel, General distribution

**Emergency Dermatology (DON'T MISS):**
1. **Meningococcal rash**: Non-blanching purpura + fever
2. **Stevens-Johnson syndrome**: Mucosal involvement, blistering
3. **Necrotizing fasciitis**: Pain out of proportion, crepitus
4. **Ophthalmic zoster**: V1 dermatome, Hutchinson sign (tip of nose)
5. **Anaphylaxis**: Urticaria + angioedema + respiratory distress

**Australian Context MUST-MENTION:**
- **Sun protection advice** (every dermatology consult)
- **Melanoma screening** (Australia highest rates)
- **Tinea pedis** as cellulitis portal of entry
- **Australian drug names** (paracetamol, lignocaine)
- **PBS medications** (affordable treatment options)

**IMG Common Mistakes to Avoid:**
1. American terminology (diaper, paracetamol)
2. Missing meningococcal (non-blanching rash)
3. Poor lesion description (not using ABCDEFG)
4. Not offering chaperone (intimate exams)
5. Not safety-netting for melanoma changes
