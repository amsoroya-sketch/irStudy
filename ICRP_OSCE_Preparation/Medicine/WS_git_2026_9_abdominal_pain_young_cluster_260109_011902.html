Chronic Abdominal Pain in a Young Patient (IBD / Coeliac Cluster)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / 2.Abdominal pain case (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Case Overview and Learning Objective

This is a recurrent AMC online-exam cluster (seen repeatedly in 2023-2024). It differs from the usual abdominal pain stations, where 95% of patients are mid-aged to elderly with acute presentations. Here the patient is young and the pain is chronic - 'I have had abdominal pain for a number of months and it is not getting better.'

The learning objective is to expand the gastrointestinal (GI) differential in a young patient with chronic abdominal pain and dominant GI symptoms, rather than anchoring on an acute abdomen. Because there are 6 minutes for history, there is time to broaden the enquiry.

Presenting Scenario and Task

Stem: A young patient presents to general practice complaining of abdominal pain (acuteness not stated). History taking is 6 minutes in a face-to-face exam; all physical examination findings are provided on a card/screen if the station is offline.

Tasks:

  • Take a history (6 minutes), including all red-flag questions.
  • Review the physical examination findings provided.
  • Explain the diagnosis and differentials.

Expanded GI Differential for Chronic Abdominal Pain in the Young

When a young patient has chronic abdominal pain with prominent GI symptoms (diarrhoea/constipation/bloating), expand the GI group:

  • Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) - Crohn disease and ulcerative colitis; this is the age at which it becomes symptomatic.
  • Coeliac disease - often becomes symptomatic at this age.
  • Lactose intolerance - grouped with coeliac as diet-related causes.
  • Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) - psychosocial contribution assessed.
  • Gastroenteritis / chronic infection - travel history important; consider hepatitis.

Beyond the GI group, still screen the KUB group (each urinary differential can occur) and, depending on whether pain is upper or lower, PUD/liver-GB (upper) or pelvic/genital causes (lower). Note: the site is often 'all over', so the pain rarely localises.

Structured History

Haemodynamic stability: confirm BP, PR, RR and temperature with the examiner.

Introduction and rapport: introduce yourself, use an open question, show empathy, address the patient's worry and offer analgesia if needed.

Pain assessment (SIQORAA): Site (often diffuse/'all over'), Intensity (0-10), Quality (dull vs stabbing), Onset (months; constant vs intermittent; progression), Radiation (typically none), Alleviating/Aggravating factors (relationship to food; note that relief in the fetal position is non-specific).

General GI and Red-Flag Questions

General GI: nausea/vomiting, bloating, flatus, stool character, diarrhoea/constipation, mucus in stool, fever.

Red flags (ask regardless of age - young patients can still have serious disease): weight loss, appetite change, lumps/bumps, fatigue/dizziness, fresh blood in stool, recent change in bowel habit, family history of cancer. These questions screen for more than malignancy.

Differential-Specific Questions

IBD: diarrhoea and blood in stool (screened above) plus extra-intestinal manifestations - joint pain/arthropathy, uveitis/episcleritis, erythema nodosum and pyoderma gangrenosum, and hepatobiliary features (fatty liver, primary sclerosing cholangitis); mouth ulcers.

Coeliac disease: greasy/steatorrhoeic stools (layman screen - does it stick to the pan, float, or is it hard to flush?); family history of IBD/coeliac.

Lactose intolerance: is pain triggered by dairy (milk, cheese)?

Gastroenteritis: recent travel (occupation in older patients).

IBS: mini-HEADSS/psychosocial screen - home situation, who they live with, relationships, stress at home and at work/study.

KUB: dysuria, urinary frequency, haematuria.

PUD/GORD: medications (e.g. NSAIDs), spicy food, tea/coffee (avoid asking a 16-year-old about alcohol), heartburn, bitter taste.

Liver/GB: jaundice; previous pain after large fatty meals.

Other: DKA screen (polydipsia, polyuria), tiredness.

Worked Scenario 1 - IBD (young female)

History findings: pain for a few months; stopped dairy without improvement; no stress; a little nausea, no vomiting; stools soft (not frank diarrhoea); no red flags; no blood in stool.

Physical examination (on card): abdomen normal; rash on both legs with tender nodules ~2 cm each = erythema nodosum (red, raised, hard-cored, tender nodules confined to the legs).

Teaching point: erythema nodosum is a common extra-intestinal presentation of IBD. Consider sarcoidosis as an important second differential (a young patient with cough plus erythema nodosum) - a CXR is worthwhile. The history is deliberately vague; the diagnosis is clinched by the extra-intestinal sign.

Diagnosis explanation: IBD - an immune-mediated inflammatory condition of the bowel. Reasons given: longstanding pain, soft stools (diarrhoea expected but not mandatory), and the leg rash (erythema nodosum) linked to IBD.

Chronic DDx offered: GI - IBD, coeliac, lactose intolerance, gastroenteritis, IBS, (rarely) bowel cancer; KUB - UTI, renal/bladder stones; upper - PUD, biliary colic; other - DKA (unlikely in chronic pain).

Worked Scenario 2 - Coeliac Disease (young male, 6 months)

Stem: young man with abdominal pain for 6 months; saw his GP 2 weeks ago and examination was normal (so PE is normal by design).

History findings: pain all over the stomach; bloating and pain worse after eating pasta and bread; loose stools that are bulky, smelly and hard to flush; no stress; no blood in stool; IBD questions negative; incidentally anaemic and taking iron supplements (signifying malabsorption / iron-deficiency anaemia).

Teaching point: bloating + loose stools + abdominal pain mandates a GI focus; the gluten-containing trigger foods (pasta, bread) plus steatorrhoea and iron-deficiency anaemia point to malabsorption. IDA helps differentiate malabsorptive causes (coeliac, IBD).

Diagnosis explanation: coeliac disease. Reasons: longstanding pain, gluten-containing trigger foods, characteristic bulky/smelly/hard-to-flush loose stools. DDx to mention: IBD, gastroenteritis, lactose intolerance, IBS, KUB causes (UTI/stones), PUD, biliary colic, DKA.

Summary Approach

Most abdominal-pain stations (~95%) are acute; a minority (~5%) are chronic. When you encounter a chronic abdominal pain case in a young patient, do not forget to expand the GI differential (IBD, coeliac, lactose intolerance, IBS, chronic GE) while still completing all red-flag questions. Keep these chronic differentials at the end of your standard acute-abdomen structure.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ Coeliac disease is explained to the patient as a 'gluten sensitivity / gluten allergy'.
Coeliac disease is an autoimmune (immune-mediated) enteropathy triggered by dietary gluten, not an allergy or simple intolerance. A patient-friendly explanation is: 'Your immune system reacts abnormally to gluten (a protein in wheat, rye and barley), damaging the lining of the small bowel and impairing nutrient absorption.' Avoid the word 'allergy'.
✏️ Diagnosis is asserted clinically without stating the confirmatory pathway; the note does not mention that patients must remain on a gluten-containing diet for accurate testing.
Australian diagnosis of coeliac disease requires anti-tissue transglutaminase IgA (tTG-IgA) with total IgA (to exclude IgA deficiency) while the patient is still eating gluten, followed by duodenal biopsy at gastroscopy for confirmation. HLA-DQ2/DQ8 is used to exclude the diagnosis. Do not start a gluten-free diet before testing, as this normalises serology and histology.
✏️ IBD is described as 'auto-immune' and lactose intolerance is grouped under 'food allergies'.
IBD (Crohn disease, ulcerative colitis) is best described as immune-mediated/inflammatory rather than classically autoimmune. Lactose intolerance is an enzyme (lactase) deficiency causing carbohydrate malabsorption - it is neither an allergy nor an autoimmune condition. Use accurate patient wording for each.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add the Australian confirmatory investigation set: coeliac serology (tTG-IgA + total IgA) on a gluten-containing diet, iron studies/FBE (IDA), faecal calprotectin as a non-invasive IBD screen, CRP/ESR, and referral for gastroscopy + duodenal biopsy (coeliac) or colonoscopy (IBD).
⭐ Add a mandatory pregnancy test (urine/serum beta-hCG) and gynaecological differentials (ectopic pregnancy, ovarian pathology, PID) for any young female with abdominal pain - a notable omission in the current structure and a key safety-net item.
⭐ Reference RACGP/eTG guidance: eTG 'Coeliac disease' and 'Inflammatory bowel disease' for diagnosis and management, and the Coeliac Australia patient resources for gluten-free dietary counselling and dietitian referral.
⭐ For the erythema nodosum finding, add the broader EN differential to consider on examination (IBD, sarcoidosis, streptococcal infection, tuberculosis, drugs, pregnancy) and justify the CXR to exclude sarcoidosis/TB.
⭐ Add safety-netting and follow-up: red-flag review (weight loss, PR bleeding, nocturnal symptoms, anaemia) warranting urgent specialist referral, and structured psychosocial (HEADSS) assessment when IBS is considered in an adolescent.
