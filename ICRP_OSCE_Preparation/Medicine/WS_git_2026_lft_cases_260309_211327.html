Deranged Liver Function Tests (Alcoholic Hepatitis / NAFLD)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / class 5 liver cases (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Stem & Tasks

Your next patient is a 40-year-old man who has presented to discuss his LFT results.

Tasks:

  • Take a focused history
  • Ask for the physical examination findings (PEFE)
  • Explain your diagnosis and differentials to the patient

Teaching note: The AST, ALT and GGT are only mildly elevated (typically 4-5 times the upper limit of normal, i.e. around 100 U/L, not 500-600). The patient has no complaints but attends to discuss deranged LFTs. Because the stem centres on the liver, approach it as a liver case: build a symptom picture, screen for causes, then explain diagnosis and differentials.

Approach / Framework

Structure the liver history around: (1) symptoms of liver disease, (2) differentials of deranged LFTs. Screen systematically for:

  • Red flags / cancer: pancreatic, cholangiocarcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)
  • Hepatitis: risk factors A-B-P-F-I-T-N-SEX (alcohol/antibiotics, blood transfusion, piercing/tattoo, food, IV drug use, travel, occupation, sexual history)
  • Gallbladder: stones causing deranged LFTs
  • Surgery
  • Fever (always asked last in liver cases so it can quickly change the working diagnosis to cholangitis)

History of Presenting Complaint

Open question: "How can I help you today?" → "I'm here for my results. I had some blood tests and was told to come and discuss them."

Respond: "Yes, I have your results. Before we go through them, I'd like to ask a few questions to better understand the situation, then we'll discuss the results and the condition towards the end. Is that okay?"

Liver symptoms:

  • Any yellowish discolouration of the skin? (jaundice)
  • Any itchiness?
  • Any dark urine?
  • Any pale stools?

Red Flags (Cancer Screen)

  • Any unintentional weight loss?
  • Any loss of appetite?
  • Any lumps or bumps?
  • Any recent tiredness/fatigue?
  • Any family history of cancer (pancreatic, liver, gallbladder)?

Hepatitis Risk Factors

  • A - Alcohol & Antibiotics: "Do you drink alcohol?" If yes, probe amount and duration. Patient: "1 bottle of wine each night for 20 years" (a significant amount over a long time). Any recent antibiotic use?
  • B - Blood: Any recent blood transfusions?
  • P - Piercing/Tattoo: Any piercings or tattoos?
  • F - Food: Any street food recently?
  • I - IV drug use
  • T - Travel
  • N - Occupation: Restaurant owner (needle-stick not relevant here)
  • SEX - Sexual history: Screen for STIs (safe sex, condom use) - brief, not detailed in this case

Gallbladder, Surgery, Fever

  • Gallbladder: Any previous abdominal pain after large fatty meals? (gallstones)
  • Surgery: Any history of surgery? (AMC has given a history of cholecystectomy in liver cases, which removes GB from the differential)
  • Fever, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting (screen for cholangitis)

History findings: Drinks 1 bottle of wine every day for 20 years; restaurant owner; lifestyle otherwise okay; no other risk factor.

Physical Examination (PEFE)

Choose a focused, core examination based on history and stem.

  • General appearance: jaundice? cachexia? (avoid over-examining - not the main area)
  • Vital signs: BP, temperature, RR, PR (note temperature)
  • Abdominal examination (core):
    • Inspection: symmetrical movement with respiration; any distension?
    • Palpation: tenderness; rigidity, guarding, rebound tenderness (all negative)
    • Hepatomegaly? Splenomegaly? (ask specifically, not just "masses")
    • Percussion: shifting dullness (thinking of liver disease/ascites)
  • Skip auscultation and DRE in this case
  • Office test: urine dipstick - bilirubin positive (+)

Diagnosis & Explanation - Alcoholic (Steato-)Hepatitis

Diagnosis: Alcohol-induced hepatitis / alcoholic steatohepatitis.

Alcoholic liver disease has a typical LFT pattern: AST:ALT ratio >2 (e.g. AST 95, ALT 40 → ratio 2.4). Enzymes are usually only mildly elevated (2-5x upper limit of normal), in contrast to the marked rises seen in acute viral hepatitis.

Explain: "Drinking alcohol over a long time can cause inflammation in your liver - this is what we call hepatitis. In the long run it can progress to cirrhosis, where the liver stops working properly."

Reasons for the diagnosis:

  1. Long-standing heavy alcohol intake
  2. Other liver diseases ruled out - cancers, hepatotoxic medications, viral hepatitis risk factors
  3. Reference the LFTs - only mild (2-3x) elevation and AST:ALT ratio >2 supports alcohol-related liver disease

Second Sub-case - Fatty Liver / NAFLD (RUQ pain, high TG/cholesterol)

A 35-year-old man presents to general practice with mildly raised AST/ALT (around 60-70 U/L, less than double normal), no hepatitis risk factors and no alcohol. In the 2025 version he has RUQ pain, raised triglycerides and cholesterol.

Pain assessment (SOCRATES): Site (RUQ), Intensity (0-10 scale), Quality (dull vs stabbing), Onset (first episode? constant/intermittent? worsening?), Radiation (to the back), Aggravating/relieving (relation to food, position).

General GI screen: nausea/vomiting, bloating, flatus, diarrhoea, constipation, fever. Then liver & GB screen (cancer red flags, hepatitis RF, gallstones, surgery, fever), and lifestyle (diet, exercise) given the dyslipidaemia.

Diagnosis: Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) / non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH). "When there is a high amount of fat in your blood, some is stored in the liver, causing inflammation." Reasons: other liver diseases ruled out; only mild (<4-5x) enzyme elevation, which occurs in either alcoholic or non-alcoholic fatty liver, and this patient does not drink.

Note: Fatty liver is typically painless; the presence of pain argues against it, but the 2025 AMC version retained the abdominal pain complaint.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The notes estimate 1 bottle of wine as '12-14 standard drinks' and '70 SD weekly'.
In Australia one standard drink = 10 g alcohol. A standard 750 mL bottle of wine at ~13% contains ~7-8 standard drinks, so daily consumption is ~50-55 standard drinks/week, not 70. The clinical point (well above the NHMRC guideline of no more than 10 standard drinks/week and no more than 4 on any day) still stands, but the arithmetic should be corrected.
✏️ Terminology 'NAFLD / NASH' and 'alcoholic steatohepatitis' reflects older nomenclature.
Since the 2023 multi-society consensus, NAFLD is now termed MASLD (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease) and NASH is MASH; alcohol-related fatty liver falls under MetALD/ALD. Candidates can still use NAFLD/NASH in the exam but should be aware of current terminology and that diagnosis requires exclusion of significant alcohol and other causes.
✏️ The dipstick finding of urinary bilirubin is presented as supporting alcoholic/fatty hepatitis.
Conjugated (direct) hyperbilirubinuria indicates a cholestatic/obstructive picture and should prompt reconsideration of biliary obstruction or significant hepatocellular injury; it is not a typical feature of uncomplicated fatty liver and warrants fractionated bilirubin and imaging.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add an explicit alcohol-related management pathway per RACGP/eTG: brief intervention, AUDIT-C screening, thiamine supplementation, assessment for alcohol withdrawal, and referral to drug and alcohol services - the case stops at diagnosis without management.
⭐ Add the standard 'liver screen' bloods that AMC expects to be verbalised: hepatitis B surface antigen and hepatitis C antibody, iron studies (haemochromatosis), autoimmune markers (ANA, anti-smooth muscle, anti-LKM), caeruloplasmin (Wilson in younger patients), alpha-1-antitrypsin, and a liver ultrasound +/- FibroScan.
⭐ Surface a missing red flag: screen for features of decompensation (ascites, encephalopathy/confusion, variceal bleeding, easy bruising) and calculate a Maddrey discriminant function if severe alcoholic hepatitis is suspected.
⭐ Add metadata linking to related irStudy topics (jaundice work-up, gallstone disease) and note the three-sub-case structure so the renderer can present alcoholic hepatitis vs NAFLD as distinct scenarios.
