Physical Examination Findings from Examiner (PEFE) — Structure for Abdominal Pain

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / 1. Structure class (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Transition After the Bell

After hearing the bell, signpost the transition to the patient before turning to the examiner: "Thank you Jill, I will be talking to my examiner. I will be back to you."

1. General Appearance — PRICCLED

  • P — Pallor
  • R — Rash
  • I — Icterus (jaundice)
  • C — Cyanosis
  • C — Clubbing
  • L — Lymphadenopathy (LAD)
  • E — Oedema
  • D — Dehydration

If the patient presents with abdominal pain, prioritise asking:

  1. Level of consciousness — alert or drowsy?
  2. Dehydration
  3. Pallor
  4. If RUQ pain — icterus (jaundice)

If the examiner says "as you can see in the patient", then move on.

2. Vital Signs

  • BP, PR, RR, temperature
  • Pulse: rate, rhythm, postural drop (postural/orthostatic hypotension)

3. Abdominal Examination

Inspection:

  • Is the abdomen moving symmetrically with respiration?
  • Do I see Cullen sign or Grey Turner sign? (retroperitoneal haemorrhage, e.g. severe pancreatitis)
  • Any distension?

Palpation:

  • Tenderness or mass on superficial and deep palpation?
  • Guarding
  • Rigidity
  • Rebound tenderness

Percussion:

  • Any tenderness on percussion?

Auscultation:

  • Bowel sounds absent or present?

Inguinal area:

  • Any lumps or swellings? (hernial orifices)

DRE (digital rectal examination): request with consent and chaperone — masses, tenderness, melaena, fresh blood, stool on glove.

4. Lower Abdominal / Pelvic Examination

Ask the examiner: "Examiner, I want to examine my patient with consent and in the presence of a chaperone."

Inspection:

  • Skin: redness or rashes
  • Lumps or swelling
  • Bleeding or discharge

Speculum examination:

  • Cervix: bleeding or discharge
  • Vaginal wall

Bimanual examination:

  • Cervical motion tenderness (PID, ectopic pregnancy)
  • Uterus: size, tenderness, border
  • Adnexa: mass, tenderness

5. Testicular Examination

With consent and a chaperone:

  • Inspection: swelling, redness
  • Palpation: mass or tenderness

6. Chest Examination (listed in source as 'Upper Abdominal Examination')

Screens for referred/extra-abdominal causes (ACS, lower lobe pneumonia):

  • CVS: S1 and S2 normal? Any murmurs?
  • Respiratory: air entry equal? Any added sounds?

7. Office Tests

  • Glucometer (BSL)
  • Urine dipstick test (UDT)
  • Urine pregnancy test (UPT)
  • ECG
  • Spirometry
  • Proctoscopy

Rules of thumb:

  • UDT in all cases
  • UPT in women where relevant (see expert correction — all women of reproductive age with abdominal pain)
  • BSL if DKA is suspected

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: history-taking-expert (Australian clinical supervisor, teaching hospital, NSW) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The office-test rule states urine pregnancy test 'in lady if relevant', leaving the decision to perceived relevance.
In Australian emergency and general practice, a urine pregnancy test is mandatory in ALL women of reproductive age presenting with abdominal pain, regardless of stated LMP or contraception, to exclude ectopic pregnancy — a not-to-miss diagnosis. Framing it as optional risks the classic AMC and medicolegal pitfall of the missed ectopic.
✏️ The source lists the cardiorespiratory screen under the heading 'Upper abd examination' and abbreviates cardiovascular examination as 'CUS'.
This is a chest (cardiovascular and respiratory) examination screening for extra-abdominal causes of abdominal pain (ACS, lower lobe pneumonia), not part of the abdominal examination proper. The abbreviation should read CVS. The heading has been clarified in the structured sections.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ The DRE line is left blank in the source. Complete it with specific PEFE requests: perianal inspection, masses, tenderness, melaena or fresh blood, stool colour on the glove — and state consent and chaperone, as for the pelvic and testicular examinations.
⭐ Add hepatobiliary-specific findings to the abdominal examination: Murphy's sign (acute cholecystitis), hepatomegaly/liver edge, splenomegaly, shifting dullness for ascites, and renal angle (costovertebral) tenderness for pyelonephritis/renal colic — all commonly requested in AMC GIT PEFE stations.
⭐ Add examination signs of peritonism and AAA to inspection/palpation: patient lying still versus writhing (colic), board-like rigidity, and palpation for a pulsatile expansile mass in patients over 50 — ruptured AAA is a not-to-miss cause of abdominal pain.
⭐ Note for the vitals step that a postural drop plus tachycardia in abdominal pain suggests hypovolaemia (bleeding ectopic, ruptured AAA, GI bleed) and should trigger immediate escalation and calling for senior help (000/rapid response in a community setting) before proceeding with the remaining PEFE.
⭐ Remove or qualify spirometry in the office tests: it has no role in the acute abdominal pain PEFE; a venous blood gas and ketones (for suspected DKA with SGLT2 inhibitor use, where BGL may be near normal) are the higher-yield bedside additions in Australian practice.
