Functional Neurological Disorder (Diagnosis Explanation and Management Counselling)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med workshop 2 - 2026 / CLASS 2 NEUROLOGY (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: neurology

Overview and Task

This case began as a pilot and quickly became a scored station. You are an intern/HMO on the ward. The patient is a 37-year-old who had a car accident 2 months ago; all imaging was normal. He presents to ED today unable to walk, and the neurologist has reviewed him and diagnosed functional neurological disorder (FND).

Tasks: ask the examiner for the physical (neurological) examination (2 minutes), explain the diagnosis to the patient (2 minutes), and counsel him on the management plan (3 minutes). The predominant assessment area is counselling and management, and communication skills are central. The notes advise not framing the discussion around a mind-body/psychological axis.

What is FND

FND comprises neurological symptoms (such as blackouts, paralysis and weakness) caused by a complex combination of biological, psychological and social factors affecting how the brain functions. There is inconsistency between the examination and the symptoms – the symptoms do not match a recognised disease or the examination findings.

DSM-5 criteria for FND:

  • One or more symptoms of altered voluntary motor or sensory function
  • Clinical findings provide evidence of incompatibility between the symptom and recognised neurological or medical conditions
  • The symptom is not better explained by another medical or mental disorder
  • The symptom causes clinically significant distress or impairment in social/occupational functioning

The most recent DSM-5 model removed both the requirement for a recent psychological stressor and the need to exclude feigning when diagnosing FND. FND is similar to conversion disorder and works mainly on neurological symptoms.

Physical Examination

Introduce yourself, build initial rapport, then ask the examiner for findings before returning to the patient.

  • General appearance: level of consciousness (alert/drowsy), any abnormal limb position or posture
  • Gait: ask the patient to walk on heels and toes (recommended in FND; the patient has dorsiflexion weakness)
  • Upper and lower limb neurology: inspection (wasting, fasciculations, tremor), tone at each joint, power, reflexes (biceps/triceps, knee/ankle), sensation by dermatomes, coordination (finger-to-nose, heel-to-shin)
  • Cranial nerves: fundoscopy, visual acuity and fields, pupils, eye movements, trigeminal sensation/motor, facial movements
  • Special tests: Hoover sign, clonus, plantar reflexes, pronator drift

Hoover sign: first check power of hip extension (the patient reports weakness), then reassess hip extension while asking the patient to flex the contralateral hip; in FND, hip extension becomes strong during contralateral hip flexion. This positive Hoover sign is a key rule-in feature of functional lower-limb weakness.

Typical findings in this case: ankle held in dorsiflexion with stiffness/hypertonia; clonus, plantar reflexes and pronator drift are normal. Because the diagnosis is already set by the stem, additional findings would not change it.

Explaining the Diagnosis

Thank the patient for waiting, ask for any concerns first, then explain the purpose of the examination (to check the nerves and brain are functioning normally). Deliver the diagnosis clearly:

  • The neurologist and I believe you have a condition called functional neurological disorder
  • Explain what it is: a functional movement problem in the ankle; the nerves and brain are not damaged and there is no brain injury, stroke or bleed after the accident, but the nerves are not working properly – which is why we call it functional
  • Emphasise you believe the symptoms are real: the patient is not imagining or putting on the symptoms, and you see many patients with similar problems
  • Reassure: because there is no major nerve damage, there is a high chance of improvement, and the team will support the patient

Communicate the diagnosis positively (explain what they do have, e.g. functional weakness), emphasise the mechanism over the cause, use metaphors (software rather than hardware problem, a short circuit), stress reversibility and common occurrence, and invite questions before moving to management. Be alert to a hidden agenda – the patient may ask do you believe my symptoms.

Management

Emphasise a multidisciplinary team (MDT) approach using allied health, since this is a functional problem without major nervous-system damage:

  • Neurologist and GP: regular review to monitor progress
  • Physiotherapist: retraining muscles to move and function normally, balance and flexibility exercises, activity pacing and long-term self-management
  • Occupational therapist: review symptoms and home, provide mobility equipment (walking stick/walker) as a short-term aid, support return to daily activities
  • Psychologist: cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) – cognitive work to understand symptoms and unhelpful thoughts, behavioural techniques such as relaxation. CBT alone has proven beneficial in FND, and can also treat comorbid psychological conditions
  • Social worker: if further support or disability support is needed

Investigations are generally not repeated once FND is confidently diagnosed on positive examination findings; in this case prior imaging after the MVA was already normal. Provide reading material and support via FND Australia and the Better Health Channel, review regularly, and reassure the patient throughout.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The notes at times imply FND is diagnosed only after normal investigations/exclusion of disease.
FND is a rule-in (positive) diagnosis based on positive clinical signs (for example a positive Hoover sign, tremor entrainment, distractibility) and demonstrable internal inconsistency between symptoms and examination, supported by appropriate investigations that exclude mimics. It should not be presented to the patient purely as a diagnosis of exclusion.
✏️ The notes equate FND with conversion disorder and OCR fragments use older/stigmatising framing.
In current DSM-5/ICD-11 and Australian practice the preferred terms are functional neurological disorder and functional weakness. When communicating with the patient, avoid the older labels conversion disorder or hysteria, which can be stigmatising and undermine rapport.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ List the positive rule-in signs used to demonstrate FND to the patient (Hoover sign, tremor entrainment test, give-way weakness, dragging monoplegic gait, distractibility), which both confirm the diagnosis and build the patient explanation.
⭐ Screen for and manage comorbid mental-health conditions relevant after a motor-vehicle accident (depression, anxiety, PTSD, acute stress disorder); treat if present, since these commonly co-occur and affect prognosis, while still leading with a non-blaming neurological explanation.
⭐ Add clear safety-netting and follow-up: GP shared care, referral to a specialist FND/neuropsychiatry service where available, and a plan for what to do if symptoms worsen or new red-flag features appear.
⭐ Provide specific Australian patient resources (FND Australia, Better Health Channel, neurosymptoms.org) and confirm consent/use an interpreter if needed; the patient is an adult, so communicate directly with him.
⭐ Add guidance that mobility aids and splints should be short-term adjuncts within a movement-retraining program rather than long-term supports, to avoid reinforcing abnormal movement patterns, and note that antipsychotics/benzodiazepines are not indicated for FND itself.
