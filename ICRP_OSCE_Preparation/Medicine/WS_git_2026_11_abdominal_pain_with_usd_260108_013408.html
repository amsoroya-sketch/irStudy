Peptic Ulcer Disease — Abdominal Pain with Incidental Gallstones on Ultrasound

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / 2.Abdominal pain case (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Station Stem and Tasks

Your next patient is a 40-year-old man who presents to your general practice complaining of ongoing abdominal pain. He has done a recent ultrasound (USD) and it shows gallstones.

  • Task 1: Take history from the patient for 6 minutes.
  • Task 2: Explain your diagnosis and differentials to the patient.

This case has appeared several times throughout the year. Key stem detail: on the ultrasound there is no inflammation, no thickened gallbladder wall, and no features of cholecystitis.

Case Design: The Ultrasound Distractor

Dr Amir labels this as one of two "ultrasound in abdominal pain" cases. The companion 2025 case is a woman with right lower quadrant pain and an ultrasound, which trends toward Mittelschmerz. In both designs, the ultrasound report deliberately includes incidental gallstones — with no gallbladder inflammation and no wall thickening — plus a patient concern (in the companion case, the patient's mother had cholecystitis).

The case designer is effectively baiting the candidate: "abdominal pain + gallstones on the stem — please come and wrongly diagnose cholecystitis." Some candidates still conclude biliary colic. The primary assessment area (PAA) here is history taking — the incidental gallstones must not short-circuit a proper history.

History Structure

The structure is the same for all abdominal pain cases, with minor context adjustments:

  1. Haemodynamic stability: "Before starting the case, I want to make sure my patient is vitally and haemodynamically stable — can you tell me the vitals?" The examiner gives BP, pulse, respiratory rate and temperature — keep an ear open for fever.
  2. Introduction: "My name is Amir, I'm your doctor for today. How can I help you?"
  3. Open-ended question: "Tell me more about the pain." Patient: "I've been having this pain. I recently did the scan and they told me I have gallstones. The pain is ongoing and not getting better."
  4. Address concerns, offer painkillers ("I'll arrange some"), and move on.

Pain Assessment (SIQORAA)

  • S — Site: "Where is the pain?" — "Upper abdomen." Probe: "Where specifically?" — "In the upper middle of my stomach" (epigastric).
  • I — Intensity: rate on a 1–10 scale.
  • Q — Quality: dull ache or sharp?
  • O — Onset/pattern: "When did it start? How long have you had this pain? On and off, or continuous?" — "Continuous; it doesn't go away." "Gradually getting worse?" — "Staying the same, not getting better."
  • R — Radiation: ask whether the pain radiates anywhere.
  • A, A — Alleviating and aggravating factors: "Anything that makes it better or worse?" — "When I eat, it gets worse." Probe further: "Any specific food?" (spicy food worsens it), "How soon after eating do you get the pain?", "Any position that changes it?"

Key History Findings

  • Pain in the upper (epigastric) abdomen
  • Takes ibuprofen regularly for back pain (NSAID risk factor)
  • Antacids make the pain better
  • Worse with chilli/spicy food
  • Drinks 1–2 coffees per day
  • No red flags (no weight loss, no bleeding, no dysphagia, no vomiting)

Differential Framework and Diagnosis

DDx groups for epigastric pain:

  1. PUD / GORD — the key questions that must not be forgotten are the red flags.
  2. Pancreas
  3. Liver and gallbladder
  4. ACS and lower lobe pneumonia (extra-abdominal mimics of upper abdominal pain)

Some candidates anchor on biliary colic, ask only biliary colic and liver questions, and jump straight to the differentials — this misses the actual diagnosis.

Diagnosis explained to the patient: "This is a condition called peptic ulcer disease — most likely you have an ulcer in your stomach."

Reasons:

  • Pain in the upper epigastric area, worse with eating and spicy food
  • Risk factor: regular ibuprofen (NSAID) use
  • Relief with antacids

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The diagnosis section lists 'taking Ibuprofen & anti-acids' together as 'risk factors for ulcers'. Antacids are not a risk factor for ulcers; relief with antacids is a supporting symptom of acid-related disease.
Separate the reasoning: ibuprofen (NSAID) is the risk factor; relief with antacids and aggravation by food/spice are supporting symptomatic features of peptic ulcer disease/dyspepsia. Phrase it to the patient as: 'Your regular anti-inflammatory (ibuprofen) can damage the stomach lining, and the fact that antacids relieve your pain supports an acid-related ulcer.'

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add the mandatory dyspepsia red-flag (alarm feature) checklist to the notes, as expected in Australian practice (RACGP/eTG): unintentional weight loss, dysphagia, odynophagia, haematemesis or melaena, iron-deficiency anaemia, persistent vomiting, age >50 with new-onset symptoms, family history of upper GI cancer — any of these mandates gastroscopy referral rather than empirical therapy.
⭐ Add Australian management for closure/extension: cease or minimise the NSAID (substitute paracetamol for back pain), test for Helicobacter pylori (urea breath test or stool antigen after appropriate PPI washout) and eradicate if positive, and start a PPI (e.g. esomeprazole, PBS-listed) per current eTG; arrange gastroscopy if red flags or no response.
⭐ Add a teaching point on incidental gallstones: asymptomatic gallstones are common and generally require no treatment in Australia; biliary colic pain is typically severe, episodic RUQ/epigastric pain lasting 30 minutes to several hours after fatty meals — clearly different from constant epigastric pain relieved by antacids. This arms candidates to explain to the patient WHY the gallstones are not the cause.
⭐ Add lifestyle advice to the counselling script: reduce alcohol and smoking (both impair ulcer healing), moderate caffeine, avoid trigger foods — aligns with RACGP patient-education resources.
