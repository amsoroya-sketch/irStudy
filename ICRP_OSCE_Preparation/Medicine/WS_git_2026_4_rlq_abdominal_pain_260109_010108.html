RLQ Abdominal Pain (Ectopic Pregnancy; Mittelschmerz Variant)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / 2.Abdominal pain case (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Case Stem (2025)

Your next patient is a 27-year-old lady who presents to your general practice complaining of right lower abdominal pain for 4 days. The pain has been getting worse in the last 24 hours. (Some recalls add to the stem/findings: the patient is feverish and has vomited.)

  • Task: Take history for 4 minutes
  • Explain diagnosis and differentials

Opening and Pain Assessment (SIQORAA)

  • Introduction: "My name is Amir, I am your doctor for today."
  • Open-ended question: "How can I help you?" — the patient gives a statement; respond to it: "I'm so sorry to hear that, Lily. I'll need to get a better understanding about your pain to figure out what's going on. Is it okay to go through a few questions?" → Yes.
  • "Are you in any pain now?" → Yes. "Do you want any painkiller?" → Yes/No.

Pain exploration:

  • Site: right lower side of the stomach. Shift (key point): "Where did the pain start — the same point or somewhere else?" → "It started and stayed at the same place." (No shift, so appendicitis is less likely.)
  • Intensity: 5/10.
  • Quality: dull ache.
  • Onset/time: already given; on/off or constant? gradually getting worse?
  • Radiation: No.
  • Alleviating/aggravating factors: nothing better/worse; no relationship to food; no positional change.

GI Screening and Red Flags

  • Nausea, vomiting? → "Yes, I have nausea and I have vomited 3 times." (probe further — see key questions below)
  • Bloating? → No
  • Passing wind? → Yes
  • Diarrhoea? → Yes — "When did it start?"
  • Constipation? → No
  • Fever? → screened (feverish per stem — probe further)

Red flags (cancer questions): weight loss → No; appetite loss → No; lumps and bumps → No; tired/dizzy screen (acute → dizzy; chronic → tired).

Specific questions: fresh blood in stool → No; dark stool → No; recent change in bowel motion → No; family history of cancers → No; previous abdominal surgery → No; regular medications → No.

KUB and Pelvic Groups (Key Positive Findings)

KUB group: dysuria → No; frequency → No; blood in urine → "Not really."

Pelvic group:

  • Hernia: groin swelling/lumps? → No
  • Female with lower abdominal pain — symptoms: vaginal bleeding → No; vaginal discharge → No
  • Period history: "When was your last menstrual period?" → 5 weeks ago. "Are your periods regular?" → irregular/regular. "How often?" → every 4–6 weeks. (Pregnancy cannot be excluded. If LMP is more than 4 weeks ago, always clarify cycle regularity — a woman who cycles every 3 months is less concerning at 6 weeks than one with regular 4-weekly cycles.)
  • Sexual history (screening): sexually active → Yes; safe sex/condoms → Yes; ever diagnosed with an STI → Yes — chlamydia at age 18

Probing the Positive Findings

Key history findings: diarrhoea; missed period (LMP 5 weeks ago); irregular periods every 4–6 weeks; history of chlamydia at 18.

Probe each positive finding:

  • Feverish: "Have you checked your temperature?" → No. "Any chills?" → Yes/No.
  • Vomiting (3 times): the single most important probe is the colour — "Is it green?" (bilious vomiting raises obstruction). Blood, number of episodes, volume and odour also matter, but colour first.
  • Diarrhoea: when did it start? any blood in the stool?
  • Late period: regularity and cycle length as above.

"Multiple findings drama" case: there are positives pointing to PID, ectopic pregnancy and appendicitis simultaneously. The candidate who rules out multiple differentials will uncover multiple findings — finding several positives means you are doing a good job, not a bad one.

PEFE

  • General appearance: "As you can see." (skip if the examiner is terse)
  • Vital signs: BP, PR, RR, temperature → Temp 37.5 °C (37.4 °C is the practical threshold used in teaching; AMC often gives borderline values deliberately)
  • Abdominal examination:
    • Inspection: symmetrical respiratory movement → Yes; Cullen/Grey Turner → No; distension → No
    • Palpation: RLQ tenderness; rigidity (+); guarding (+); rebound tenderness (+); masses → No
    • Percussion: RLQ percussion tenderness → Yes
    • Auscultation: bowel sounds present → Yes
  • Inguinal area: normal. DRE: normal.
  • Appendicitis special tests (appendicitis still on the list): Rovsing's sign → negative; psoas or obturator sign (obturator easier to perform in F2F) → negative
  • Pelvic examination (consent + chaperone):
    • Inspection: rashes/redness → No; lumps/swelling → No; discharge/bleeding → No
    • Speculum: cervix and vaginal wall structurally normal; no bleeding or discharge at the cervix
    • Bimanual: cervical motion tenderness positive; uterus — anteverted (recalled as "inverted" on the card), normal size, empty; right adnexal tenderness, no mass
  • Office tests: urine dipstick → normal; urine pregnancy test → POSITIVE

PE findings card: Temp 37.5; RLQ tenderness and rebound tenderness; guarding and rigidity; CMT (+); normal-size uterus; right adnexal tenderness; urine pregnancy test (+).

Diagnosis: Ectopic Pregnancy

Diagnosis: "Most likely you have a condition called ectopic pregnancy."

Explanation: "In this condition the pregnancy forms in the tube instead of the womb — an unusual position for the pregnancy sac."

Reasons:

  • Missed period (LMP 5 weeks ago)
  • Positive urine pregnancy test today
  • The vomiting is most likely pregnancy-related (early-pregnancy nausea and vomiting)
  • On examination: the womb is empty and of normal size, and the opening of the womb is painful when touched (cervical motion tenderness), with right adnexal tenderness

(Regarding the recalled reasoning that "even feeling feverish is a feature of ectopic pregnancy" — see expert corrections: fever is not a typical feature of ectopic pregnancy and should push you to reconsider PID/appendicitis, not be used to support ectopic.)

Differentials

  1. Appendicitis: "An inflammation in a pouch of bowel — not likely because the special tests are negative on examination and your pain has not shifted."
  2. Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID): "An infection of the womb — you have had a previous STI, fever and vomiting, but the positive pregnancy test and the absence of vaginal discharge make PID less likely."
  3. GI group: hernia, bowel obstruction, gastroenteritis, IBD, volvulus
  4. KUB group: UTI, ureteric colic
  5. O&G group: ovarian torsion, ovarian cyst, ovarian cancer
  6. Others: DKA

Variant Case: 23-Year-Old — Mittelschmerz (Ovulation Pain)

Your next patient is a 23-year-old lady presenting to general practice with abdominal pain.

  • Task: Take history for 6 minutes; explain your diagnosis and differentials.

Findings: pain in the RLQ, no shift; LMP 2 weeks ago (i.e. mid-cycle); pain since this morning; mild intensity (4–5/10); dull; no radiation; previous similar self-resolving episodes (2).

Diagnosis: Mittelschmerz — also known as ovulation pain or mid-cycle pain.

Explanation: "In the middle of your menstrual cycle, usually around day 14 (assuming a regular 28-day cycle), the egg is released from the ovary. This releases some fluid into the tummy, which causes pain."

Reasons:

  • Site — one-sided lower abdominal (RLQ) pain
  • Mild intensity (4–5/10)
  • Timing — immediately around mid-cycle (period 2 weeks ago)
  • Previous self-resolving episodes
  • Differentials excluded — mid-cycle ovulation pain is a diagnosis of exclusion

There is no PE in this variant, so this is a provisional diagnosis — the most likely cause.

DDx for RLQ pain in this variant: GI — appendicitis, bowel obstruction; KUB — UTI, ureteric colic; hernia; pelvic; obstetric — ectopic pregnancy; gynaecological — PID, ovarian torsion; shingles.

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: history-taking-expert (Australian clinical supervisor) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The diagnosis explanation states 'Even your feeling feverish (+) is a feature of ectopic pregnancy'. Fever is not a recognised feature of ectopic pregnancy.
Fever/low-grade temperature in a woman with RLQ pain points towards PID or appendicitis, not ectopic pregnancy. In this case the fever should be acknowledged as an unexplained finding that keeps PID/appendicitis on the differential, and the ectopic diagnosis should rest on the missed period, positive β-hCG, empty normal-sized uterus, CMT and adnexal tenderness.
✏️ The case ends at 'explain diagnosis and differentials' with no escalation step, implying the GP consultation can conclude with an explanation.
A suspected ectopic pregnancy is a gynaecological emergency in Australian practice: the patient must be referred immediately to an emergency department / early pregnancy assessment service for serum quantitative β-hCG and urgent transvaginal ultrasound, kept nil by mouth if unstable, and transported by ambulance (call 000) if there are signs of rupture (severe pain, shoulder-tip pain, syncope, haemodynamic instability). Even where the AMC task omits management, candidates should signpost urgent referral in the closing statement.
✏️ The PEFE card is recalled as 'Inverted uterus, normal size'. 'Inverted uterus' is an acute obstetric emergency (uterine inversion) and cannot be a routine bimanual finding here; this is an OCR/recall artifact.
The intended finding is an anteverted (or retroverted) uterus of normal size — i.e. a normal, empty uterus. Corrected in the structured sections with the recall noted.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add ectopic pregnancy risk factors as an explicit checklist for the history (previous PID/chlamydia — present in this case, previous ectopic, tubal surgery, IUD in situ, assisted reproduction, smoking): linking the patient's chlamydia at 18 to tubal damage strengthens the diagnostic reasoning the examiner is marking.
⭐ Add a note that a positive urine β-hCG with an empty uterus on later ultrasound defines a pregnancy of unknown location, and that management options (expectant, methotrexate, surgical) are decided in hospital — useful for the follow-up questions AMC examiners commonly ask after the diagnosis explanation.
⭐ For the Mittelschmerz variant, add Murtagh's management line: explanation and reassurance, simple analgesia (paracetamol), and local heat if pain is severe — turning a diagnosis-only recall into a complete GP consultation.
⭐ Add a safety-net statement template for the Mittelschmerz variant (return if pain worsens, fever develops, syncope, or a period is missed) since ruptured ovarian cyst, torsion and early ectopic can mimic ovulation pain.
⭐ Tag the 'multiple findings drama' concept in platform metadata — this station is the best available teaching example that eliciting several positive findings across PID/appendicitis/ectopic reflects thorough history taking, not confusion.
