Acute Pancreatitis — 40-Year-Old Man with Upper Abdominal Pain (2025 Case)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / 2.Abdominal pain case (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Station Stem and Tasks

Your next patient is a 40-year-old man who presents to you complaining of abdominal pain.

  • Task 1: Take history for 6 minutes.
  • Task 2: Explain the diagnosis with reasons, and differentials, to the patient.

Plan: introduction, history, DDx, good closure.

Brainstorming in the 2-Minute Reading Time

Keep in the back of the mind that if the pain turns out to be right upper quadrant, the job is to differentiate biliary colic vs cholecystitis vs cholangitis. This might also be the famous pancreatitis case (AMC used it heavily in 2025 — reportedly about 20 times), or it might be peptic ulcer disease (PUD). At the start we do not know which one we are dealing with.

Four major steps of history taking:

  1. Introduction + haemodynamic stability — do not forget; we do not yet know whether it is a chronic or acute case.
  2. Explore the pain (SIQORAA) — for an acute upper abdominal pain of unknown site, the critical component is the alleviating and aggravating factors: ask specifically about food/eating and position, because biliary colic relates to food, pancreatitis relates to position (relief leaning forward), and PUD relates to food.
  3. Rule out the DDx: red flags first (before working through RUQ/gallbladder/liver, pancreatitis and PUD questions), then general GI questions.
  4. Good closure.

History Findings

  • Had a large meal at lunch (with alcohol/wine)
  • Severe pain in the upper abdomen (epigastric area) since then
  • Radiating to the back
  • Nothing makes it better
  • Non-verbal clue: the patient is recalled sitting in the chair bending forward — pain improves when leaning forward
  • On a GLP-1 agonist (Ozempic/semaglutide)

Because the pain came on after a large meal with alcohol, it could initially suggest cholecystitis or pancreatitis; the positional relief and the GLP-1 use push strongly toward pancreatitis.

The Weight-Loss Distractor (New AMC Design in 2025)

An interesting deliberate distractor appears in the history:

  • "Do you have any loss of weight?" — Yes.
  • "How much?" — 3 kg.
  • "Over what period?" — 1 month.
  • "Is it intentional?" — Yes.

Are we concerned about this? Not really. This was the first time the AMC used this design: it aims to pick out candidates who hear "yes" to weight loss and treat it as a significant red flag without quantifying it. When probed, 3 kg over 1 month that is intentional is not a significant red flag.

Diagnosis and Case Traps

Diagnosis: acute pancreatitis.

An additional finding that supports pancreatitis: the patient is on a GLP-1 agonist (Ozempic/semaglutide), and pancreatitis is a recognised adverse effect of GLP-1 agonists. Combined with alcohol intake the night before ("I drank alcohol last night and partied"), the picture fits acute pancreatitis.

How candidates went wrong:

  • Some were misled by the weight loss and diagnosed cancer.
  • Some were misled by the large meal and the wine, missed the positional relief and the Ozempic, and diagnosed a gallbladder problem.

This case is a good example of "multiple findings drama" — several superficially conflicting clues that must be weighed together.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add the confirmatory pathway expected in Australian practice: serum lipase (preferred over amylase; diagnostic when >3x the upper limit of normal), LFTs to screen for a biliary cause, and abdominal ultrasound to look for gallstones as the aetiology — per eTG (Gastrointestinal: acute pancreatitis) and RACGP guidance. The teaching note currently stops at the clinical diagnosis.
⭐ Add the classic pancreatitis pain descriptor to the SIQORAA teaching: constant severe epigastric pain radiating through to the back, relieved by sitting forward and worse lying supine, often with nausea and vomiting — this ties the non-verbal clue (leaning forward) explicitly to the expected verbal findings.
⭐ Add a brief aetiology screen to the history structure: alcohol quantity in standard drinks (Australian guideline: no more than 10 standard drinks/week), known gallstones, triglycerides, and a full medication review (GLP-1 agonists, thiazides, sodium valproate, azathioprine) — the case hinges on medication history yet the notes never show the candidate asking a structured medication question.
⭐ Add initial management for closure/extension: this is an emergency — refer to ED (call 000 if deteriorating in the community), nil by mouth initially, aggressive IV crystalloid, analgesia, antiemetics, and early severity assessment; the GLP-1 agonist should be withheld and its ongoing use reviewed.
⭐ Metadata to add: flag this as a 2025-pattern station where role players volunteer 'pseudo red flags' (intentional 3 kg weight loss) — candidates should be taught to quantify every red flag (how much, over how long, intentional or not) before assigning significance.
