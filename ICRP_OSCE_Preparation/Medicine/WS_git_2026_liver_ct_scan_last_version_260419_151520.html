Acute Epigastric Pain - Gallstones, Diverticulosis & Incidental Hypodense Liver Lesion

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / class 5 liver cases (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Stem & Tasks

Your next patient is a 57-year-old man presenting to ED with epigastric pain for 2 hours. Analgesia has been given and the pain has subsided. This is the first time he has had this pain. He is generally healthy with no past medical history. Blood results are pending. A CT scan has been done.

Tasks: explain the CT scan to the patient; explain your diagnosis and differentials; request further investigations.

Teaching note: This is an acute (2-hour) first-episode epigastric pain. It is unlikely to be pancreatitis (no alcohol; lipase/amylase pending). Two things need explaining: the CT findings and the differentials for both the hypodense liver lesion and the epigastric pain.

CT Scan Findings

  • Multiple calcified gallbladder stones in the GB; no acoustic shadow issue; common bile duct (CBD) not dilated.
  • Diverticulosis (non-inflamed) - so not diverticulitis; and the pain is epigastric, not lower abdominal.
  • Gallbladder wall not thickened, no pericholecystic fluid, no signs of inflammation - so not cholecystitis.
  • A hypodense liver lesion with no portal vein dilation.

Two issues to decide on: biliary colic and the hypodense liver lesion (differentials include both benign and malignant - primary cancer or metastasis). Biliary colic is less likely because this is a first episode (biliary colic tends to recur); severe pain settling with analgesia does not mean it resolves by itself.

Opening & Addressing Concern

"How are you feeling now?" → "Much better, they gave me painkillers." "Can you tell me what has happened so far?" → "This weird pain started two hours ago, quite severe, so I came to ED. They did bloods and scans and gave me painkillers. I'm worried about what's happening."

"I understand your concern. I'm here to help. Let's go through the findings together and work out what's going on."

Explaining the Gallbladder & Diverticula

"We've done a scan of your abdomen. We checked your gallbladder - the sac behind the liver that helps digest food. We found a few stones, but none are blocking the outlet or the tubes to the bowel, and there are no signs of inflammation (the wall isn't thickened and there's no surrounding fluid)."

"In the bowel we saw some pouches - diverticula/diverticulosis - which develop from long-standing constipation and pressure on the bowel wall. These are not the cause of your pain (they'd cause lower-abdominal pain) and they are not inflamed."

Explaining the Liver Lesion & Diagnosis

"Looking at your liver, we found a lump/lesion approximately 5 x 7 cm. It looks darker than the surrounding tissue, so we call it a hypodense lesion."

"To find the exact cause of the pain we need further investigation. Gallstones can cause acute, short-lived pain (biliary colic) that settles by itself; but with no inflammation and a normal wall thickness, gallstones are most likely not the cause of your pain."

"I am concerned about the lump in your liver. It could be a liver cancer or a spread of cancer to the liver, but it could also be benign - an adenoma, cyst, abscess or haemangioma."

Differentials for the epigastric pain: cholecystitis, cholangitis, hepatitis, pancreatitis, pancreatic cancer, peptic ulcer disease, lower lobe pneumonia, acute coronary syndrome (ACS).

Investigations

Liver lesion: MRI with contrast (special/triphasic protocol); possible liver biopsy; LFTs; tumour marker AFP.

Metastasis: colonoscopy and endoscopy (bowel/stomach).

Pancreas: lipase and amylase (could still be pancreatitis with an incidental lump).

Other: ESR, CRP, FBE; PSA in an older man.

Priority: MRI and liver biopsy are top of the list - the key task is investigating the hypodense lesion.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ Acute severe epigastric pain in a 57-year-old is worked up primarily as gallstones/liver lesion, with ACS listed only briefly at the end.
Epigastric pain in this age group must not miss ACS - an ECG and troponin are mandatory early investigations before attributing pain to biliary/hepatic causes, and this should be stated up-front rather than as a late differential. Per Australian emergency practice this is a can't-miss diagnosis.
✏️ A hypodense liver lesion is characterised only by density; the case does not distinguish benign from malignant on imaging features.
Characterisation depends on contrast-enhancement pattern: a haemangioma shows peripheral nodular discontinuous enhancement with centripetal fill-in, a simple cyst shows no enhancement with fluid density, and malignant lesions (HCC/metastasis) show characteristic arterial enhancement/washout. Triphasic contrast CT or MRI is the correct next step, which the case does reach in investigations.
✏️ The case notes the CBD is not dilated and stones are in the GB, but does not close the loop on choledocholithiasis given a first severe episode.
Even with a non-dilated CBD, if pain and LFTs suggest transient stone passage, MRCP is the appropriate test to exclude choledocholithiasis; this should be added to the biliary work-up alongside the pending LFTs.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Move ACS/cardiac screening to the front: state ECG + troponin as immediate investigations for epigastric pain, and screen for cardiac risk factors and associated symptoms (sweating, nausea, radiation) as red flags.
⭐ Add explicit contrast-phase imaging rationale (arterial/portal-venous/delayed) and note that ultrasound is first-line for gallstones while triphasic CT/MRI characterises the liver lesion - align terminology with Australian imaging guidance.
⭐ Add a management/safety-net section (currently ends at investigations): urgent specialist referral for the liver mass, safety-net symptoms prompting return (fever, jaundice, worsening pain), and clear communication that the diagnosis is not yet confirmed.
