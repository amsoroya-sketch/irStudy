Anal Pain

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / class 7 Lower GI (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Overview

Anal pain became a recurring AMC OSCE group from the 2022 online exam onward. Early stations were history-only; more recent stations add a physical examination component and frequently present a clinical photograph of the perianal region to guide the diagnosis.

Key examiner traps: distinguishing pain that is worse on defecation (fissure, thrombosed haemorrhoid, proctitis) from pain unrelated to defecation, and recognising the diagnostic photo (e.g. thrombosed haemorrhoid, pilonidal sinus, fistula opening, or an ulcerated bleeding lesion suggesting anal cancer).

Differential Diagnosis

  • Perianal abscess
  • Pilonidal abscess / sinus (midline, between the buttock cheeks)
  • Anal fissure
  • Thrombosed haemorrhoid
  • Proctitis (inflammatory - IBD; infectious - STI) and prostatitis
  • Perianal fistula (fistula-in-ano)
  • Anal / rectal cancer (red-flag diagnosis)
  • Skin conditions: dermatitis, herpes simplex, warts, molluscum contagiosum, skin tags
  • Rectal prolapse
  • Urethritis (if urinary or STI symptoms)

History Taking

1. Introduction - hand hygiene, name and role, confirm patient's name, consent, open question and respond to concern.

2. Explore the complaint (SIQORAA / timing):

  • Site: Is it immediately around the back passage, or on the buttocks/midline?
  • Onset & timing: How long? First episode? Constant or intermittent? Getting worse?
  • Character / quality & intensity of the pain (0-10 severity).
  • Radiation, alleviating and aggravating factors: Is the pain worse when opening your bowels? (Worse on defecation suggests fissure, thrombosed haemorrhoid, proctitis - not pilonidal abscess.)

If a lump is also present: site (if not given), ask the patient to describe it, is it painful to touch, any bleeding or discharge, can it be pushed back in? With only ~4 minutes, run a faster SIQORAA - do not re-ask site/timing already known.

3. Differential-directed questions:

  • General GI: nausea, vomiting, bloating, flatus, diarrhoea, constipation, fever.
  • Red flags (cancer): blood in stool, change in bowel habit, weight loss, loss of appetite, lumps/bumps, fatigue, family history of bowel cancer.
  • Anal fissure: blood in stool, constipation, recent straining.
  • Haemorrhoids: lump at back passage, reducible, heavy lifting.
  • Perianal abscess: fever/chills, discharge, redness, rash, swelling.
  • Pilonidal abscess: confirm midline swelling between the buttock cheeks.
  • Proctitis / prostatitis: dysuria, urinary frequency, penile discharge; detailed sexual history (sexually active, safe sex, partner details, type of sex, multiple partners).
  • IBD: joint pain, blurred vision, rashes.

4. Past medical history and family history.

Physical Examination (PEFE)

  • General appearance and vital signs.
  • Abdominal examination (focused): inspection for distension; palpation for masses/tenderness; auscultation.
  • DRE / perianal inspection: perform with consent and a chaperone. Inspect first; if a photograph is provided by the examiner, no further inspection questions are needed.

Photo clues: a midline lesion between the buttock cheeks suggests pilonidal sinus/abscess; a skin opening discharging without infective/malignant features suggests fistula-in-ano; an ugly, irregular, surface-bleeding lesion suggests anal cancer.

Diagnosis &amp; Explanation (worked examples)

Perianal abscess: "An infection close to the back passage causing a collection of pus."

Pilonidal abscess (hair cyst): "A pocket in the skin containing an ingrown hair; when it gets infected and fills with pus we call it an abscess. The midline site supports this."

Perianal fistula: "A tunnel-like passage between the bowel and the skin that drains stool through an opening. The photograph shows an opening with no features of infection or cancer."

Always list the differential diagnoses considered, led by the red-flag diagnosis (anal/bowel cancer).

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The notes label pilonidal abscess a 'hair cyst' and describe it interchangeably as a pilonidal sinus. These are related but distinct: a pilonidal sinus is the chronic midline pit/tract, whereas a pilonidal abscess is the acute infected collection.
Explain to the examiner/patient that a pilonidal sinus is a chronic skin tract in the natal cleft that traps hair and debris; a pilonidal abscess is the acute infected form. Acute abscess management is incision and drainage; definitive treatment of the sinus is elective excision. Avoid the loose term 'hair cyst'.
✏️ The differential list omits the strong association between fistula-in-ano/perianal disease and Crohn disease, and the notes do not mention that a fissure that is atypical (lateral, multiple, painless) should prompt consideration of Crohn disease, malignancy or infection.
Add Crohn disease screening when perianal fistula, abscess or atypical fissure is found. A typical primary anal fissure is posterior midline and painful on defecation; lateral, multiple or painless fissures warrant investigation for IBD, malignancy, HIV or syphilis (per Therapeutic Guidelines / RACGP).

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add a clear safety-netting and red-flag statement: any perianal lesion that is ulcerated, indurated, irregular or bleeding on the surface, or associated with weight loss/change in bowel habit, requires urgent colorectal referral to exclude anal or rectal cancer (and HPV/HIV risk assessment for anal SCC).
⭐ Add explicit Australian management pointers: acute perianal/pilonidal abscess needs surgical incision and drainage (antibiotics alone insufficient); thrombosed external haemorrhoid within ~72 hours may benefit from excision, otherwise conservative care (analgesia, stool softeners, warm baths); chronic anal fissure - topical GTN or diltiazem per eTG.
⭐ Structure the pain history explicitly with SOCRATES and quantify severity (0-10); if pain is severe, offer analgesia (paracetamol +/- topical agents) during the consultation rather than deferring.
⭐ Add metadata linking to related irStudy stations (rectal bleeding, constipation, sexual history/STI) and note the AMC trend of providing clinical photographs - candidates should practise interpreting perianal images.
