Central Abdominal Pain (Small Bowel Obstruction, Strangulated Hernia, Mesenteric Ischaemia)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / 2.Abdominal pain case (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Case Stem (Version 1)

These cases started appearing about 2 years ago (online exam; not yet face-to-face) — a newly designed case.

Your next patient is a 50-year-old man who presents to the ED complaining of pain in the centre of the abdomen for a few hours. Painkillers have already been given.

  • Task: Take history
  • Physical examination findings will be given to you on a card at 6 minutes
  • Explain diagnosis and differentials

Because analgesia has already been given, the painkiller offer can be skipped. The format resembles the MSK knee-pain card cases: history, then PE appears on a card.

Concept: What Does "Central" Abdominal Pain Mean?

Central abdominal pain is tricky: the doctor pictures the umbilicus, but patients describe pain above, below, or to either side of the umbilicus as "central". So "central" effectively covers the entire mid-abdomen — middle-up, middle-down and middle-lateral.

  • With 6 minutes of history, rule out all differential groups.
  • Do not assume hernias only cause lower-quadrant pain — case 2 in this set is a hernia causing central bowel obstruction.
  • The concept is the same acute-abdomen assessment: a specific site (e.g. RLQ) narrows the list; "central" means keeping a broad priority list within the available time.

Opening: Haemodynamic Stability and Rapport

  • Haemodynamic stability: "Before starting, I want to make sure my patient is vitally/haemodynamically stable — can you tell me the vitals?" → Examiner: BP 110/70 mmHg, pulse 88/min, RR 12/min, temperature 36.5 °C. (Ask one by one if needed.)
  • Introduction: "My name is Amir, I'm your doctor for today. How can I help you?" → Gary: "I'm in pain."
  • Open-ended: "Tell me more about the pain." → "I've this pain in the middle of my stomach, it's getting worse and so bad that I can't take it anymore." (The distress is audible even without the word "worried".)
  • Respond to concern: "Gary, I understand this is distressing and that you've been in pain. I'm here to help you. I need a better idea about your pain, so I'll ask a few questions to be able to help you better. How does that sound?" → "Yeah."

Pain Assessment (SIQORAA)

  • Site: "Where exactly is the pain?" → centre of the stomach. "Exactly on your belly button, above it or below it?" → "Just around the belly button." (Still non-localising.)
  • Intensity: 5/10.
  • Quality: dull ache.
  • Onset/time: a couple of hours ago; first such episode; constant; getting worse (yes/no).
  • Radiation: No.
  • Alleviating/aggravating: nothing makes it better or worse; not food-related; not positional.

At this point we are still blank and cannot narrow the differential — stick to the structure; with 6 minutes everything can be ruled out systematically.

GI Screening (Key Positive Findings)

  • Nausea? → Yes
  • Vomited? → No
  • Bloating? → Yes
  • Passing wind/gas? → No — "Since when?" (a couple of hours without flatus is normal; days are not)
  • Diarrhoea? → No
  • Constipation? → No — but there is a better question:
  • "When was the last time you opened your bowels?" → "A few days ago."
  • Fever? → No

Not passing wind + bowels last opened days ago → this is a bowel obstruction case. The passing candidate recognises this early and then rules out the causes of obstruction.

Red Flags, Surgical History, KUB

Red flags (bowel cancer questions): weight loss → No; appetite loss → No; lumps and bumps → No; tired/dizzy screen. Fresh blood in stool → No (occurs in cancer; may occur in obstruction with necrosis); dark stool → No; recent bowel-habit change → No; family history of bowel cancer → No; bowel cancer screening status (iFOBT program).

Surgical history: "Have you had any previous abdominal surgery?" → "Yes — appendix (or gallbladder) removed 5–10 years back."

Medications: No.

KUB group: dysuria → No; frequency → No; blood in urine → No.

Pelvic/Genital, Vascular and Other Questions

Pelvic/genital group: groin swelling/lumps (hernia) → No; scrotal swelling/redness; testicular pain.

Other key questions:

  • Chest pain? ACS pain can be referred to the upper abdomen (supra-umbilical pain — think ischaemic heart disease).
  • Racing/funny heartbeat (AF)? Mesenteric ischaemia — embolic or thrombotic mesenteric vascular disease. AAA and mesenteric ischaemia share cardiovascular risk factors.
  • History of heart disease or blood-vessel disease? Family history of heart attack or stroke? High blood pressure, high blood sugar, high blood fats?
  • Rashes on the abdomen? (shingles — usually unilateral, but ask within the structure)
  • Injuries/trauma to the abdomen?

GORD/PUD can be excluded later if time permits after the upper-abdominal screen.

Good closure: causes of bowel obstruction to actively exclude — cancer, hernia, adhesions.

Version 1 Findings, PE Card and Diagnosis: Small Bowel Obstruction

History summary: not passed stools in 2 days; bloated; not passing gas/wind; previous abdominal surgery (any type).

Physical examination card:

  • Temperature normal
  • Generalised abdominal tenderness, no rebound
  • Guarding
  • Hyperresonance on percussion
  • Bowel sounds absent
  • DRE: empty rectum
  • Urine dipstick normal

Small bowel obstruction — symptoms: cramping abdominal pain, nausea/vomiting, obstipation. Examination: dehydration, abdominal distension (most common finding), hyperresonance on percussion.

Bowel sounds teaching point: in the early phase of obstruction bowel sounds are high-pitched (tinkling) before becoming absent; hyperresonance reflects trapped gas.

Causes of SBO: adhesions, tumours, hernia, Crohn disease flare, traumatic haematoma, volvulus. (IBD screening is partly covered by the blood-in-stool question; extra-intestinal IBD features are usually beyond this station's time.)

Adhesions note: there is no firm evidence that intra-abdominal adhesions are painful apart from complications such as bowel obstruction; adhesions classically cause small bowel obstruction (occasionally large bowel), so "small bowel obstruction" or simply "bowel obstruction" are both acceptable labels.

Abdominal X-ray: multiple air-fluid levels on an erect film indicate small bowel obstruction.

Investigations and Management (SBO)

Investigations:

  • Erect (upright) abdominal X-ray — the single most useful first test (erect and supine films can both be done)
  • CT abdomen
  • Blood tests as clinically indicated

Management:

  • Treat the cause (e.g. hernia must be addressed)
  • Nil by mouth
  • IV fluids (replace losses before dehydration develops)
  • Nasogastric tube decompression
  • Surgery for complications (hernia → surgery; necrosis → surgery; ± antibiotics)

Explaining the diagnosis: "Most likely you have a condition called small bowel obstruction. Your bowels are blocked by an adhesion band — scar tissue that forms after surgery. On history you are not passing wind and have not opened your bowels, and you have had previous surgery. On examination I could not hear bowel sounds, and when I tap on your tummy it sounds like there is extra air inside."

DDx (differentials of bowel obstruction): bowel cancer; strangulated/incarcerated hernia; trauma and haematoma; volvulus (twisting of the bowel); IBD (Crohn/UC); others — gastroenteritis, appendicitis, AAA, mesenteric ischaemia, UTI, kidney/ureteric stone, epididymo-orchitis, IHD, pancreatitis.

Version 2: 55-Year-Old Lady — Strangulated Inguinal Hernia

Your next patient is a 55-year-old lady presenting to general practice with central abdominal pain.

  • Task: Take history; PE findings on a card at 6 minutes; explain diagnosis and differentials.

History findings: nausea (+); not passed wind/gas; not opened bowels; ± swelling in the groin. Remember the frequently forgotten question: "When was the last time you opened your bowels?" (asked when the patient is not passing gas — this is different from screening for constipation).

PEFE: general appearance (alert/drowsy, dehydration, pallor); vitals (as given at the start); abdominal examination — inspection (symmetry, Cullen/Grey Turner, distension), palpation (tenderness — site less critical; rigidity, guarding, rebound key questions; masses), percussion, auscultation (bowel sounds).

Critical step — inguinal examination: "I would like to examine the inguinal area…" → Examiner: "What are you looking for?" → "Any swelling or lump." → "Yes, there is a swelling and lump in the inguinal area." Key-point questions: Is it tender? → Yes. Is it reducible? → No. (Diagnosis made.) DRE: normal. Office tests to finish.

PE card: tenderness in LLQ; exaggerated bowel sounds; large hernia in the left groin.

Diagnosis: "You have a condition called a strangulated/incarcerated inguinal hernia. A hernia is a sac that bulges out of the tummy; in your case it is in the groin, and the bowel is stuck in the sac and has blocked the bowels." (See expert corrections for the incarcerated-vs-strangulated distinction.)

Reasons: not passing wind; nausea/vomiting (occurs when the bowel is blocked); on examination — an irreducible, tender groin swelling.

Teaching note: this is an abdominal-pain case in which hernia is found while ruling out differentials — it is not a "hernia case", so risk-factor questions (heavy lifting, chronic cough, constipation) are not required here; they belong in the groin-lump presentation.

Version 3 (2017/18 F2F): 60-Year-Old Man — Mesenteric Ischaemia

Your next patient is a 60-year-old man presenting to general practice with abdominal pain for a few days.

  • Task: Take history for 4 minutes; ask physical examination from the examiner; explain diagnosis and differentials.

History findings: pain all over the stomach (suggests generalised peritonitis — ask upper, lower and both sides); "racing/funny, fast" heartbeat (AF); in the older version, dark stools (representing blood in the stool).

Structure: introduction → explore pain → GI general questions → red flags → KUB → other DDx early (rashes, trauma, mesenteric ischaemia: "Any racing of your heart?" → "Yes, my heart is beating a little funny and fast") → genital questions → liver & gallbladder questions.

PEFE (key point — pulse and rhythm): PR 90, irregular; generalised abdominal tenderness (ask rigidity, guarding, rebound); DRE — black/dark stools.

Mesenteric Ischaemia Reference Notes

Acute mesenteric ischaemia results from reduction or cessation of arterial inflow — most often superior mesenteric artery occlusion, either an embolus (classically from atrial fibrillation) or thrombosis of an already diseased, atherosclerotic vessel. Necrosis of the intestine soon follows if intervention is delayed.

Clinical features:

  • Abdominal pain gradually becoming intense; patients develop a "fear of eating"
  • Profuse vomiting; diarrhoea, with blood in about one-third of patients (later)
  • Diagnostic pattern: anxiety and prostration + intense central abdominal pain + profuse vomiting ± bloody diarrhoea → mesenteric arterial occlusion
  • Pain out of proportion to examination findings — the classic description: the patient reports 10/10 pain and is screaming, yet palpation reveals only minimal tenderness that does not match the history
  • Generalised tenderness, rigidity and rebound over infarcted bowel are later findings; shock develops later; AF (and other signs of atheroma) may be present
  • Septic features — confusion, dizziness, drowsiness

Investigations: CT (angiography) gives the best definition; mesenteric arteriography if embolus suspected; commonly only diagnosed at laparotomy.

Management: early surgery may prevent gut necrosis; resection of necrosed gut may be life-saving. Early diagnosis (within a few hours) is essential. Notes: mesenteric venous thrombosis can occur, usually with circulatory failure; inferior mesenteric artery occlusion is less severe with better survival.

Version 3 Diagnosis and Differentials

Diagnosis: "You have a condition called mesenteric ischaemia. Your heart is beating irregularly (atrial fibrillation), which can form a clot in the heart; the clot has travelled to the blood tubes of your bowels and is blocking them, causing the pain."

Reasons:

  • Generalised abdominal pain (felt all over the stomach)
  • Blood in the stools, in the form of dark stools (in late ischaemia/necrosis, blood in the stool — often fresh — is found)
  • An irregular pulse on examination

DDx (generalised peritonitis case): ruptured viscus — perforated peptic ulcer, ruptured appendicitis, ruptured diverticulitis, ruptured AAA, intra-abdominal haemorrhage (traumatic or non-traumatic); hernia; bowel obstruction; IBD.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The hernia variant uses 'strangulated/incarcerated inguinal hernia' interchangeably in the diagnosis statement.
These are distinct: an incarcerated hernia is irreducible; a strangulated hernia is irreducible WITH compromised blood supply (tender, tense, ± overlying skin changes, systemic features). A tender, irreducible hernia causing obstruction should be labelled 'incarcerated hernia with bowel obstruction, with suspected strangulation' — strangulation is a surgical emergency requiring immediate transfer (from general practice: call 000/arrange urgent ambulance transfer, nil by mouth, analgesia).
✏️ The SBO PE card lists 'Any distension? => No' while the reference box in the same document states abdominal distension is the most common examination finding in SBO — an internal inconsistency from the exam recall.
Distension is the most common finding in established SBO; its absence early in the course does not exclude obstruction. Candidates should still ask about distension and interpret a 'No' as consistent with early obstruction rather than evidence against it.
✏️ In the mesenteric ischaemia variant, the GP setting ends at 'explain diagnosis and differentials' with no escalation, and the notes do not state the time-critical action.
Suspected acute mesenteric ischaemia identified in general practice mandates immediate emergency transfer (call 000) for urgent CT angiography and surgical review — mortality rises steeply with delay. Early surgery within a few hours is the survival determinant, as the case's own reference box notes.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add the term 'obstipation' (absolute constipation — no flatus and no stool) explicitly to the SBO section with the paired screening questions ('Are you passing wind? When did you last open your bowels?'), since the notes identify these as the most commonly missed exam questions.
⭐ Add expected biochemistry for mesenteric ischaemia (raised lactate on VBG — report in mmol/L — leukocytosis, metabolic acidosis) and note that a normal lactate early does not exclude ischaemia; this pre-empts the standard examiner follow-up on investigations.
⭐ Add a CHA2DS2-VA/anticoagulation teaching link: newly identified AF causing an embolic event should prompt anticoagulation review after the acute episode — connects this station to the irStudy cardiology module.
⭐ Add a one-line analgesia principle for all three variants: adequate opioid analgesia does not mask surgical signs and should not be withheld in acute abdomen (contemporary Australian ED teaching), complementing the stem's 'painkillers already given'.
⭐ Create a comparison table (SBO vs strangulated hernia vs mesenteric ischaemia) as platform metadata: key discriminators are obstipation + previous surgery + absent/tinkling bowel sounds; irreducible tender groin lump; and pain out of proportion + AF + dark stools respectively.
