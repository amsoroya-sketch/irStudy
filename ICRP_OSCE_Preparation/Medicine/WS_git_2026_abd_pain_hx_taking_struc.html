Abdominal Pain — History Taking Structure

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / 1. Structure class (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Safe Diagnostic Model (Murtagh PROMPT Approach)

A safe diagnostic model for abdominal pain follows Murtagh's diagnostic sequence:

  • Probability — what are the most likely diagnoses?
  • Serious disorders not to be missed — red-flag conditions
  • Often missed (pitfalls) — commonly overlooked causes
  • Masquerades — conditions that mimic abdominal pathology
  • Is the patient trying to tell me something? — hidden agenda or concern

Chronic/Recurrent Abdominal Pain — Differential Diagnosis

Probability diagnoses:

  • Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)
  • Diverticular disease
  • Mittelschmerz
  • Peptic ulcer disease

Serious disorders not to miss:

  • CVS: myocardial infarction, ruptured AAA, mesenteric ischaemia
  • Cancers: bowel cancer causing obstruction
  • Severe infections: cholangitis, salpingitis, intra-abdominal abscess
  • Pancreatitis
  • Ectopic pregnancy (EP)
  • Volvulus

Pitfalls (often missed):

  • Pulmonary causes: pneumonia (especially lower lobe)
  • Faecal impaction
  • Herpes zoster (shingles)

Step 1 — Introduction, Haemodynamic Stability and Opening

1. Haemodynamic (HD) stability:

  • If vitals are given in the stem, no need to ask.
  • If not given, ask: "I want to make sure my patient is stable. Can you please tell me his/her vitals including BP, PR, RR and temperature?"
  • Sequence: ID check, hand hygiene (sanitise), introduce yourself to the patient, then ask about vitals.

2. Open-ended questions:

  • Introduce yourself.
  • "How can I help you?"
  • "Can you tell me more about the pain?"

3. Initial statement and observed behaviours — address the patient's concern (acknowledge, empathise, reassure):

  • Acknowledge: "I understand your concern" / "I am sorry to hear that" / "I know you are experiencing a tough time."
  • Empathy: "I am here to help you" / "I am here to support you."
  • Reassure: "I need to ask you a few questions so that I can get a better idea about your pain, and I promise to make the best management plan for you."

Step 2 — Pain Assessment (SIQORAA)

  • S — Site: "Tell me where you feel the pain." If right lower quadrant (RLQ): "Where did the pain start? Did it shift to the right lower side?" (migration to the RIF suggests appendicitis)
  • I — Intensity: "On a scale of 1 to 10, 1 being the least and 10 being the worst, how bad is your pain?"
  • Q — Quality: sharp versus dull ache
  • O — Onset/Timing: "When did it start? Is it on and off (colicky) or constant/continuous? Is it getting worse?"
  • R — Radiation: "Is it travelling anywhere?"
  • A — Alleviating/Aggravating factors: "Does anything make the pain better or worse? Is your pain worse after eating?" (rules out PUD, pancreatitis, cholecystitis) "Is there any specific position that makes your pain better or worse?"
  • A — Affecting your life: ask only when the presentation is not acute.

Step 3 — Ruling Out Differentials

1. General GI screen:

  • Nausea and vomiting? (upper GI)
  • Diarrhoea or constipation? (lower GI)
  • Bloating / passing gas / wind? (middle GI)
  • Fever?

2. Red-flag GI (cancer) questions:

  • General cancer questions: loss of weight (LOW)? loss of appetite (LOA)? any lumps or bumps (lymph nodes)? tiredness / lightheadedness / dizziness?
  • Specific bowel cancer questions: blood in stool (fresh)? dark-coloured stool (melaena)? change in bowel habit? family history of bowel cancer?

3. Any previous surgery?

4. Medications: NSAIDs (peptic ulcer disease), SGLT2 inhibitors (see expert correction — associated with euglycaemic DKA), oxycodone (constipation).

KUB screen:

  • Dysuria — "Do you have any pain or burning on passing urine?"
  • Frequency — "Are you passing urine more frequently?"
  • Blood in urine?

Lower abdominal pain (LLQ, RLQ, suprapubic):

  • Hernia: any swelling or lump in the groin?
  • Obstetric & gynaecological: symptoms — any vaginal bleeding? any vaginal discharge? Period history — LMP, regular or irregular? Sexual history — sexually active? practice safe sex / use condoms? (rules out PID) ever been diagnosed with a sexually transmitted infection? (if yes, consider PID)
  • Male: pain or swelling in your private parts/scrotum? (rules out testicular torsion)

Liver and gallbladder:

  • Yellow discolouration of the skin (jaundice)?
  • Dark urine / pale stool?
  • Travel history?

PUD & GORD:

  • "Is your pain related to eating food?"
  • Risk factors: coffee, alcohol, spicy food?

ACS & lower lobe pneumonia: any chest pain, shortness of breath, cough?

Other causes:

  • Mesenteric ischaemia: older patient with AF — "Have you noticed any funny racing of your heart?"
  • Shingles: "Do you have any rashes?"
  • Trauma: "Have you had any trauma?"
  • DKA: polydipsia — "Have you noticed any increase in your thirst?" Polyuria — "Are you passing more urine lately?"

Step 4 — Good Closure

  • SADMA: Smoking, Alcohol, Drugs, Medications, Allergies
  • PMHx: previous medical and surgical history
  • FHx: family history

Summary of the Approach

  1. Intro: confirm haemodynamic stability, open-ended questions, address the patient's concern.
  2. Explore the pain: SIQORAA.
  3. Rule out differentials: GI, KUB, upper abdomen (liver, PUD), ACS/pneumonia, lower abdomen.
  4. Good closure: SADMA, PMHx, FHx.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The medication screen lists 'SGLT - pancreatitis', implying SGLT2 inhibitors cause pancreatitis.
SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin) are principally associated with euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis, an important cause of abdominal pain with near-normal BGL — check ketones, not just glucose. Drug-induced pancreatitis is classically linked to GLP-1 receptor agonists (e.g. exenatide), thiazides, sodium valproate and azathioprine. Current eTG (Gastrointestinal) and the Australian Medicines Handbook reflect this distinction.
✏️ Pain intensity scale described as '1 to 10, 1 being the least'.
The Australian standard numeric rating scale is 0–10, where 0 is no pain and 10 is the worst pain imaginable. Also, for severe pain (≥7/10) analgesia should be offered during the history, not withheld until assessment is complete — pain relief is a patient right under NSW Health pain management standards, and adequate analgesia does not mask surgical signs.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add SNAP social history and Aboriginal/Torres Strait Islander status to the closure step (Step 4): smoking pack-years, alcohol in standard drinks (Australian guideline: no more than 10 standard drinks/week), and respectful identification for Close the Gap — expected in AMC clinical stations and AHPRA-aligned practice.
⭐ Add ICE (Ideas, Concerns, Expectations) to the closure and a spoken summary back to the patient ('Have I missed anything important?') — AMC examiners award marks for patient-centred closure, and it is absent from the current Step 4.
⭐ For the 50-year-old man in the stem, explicitly add ruptured AAA screening questions (sudden severe pain radiating to the back, syncope, known aneurysm, smoking history) and note the age-specific red flag: new abdominal pain in a patient over 50 warrants AAA consideration before benign diagnoses.
⭐ Add bowel cancer screening context for a 50-year-old: ask about participation in the National Bowel Cancer Screening Program (iFOBT every 2 years from age 45–74 under current Australian guidelines) when eliciting the red-flag bowel questions.
⭐ Fix recurring OCR/spelling artifacts before publishing: 'Mittleschmerz' → Mittelschmerz, 'Volvolus' → volvulus, 'ishchemia' → ischaemia, 'thrust' → thirst, 'vagianl' → vaginal; use Australian spelling throughout (diarrhoea, ischaemia, gynaecological).
