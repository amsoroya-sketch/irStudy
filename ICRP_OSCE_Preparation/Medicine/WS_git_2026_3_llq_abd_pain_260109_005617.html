LLQ Abdominal Pain (Diverticulitis, Bowel Cancer, Small Bowel Obstruction)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / 2.Abdominal pain case (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Case Stem (Version 1)

Your next patient is a 50-year-old man who comes to your general practice complaining of LLQ abdominal pain for 1 month.

  • Task: Take history for 6 minutes
  • Explain your diagnosis and differentials

The duration is longer than an acute abdomen. The Predominant Assessment Area (PAA) is history taking — even without reaching the exact diagnosis, what determines a pass is history-taking quality: a good sequence, organisation, and coverage of differentials. AMC describes this as clinical reasoning; candidates without good clinical reasoning fail.

Brainstorm: Differentials for a 50-Year-Old with LLQ Pain

  • Red flags (any serious disorder, especially bowel cancer)
  • GI group
  • KUB group
  • Hernia
  • Genitalia (in a man, still assess)
  • Others (if there is time)

History structure: Introduction (builds initial connection) → Explore the complaint (pain) → Review the DDx (never forgetting red flags) → Good closure → move on to diagnosis.

Opening the Consultation

Enter the station, ID check, sanitise hands, sit down. Examiner: "This is Gary, your patient. Now start history taking."

Haemodynamic stability: optional in this case given the 1-month duration (it could be argued a cancer could obstruct today, so checking is defensible), but in acute abdomen cases haemodynamic stability must always be confirmed first.

  • Introduction: "Hi, my name is Amir, I'm your doctor for today."
  • Open-ended question: "How can I help you today, Gary?"

Patient statement: "I've been having this pain for 1 month now. It's not getting better. Now it's become a little bit annoying. I'm starting to worry that there might be something wrong with me."

Respond to the statement: "I do understand your concern, Gary — this is quite stressful, but I am here to help you. To get a better understanding of your pain I need to ask you a few questions; once I understand it better I'll be able to help you and provide the best management plan. How does that sound?"

"Are you in pain now?" → "No, I'm not in any pain now."

Pain Assessment (Chronic Pain Additions)

  • Site: "Can you reconfirm where the pain is?" → Left lower side of the stomach.
  • Intensity: 1–10 scale over the last few weeks → 5/10.
  • Quality: sharp ache or vague dull ache? → dull ache.
  • Onset/time: started a month ago (chronic abdominal pain). First episode ever → yes. On/off or constant → usually constant. Getting worse? → yes/no (may stay the same).
  • ** Night pain (chronic-pain red flag — not asked in acute abdomen): "Do you have any pain at night that wakes you up?" → Yes/No.
  • Radiation: "Does your pain radiate anywhere?"
  • Alleviating/aggravating: nothing makes it better or worse; no relationship to food; no positional change.
  • ** Impact (chronic pain): "How is the pain affecting your life?"

Summary so far: first episode, 4 weeks, LLQ, moderate severity, no red-flag symptoms yet.

GI Screening and Constipation Probing

  • Nausea, vomiting? → No
  • Bloating? → No
  • Passing wind? → Yes
  • Diarrhoea lately? → No
  • Constipation? → Yes — probe further. "How long?" → "A very long time."

Constipation probing: is it hard stool? difficulty passing? painful defecation? → "Sometimes it's hard and stony, that's why it's a bit hard to push out, but I haven't had difficulty passing my stools."

  • Fever? → No

Red Flags (Most Significant in Chronic Pain — Bowel Cancer Questions)

General cancer questions:

  • Lost any weight? → No (Version 1)
  • Lost any appetite? → No
  • Any lumps and bumps? → No
  • Feeling tired/dizzy? (acute → dizzy; chronic → tired)

Specific questions:

  • Fresh blood in the stool? → No
  • Dark stool (proximal GI bleeding)? → No
  • Recent change in bowel motion? → No
  • Family history of cancers? → No
  • Bowel cancer screening: "Have you done your bowel cancer screening?" (National program: an iFOBT kit is mailed out, completed at home and returned; if positive, the GP refers for colonoscopy — see expert corrections regarding the current starting age of 45.)
  • Previous abdominal surgery? → No
  • Regular medications? → No

KUB, Pelvic/Genital and Other Questions

KUB group: dysuria → No; frequency → No; blood in urine → No (a yes would change the picture substantially).

Pelvic/genital group (male with lower abdominal pain):

  • Hernia: swelling or lumps in the groin? → No
  • Swelling or redness of the scrotum/private parts? Pain in the private parts?

Other questions:

  • Rashes on the abdomen? (shingles)
  • Injuries/trauma to the abdomen?
  • Racing/funny beating of the heart? (AF → mesenteric ischaemia)

Good closure (SADMA): smoking → No; alcohol → occasional glass of wine weekly; drugs → No; medications already asked; allergies; PMHx/FHx → "generally healthy".

Key point: the key to this case is not the constipation — it is ruling out red flags before diagnosing diverticulitis.

Version 1 Findings and Diagnosis: Diverticulitis

History findings: long-standing constipation; no red flags; no weight loss. There are no PE findings — the provisional diagnosis rests on history.

  • Diverticulosis: diverticula (multiple pouches in the large bowel, e.g. on CT) with chronic constipation but no inflammation and no pain.
  • Diverticulitis: pain and tenderness because a diverticulum has become inflamed.

Explaining the diagnosis: "Most likely you have a condition called diverticulitis. A diverticulum is a pouch or pocket in the large bowel wall, usually associated with constipation. In your case one of these pouches has become inflamed." (A simple drawing is optional.)

Reasons:

  1. Site — this pain is classically in the left lower side of the abdomen.
  2. Long-standing constipation — raised pressure on the bowel wall produces the pouches, which later become inflamed.
  3. Red flags excluded — no bleeding, no weight loss, no other concerning features.

Version 1 Differentials (Best 4–5 for 50-Year-Old with LLQ Pain)

Do not list 50 undifferentiated GI diagnoses — choose a good, targeted list:

  1. Bowel cancer (most important): pain lasting a month, but no red flags — no rectal bleeding, no weight loss.
  2. Faecal impaction/constipation: severe constipation itself can block the bowel and cause pain.
  3. Hernia: "a sac in the groin" — unlikely with no groin swelling.
  4. KUB — UTI and ureteric colic: unlikely with no dysuria, frequency or haematuria.
  5. Epididymo-orchitis: testicular/tube infection can drag pain into the abdomen — unlikely with no scrotal redness or swelling.

Other DDx (mention briefly): gastroenteritis, bowel obstruction, IBD; shingles; mesenteric ischaemia; bladder cancer; kidney stones/renal colic/urinary retention; trauma.

Note: volvulus is not the same as faecal impaction — volvulus is twisting of the bowel (coffee-bean sign on X-ray); faecal impaction is impacted stool.

Diverticulitis Reference Notes

Acute diverticulitis: acute left-sided abdominal pain, usually associated with constipation; pain increased by walking and change of position. Acute pain + left-sided radiation + fever → acute diverticulitis.

Signs: tenderness, guarding, rigidity in the left iliac fossa; fever.

Complications: bleeding, perforation, abscess, peritonitis.

Investigations: CT abdomen (with contrast) is the best single test to confirm the diagnosis, indicate severity and exclude complications — recommended for a first or severe episode. Non-contrast CT is suboptimal; oral contrast often does not reach the sigmoid colon in time. If non-acute, arrange bloods and consider stool testing if diarrhoea. Conduct a urine pregnancy test in women of childbearing age, and consider pelvic ultrasound in female patients to exclude gynaecological causes.

Management of Uncomplicated Diverticulitis (GP Setting)

If uncomplicated (no perforation, no sepsis features, no generalised peritonitis, stable vitals, no mass):

  • Simple analgesia — paracetamol
  • Clear fluids until symptoms settle, then slowly upgrade to soft diet (low-residue in the acute phase)
  • Antibiotics are not routinely required: recent evidence shows no clear benefit of antibiotics in mild uncomplicated diverticulitis in systemically well, immunocompetent patients. Consider oral amoxicillin+clavulanate (Augmentin), twice daily for 5–10 days, in patients who are symptomatic but not improving with bowel rest and simple analgesia, or with: immune compromise; right-sided diverticulitis on CT; failure to improve after 72 hours of conservative treatment.
  • Red flags / safety-netting: "If the pain gets worse, you become feverish, drowsy or dizzy — go to the emergency department."
  • Arrange CT to confirm diverticulitis if not previously imaged.

Referral for acute general surgery assessment if: no improvement within 2–3 days of conservative management; clinical deterioration; suspected complications; or if intramuscular/intravenous opioid analgesia is required. Call an ambulance and monitor if the patient is systemically unwell.

Management of Complicated Diverticulitis (ED/Inpatient)

For patients with sepsis or septic shock, follow the sepsis pathway (see eTG: sepsis from a biliary or gastrointestinal source; early recognition of sepsis/septic shock in adults).

Empirical IV antibiotic therapy (three-drug regimen, per Therapeutic Guidelines):

  • Gentamicin IV (see eTG aminoglycoside dosing principles)
  • PLUS metronidazole 500 mg IV 12-hourly
  • PLUS either amoxicillin 2 g IV 6-hourly or ampicillin 2 g IV 6-hourly

Complicated diverticulitis (positive blood culture, perforation, peritonitis, sepsis/septic shock, or abscess above the size threshold) requires admission. After resolution of a complicated episode, arrange colorectal cancer screening (colonoscopy) some weeks after recovery — see eTG: colorectal cancer screening in patients with diverticular disease after an acute diverticulitis episode.

Version 2: Same Stem, Different Findings — Bowel Cancer

Same 50-year-old with LLQ pain for 1 month. Diagnostic momentum trap: if you assume diverticulitis, you fail. The PAA is still history taking.

Pain plus chronic additions: do not forget night pain and impact.

Key findings this time:

  • Constipation for a long time
  • Blood in stools (+)
  • Weight loss (+): probe — "How many kilos?" → 5 kg. "Over what period?" → 2 months. "Intentional?" → No.
  • Bowel cancer screening kit received in the post but not done

Provisional diagnosis: bowel cancer.

Sensitive explanation: "During our discussion I was considering a number of conditions. I'm concerned about the possibility of bowel cancer in your case, and I have good reasons: chronic abdominal pain for a few weeks; significant unintentional weight loss (5 kg in 2 months is not normal); and blood in your stool, which is definitely not normal." (Avoid alarming language such as "nasty growth".)

Differentials: diverticulitis (inflammation of the bowel — less likely given the red flags), faecal impaction/constipation, hernia (no groin swelling), UTI/ureteric colic (no urinary symptoms), epididymo-orchitis (no scrotal signs), plus bowel obstruction, volvulus, gastroenteritis, shingles.

Cognitive bias — diagnostic momentum: the tendency to adhere to a previous diagnostic label despite evidence to the contrary.

Version 3 (2025): 45-Year-Old — Small Bowel Obstruction

Your next patient is a 45-year-old man complaining of abdominal pain.

  • Task: Take history (prompt at 4 minutes); ask physical examination from the examiner; explain diagnosis and differentials.

The stem gives no site or timing. On asking, the pain is LLQ, 5/10; duration in days.

Key history findings:

  • No nausea/vomiting; bloating yes/no
  • Not passing wind for the last few days (commonly forgotten question)
  • No diarrhoea, no constipation, no hard stools, bowel motions otherwise normal
  • Last bowel motion 3–4 days ago ("When was the last time you passed a bowel motion?" — the question most often missed in the exam)
  • No red flags (no weight loss, no blood in stool)
  • Previous appendicectomy 10 years ago
  • No medications; no KUB or genital findings

This does not look like diverticulitis.

Version 3 PEFE

  • General appearance: alert (not drowsy), not dehydrated, no pallor.
  • Vital signs: BP, PR, RR, temperature (temperature most important).
  • Abdominal examination:
    • Inspection: symmetrical movement with respiration → Yes; Cullen/Grey Turner signs → No; appendicectomy scar (volunteered); distension → No.
    • Palpation: tenderness in the left lower quadrant/left iliac fossa. Rigidity → No; guarding → Yes; rebound → Yes/No (localised peritonism). Masses/abscess → ask.
    • Percussion: no tenderness.
    • Auscultation: bowel sounds absent.
  • Inguinal and DRE: inguinal area normal; DRE → empty rectum.
  • Scrotal examination (lower abdominal pain in a man): no swelling/redness; no tenderness or masses.
  • Office test: urine dipstick normal.

PEFE summary: appendicectomy scar; absent bowel sounds; empty rectum on DRE.

Version 3 Diagnosis and Differentials

Diagnosis: "You are having a condition called small bowel obstruction (bowel obstruction). Your small bowel is blocked by a band of scar tissue (adhesions) that formed after your previous surgery." (Adhesions classically cause small bowel obstruction.)

Reasons:

  • Abdominal pain
  • Obstipation — not passing gas and not opening the bowels for several days (the role-player is instructed not to volunteer this unless specifically asked)
  • No bowel sounds on examination; previous surgery with a visible scar

DDx of left lower quadrant pain: 1. bowel obstruction; 2. bowel cancer; 3. diverticulitis; 4. faecal impaction; 5. hernia; 6. epididymo-orchitis; 7. KUB group — UTI, ureteric colic, renal colic, bladder cancer; 8. other GI — gastroenteritis, IBD, mesenteric ischaemia, volvulus; 9. shingles.

Teaching point: the structure is memorised, but the diagnosis decision is made at the appropriate time from the findings — same station framework, three different diagnoses (diverticulitis, bowel cancer, SBO).

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The notes describe the National Bowel Cancer Screening Program as 'people >50 yrs' receiving FOBT kits in the post.
Since July 2024, the National Bowel Cancer Screening Program covers Australians aged 45–74: people aged 45–49 can opt in to receive their first kit, and kits are automatically mailed from age 50, with 2-yearly iFOBT thereafter. A positive iFOBT is followed by colonoscopy referral (Cancer Council Australia / Australian Government guidance).
✏️ The teaching narrative instructs 'I'll give you Augmentin (BD 5-10 days)' as routine for uncomplicated diverticulitis, while the pasted guideline extract elsewhere in the same notes correctly states antibiotics show no clear benefit in mild uncomplicated disease. The two statements conflict.
Current Therapeutic Guidelines (eTG) advice: antibiotics are NOT routinely required for uncomplicated diverticulitis in systemically well, immunocompetent patients — manage with analgesia (paracetamol) and bowel rest, and reserve oral amoxicillin+clavulanate for patients who are immunocompromised, not improving after 48–72 hours, or with right-sided disease. The assessed sections have been reconciled to reflect this.
✏️ OCR artifacts in the source: 'Volvolus' for volvulus, 'Chrons' for Crohn disease, 'fecal' (US spelling) for faecal, 'Epididymo-Orchitis' capitalisation, and garbled shorthand lines from handwritten annotations.
Corrected to Australian spelling (faecal, volvulus, Crohn disease, appendicectomy) in the structured sections; non-clinical OCR noise from handwritten scribbles was removed without altering clinical content.
