Upper GI Cases: Upper Abdominal Pain, Chronic Dyspepsia, Dysphagia, Haematemesis and GORD Counselling

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / 3. UGI Case (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Overview and Exam Approach

This bundle covers the upper gastrointestinal AMC clinical exam cluster: left upper quadrant / epigastric pain (peptic ulcer disease), chronic indigestion with red flags (gastric cancer), dysphagia (2025), haematemesis (new, June 2025) and GORD counselling. The dominant task is history taking; work step by step.

Entering the station: show your ID tags, sanitise your hands, and before starting ask the examiner: “Is my patient vitally/haemodynamically stable? Can you please tell me the vitals?” Example vitals given: BP 110/70 mmHg, pulse 88/min, respiratory rate 12/min, temperature 36.5°C. If required, ask for each vital sign one by one. (For a pure history-taking task, confirming haemodynamic stability is less critical — balance it against time.)

Introduction (simple and quick): “Hi Jenna, my name is Amir, I’m your doctor for today” (it does not matter whether the role is GP or medical officer), then an open-ended question: “How can I help you today?”

Case 1: Upper Abdominal Pain (LUQ) - Opening and Addressing Concern

Opening statement (patient Jenna): “Doctor, I have a pain in my left upper abdomen. It’s been going on for some time now, not an acute pain. I’m worried, I need you to fix this.”

Before starting the history, respond to the open concern:

  • Empathy / acknowledge the concern: “I’m really sorry to hear that, Jenna. The pain you describe is concerning.”
  • Reassure: “I’ll need to ask you a few questions to get a better idea about your pain, and I promise to take good care of you. Is that okay with you?”

Case 1: Pain History (SIQORA)

  • Site: even if already stated, confirm once more. Some patients indicate the epigastrium rather than the LUQ — the structure does not change.
  • Intensity: scale 1–10.
  • Quality: dull ache in this case.
  • Onset: since when? First episode? On and off or constant? Gradually getting worse? Does the pain wake you at night?
  • Radiation: none in this case.
  • Alleviating and aggravating factors:
    • General: “Does anything make it worse?”
    • Relation to position: negative here. (Acute pancreatitis pain is typically worse lying supine and relieved by sitting up and leaning forward — a helpful clinical clue.)
    • Relation to food: positive — usually worse after eating. Then ask which food: dairy suggests lactose intolerance; pasta/bread suggests coeliac disease; spicy food suggests peptic ulcer/gastritis; if all food triggers it, think peptic ulcer disease.
    • How soon after eating? Classic teaching: gastric ulcer pain starts soon after eating (and gastric ulcers carry malignant potential); duodenal ulcer pain is late post-prandial, 2–3 hours after meals (duodenal ulcers are almost never malignant).

General GI screening questions: nausea/vomiting (no), bloating (no), passing wind (yes), diarrhoea (no), constipation (no), fever (no).

Case 1: Differential Diagnosis and Red Flags

Group the differentials into three groups and always include red flags — this presentation can be pancreatic or gastric cancer. Which group to start with depends on the site: LUQ pain → start GORD/PUD; epigastric pain → pancreatitis or GORD/PUD.

  • Pancreatitis: pain better leaning forward? Nausea/vomiting (already asked). Any trauma to the abdomen? Recent fever or flu-like illness? Previous history of high fat (triglycerides) in the blood?
  • GORD/PUD (LUQ pain):
    • Red flag questions (most important) — gastric cancer screen. General: loss of weight, loss of appetite, lumps and bumps, tiredness, pain waking at night. Specific: dark stools (proximal bleeding → melaena); ever vomited blood (haematemesis — a new 2025 case); any difficulty swallowing (oesophageal or gastric cancer); family history of bowel or oesophageal cancer.
    • GORD-specific: heartburn? Bitter/metallic taste in the mouth? Sudden increase of saliva in the mouth (waterbrash)?
    • Risk factors (PUD and GORD): medications — drugs that cause gastritis/oesophagitis include NSAIDs, corticosteroids, bisphosphonates, anticoagulants, antiplatelets, methotrexate, ferrous sulphate and potassium tablets. Patients may mention over-the-counter reflux preparations such as Quick-Eze or Mylanta (antacids), Gaviscon (alginate/antacid) or Somac (pantoprazole, a PPI); if unsure what a brand is, you may ask the examiner. If taking an antacid, ask whether it helps. AMC has also used brand names for other drug classes, e.g. Micardis (telmisartan) for hypertension. Ask about paracetamol (Panadol) and ibuprofen (Nurofen) use.
    • Alcohol (if yes: how often, how much), smoking (ever smoked?), coffee/tea, spicy food.
  • Liver/gallbladder: yellow discolouration of skin? Itchiness? Dark urine or pale stools? Previous similar episodes, especially after a large fatty meal (biliary colic)?
  • Wrap up with ACS and pneumonia: any chest pain? Shortness of breath? Cough?

Closure (if time allows) — SADMA: smoking, alcohol, drugs, medications, allergies; plus past medical history and family history.

Case 1: Diagnosis and Differentials to Present

Working diagnosis: peptic ulcer disease. “After talking to you, most likely you are having a condition called peptic ulcer disease. This is an ulcer in your stomach, most likely a side effect of the ibuprofen (Nurofen) you are taking.”

Reasons: LUQ site, dull character, risk factors for ulcer (NSAID use), red flags ruled out.

Differentials to mention: “I was also thinking about gastric cancer, pancreatic cancer and oesophageal cancer — these symptoms can occur in cancer, but the red flags are all negative.” Then: GORD, pancreatitis, biliary colic, cholecystitis, cholangitis, hepatitis, hepatocellular cancer, ACS, lower lobe pneumonia, and indigestion/dyspepsia (important).

Case 2: Chronic Indigestion / Dyspepsia (6 months) - Concepts

A very vague complaint — know exactly what you are doing. If the presentation is vague, ask the patient to describe what they mean. (Analogy: if a patient says they are ‘tired’, clarify whether it is overwork, hypothyroidism or sleep apnoea.)

  • Upper abdominal discomfort may be PUD, chronic pancreatitis or a pancreatic pseudocyst.
  • Common pitfall: mistaking a cardiac problem for a GI problem — ischaemic chest discomfort after eating can be described as ‘indigestion’; biliary colic can present similarly.
  • Chronic here means 6 months. In a face-to-face exam with more than 3 tasks (this is a 4-task case) there is no time to waste — make a quick differential list.
  • Diabetic patients get gastroparesis most often (autonomic neuropathy affecting the stomach).
  • A large mass pressing on the stomach can cause these symptoms. Chronic pancreatitis does not always present with typical pain and vomiting — it can be mild discomfort, bloating and fullness long term.
  • If red flags are present, the provisional diagnosis is cancer until proven otherwise. New dyspepsia in middle or old age — think cancer.

Case 2: History Structure

For a pure history-taking task, checking haemodynamic stability first is not essential (think about time). Open-ended question: “How can I help you today?”

Patient (Jimmy): “I am having indigestion, going for 6 months now, gradually getting worse. Mylanta (an antacid) used to help but not anymore, which is concerning.”

Respond: acknowledge/empathise (“I do understand — you’ve done a great job coming in; we’re going to have a chat about this”) and reassure (“we’re going to solve this problem together — is that okay with you?”).

Explore (no SIQORA for a vague complaint — use the four boxes):

  • Describe: “Can you describe what you mean by indigestion? Is it heartburn, discomfort, or bloating?” If it is a discomfort/pain, open the pain box (SIQORA): site upper abdomen; intensity 3/5; quality burning discomfort; onset 6 months, continuous, getting worse; no radiation.
  • Timing: since when, continuous or intermittent, worsening?
  • Alleviating and aggravating factors: general first (“what makes it better/worse?” — the patient may not have noticed), then specifically relation to food and eating (worse after eating; here immediately after eating), specific foods including dairy (lactose).
  • Effect on life: 6 months of symptoms must be affecting life — ask how.

If AMC sets a bloating case instead, there is no SIQORA — use the same four concepts: describe, timing, alleviating/aggravating factors, effect on life.

General GI questions: nausea, vomiting, bloating, passing wind, constipation, diarrhoea, fever. (Approximately 2.5 minutes gone, 90 seconds left.)

Differentials — choose carefully:

  1. Red flags. General: lost weight? (positive — how many kilograms, over what period, intentional?), loss of appetite, lumps and bumps, tiredness. Specific: dark stools (if positive, we know what this is), vomited blood, difficulty swallowing.
  2. PUD: does the discomfort wake you at night? Risk factors: medications, alcohol, coffee, spicy food.
  3. GORD: metallic/bitter/acidic taste in the mouth? Sudden increase in saliva?
  4. Pancreatic pseudocyst: any trauma to the abdomen (the main cause)?
  5. Liver and gallbladder: yellow discolouration (liver)? Previous episodes of pain after large meals (gallbladder)?
  6. Lactose intolerance: relation to dairy foods?
  7. CVS — ischaemic heart disease: any chest pain or shortness of breath?

Case 2: PEFE (Physical Examination Findings from Examiner)

Most likely a malignant peptic ulcer (possibly gastric/oesophageal cancer). Ask the examiner for physical examination findings.

  • Core — abdominal examination (do not skip anything): inspection (symmetrical movement with respiration, distension?); superficial and deep palpation (tenderness, masses); percussion (tenderness); auscultation; inguinal orifices (may skip to save time); DRE — ask the examiner.
  • CVS: S1, S2, murmurs. Respiratory examination may be skipped to save time.
  • Relevant peripheral signs (red flags): general appearance — pallor, cachexia, lymphadenopathy; specifically a left supraclavicular lymph node (Virchow node) is found in GI malignancy (both online and face-to-face recalls).
  • Vital signs: BP, pulse, respiratory rate, temperature.

Case 2: Breaking the Diagnosis and Investigations

Delivering the diagnosis: be sensitive, inform the patient, but not overly alarming — there is no guarantee of cancer (it could be a bleeding peptic ulcer). Keep it clear: “I considered different causes for your problem. In your case I am concerned about the possibility of gastric cancer — that is a cancer in your stomach. This is not confirmed yet, so investigations are necessary.”

Reasons: long-standing indigestion, getting worse, no longer responding to medication; losing weight; dark stools indicating bleeding in the stomach.

Differential order: 2nd — PUD (the patient may have had PUD first, which is why Mylanta used to help; now it no longer works); 3rd — GORD; 4th — chronic pancreatitis / pancreatic pseudocyst; 5th — biliary colic; 6th — lactose intolerance; 7th — medication-induced oesophagitis/gastritis; 8th — IHD; 9th — IBS. In this case do not spend time reasoning each differential.

Investigations:

  1. Endoscopy (most important): “We pass a small tube with a camera through the food pipe down to the stomach; if we see any lesion we will take samples for biopsy.”
  2. FBE and iron studies. FOBT is not needed (we are not screening). A urea breath test can be mentioned, but H. pylori testing will be done on the biopsy anyway.

Dysphagia (2025) - Framework

This station has appeared as a pilot question and as a scored question; several versions exist and all are covered here. It is a ‘globus’-style case — use the AMC handbook and the RACGP (AJGP) dysphagia article so nothing is missed.

Step 1 — divide by anatomical location:

  1. Oropharyngeal dysphagia — high in the food pipe: delay in swallow initiation, coughing, choking or nasal regurgitation with food.
  2. Oesophageal dysphagia — patients often cannot self-localise the level, but may report a catching sensation in the throat or chest.

Key history question: “Does it happen early, when you start to swallow, or later on, in your chest?”

Step 2 — divide by mechanism:

  1. Motility disorder — problem with oesophageal movement: affects solids and liquids from the start.
  2. Obstructive disorder — a blockage: typically affects solids first; patients may report switching to pureed food. (Liquids-plus-solids can also mean severe late obstruction.)

Key differentials to hold in mind: oropharyngeal malignancy and oesophageal malignancy (pharyngeal cancer gives similar symptoms); a large thyroid mass/goitre compressing the food pipe; strictures (important — a chronic complication of chronic oesophagitis/GORD); achalasia (the classic motility problem — solids and liquids); neurological causes (myasthenia gravis, multiple sclerosis, stroke, autonomic neuropathy — these give neurological deficits); and medications.

Dysphagia: RACGP (AJGP) Differential Tables (cleaned from OCR)

Oropharyngeal pathology:

  • Head and neck malignancy: squamous cell carcinoma is the most common; other cancers include lymphoma and salivary gland tumours. Laryngitis, pharyngitis and tonsillitis can also cause odynophagia.
  • Pharyngeal diverticulum: Zenker diverticulum is the most common, due to a spasmodic upper oesophageal sphincter creating a high-pressure pharynx; often identified on barium swallow.
  • Radiation injury: post-radiotherapy effects for head and neck cancer include mucositis, xerostomia, fibrosis and strictures. Regular gum chewing to promote salivation, increased daily fluid intake and oral lubricants may help xerostomia; strictures are seen on barium swallow and require endoscopic dilatation.
  • Cervical vertebral osteophytes: commonly seen on barium swallow; may protrude into pharyngeal mucosa causing discomfort with bolus transfer. Osteophytectomy is not routinely recommended.
  • Thyroid goitre: extrinsic compression of the upper aerodigestive tract; ultrasound and CT of neck and chest are helpful initial investigations.
  • Parkinson disease: dysphagia from muscular rigidity and bradykinesia increases the likelihood of silent aspiration; swallow assessment and rehabilitation with a speech pathologist is vital.
  • Alzheimer disease: dysphagic geriatric patients with dementia often develop malnutrition, dehydration, cachexia, social isolation and declining quality of life.
  • Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (motor neurone disease): progressive neurodegeneration with bulbar involvement; no reversible treatment — management is swallow rehabilitation via speech pathology, with neurology and ENT input to manage sialorrhoea, reduce aspiration and support communication.
  • Myasthenia gravis: autoimmune antibody-mediated destruction at the neuromuscular junction.
  • Multiple sclerosis: autoimmune demyelinating disease of the CNS; dysphagia is prevalent though evidence-based management options are limited.
  • Central (stroke and other CNS) disorders: dyscoordination of the pre-oral, oral and pharyngeal phases; delayed reflexive swallow, blunted bolus propulsion, limited airway protection.

Oesophageal pathology:

  • Oesophageal adenocarcinoma: the most common oesophageal cancer; dysphagia usually presents as a sign of late-stage disease.
  • Achalasia: results from inappropriate contraction (failure of relaxation) of the lower oesophageal sphincter.
  • Distal oesophageal spasm: rapid, prolonged propagation of peristalsis causing oesophageal muscular hypertrophy over time.
  • Schatzki ring: a ring narrowing the distal oesophageal lumen; pathophysiology unclear.
  • Scleroderma / systemic sclerosis: autoimmune replacement of oesophageal musculature with fibrotic, aperistaltic tissue.
  • Autonomic neuropathy: most commonly caused by diabetes mellitus, with dysregulation of autonomic control.
  • Foreign body: presents with hypersalivation, odynophagia and bolus regurgitation; endoscopic removal is considered when medical measures (glucagon, benzodiazepines, calcium channel blockers, carbonated liquids) fail.
  • Eosinophilic oesophagitis: chronic relapsing food-related inflammatory disorder with eosinophil deposition and oesophageal fibrosis; treated with targeted allergen food avoidance and topical corticosteroids.
  • Functional dysphagia: a diagnosis of exclusion (psychogenic aetiology not explained by other causes).

Medication-induced dysphagia:

  • Sedatives (cognitive inhibition → oropharyngeal dyscoordination): benzodiazepines (lorazepam, alprazolam, diazepam); opioids (codeine, hydromorphone, fentanyl, oxycodone).
  • Antipsychotics (D2 antagonism suppressing deglutition): clozapine, haloperidol, lithium, olanzapine, quetiapine, risperidone.
  • Xerostomia-related medications (anticholinergic effect reducing saliva): antiemetics (metoclopramide, ondansetron, promethazine, prochlorperazine); decongestants/antihistamines (diphenhydramine, pseudoephedrine); antidepressants (citalopram, sertraline, amitriptyline).
  • Anticholinergics/antimuscarinics (impair oesophageal peristalsis, cause xerostomia): atropine, benztropine, hyoscine, ipratropium, oxybutynin.
  • Drugs causing oesophageal mucosal injury (caustic irritation, may cause strictures): antibiotics (clindamycin, doxycycline, erythromycin); bisphosphonates (alendronate, risedronate, zoledronic acid); NSAIDs (aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen, indomethacin); supplements (iron tablets, potassium chloride, vitamin C tablets).

Red flag symptoms associated with malignancy: odynophagia, otalgia (ear pain), neck masses, unintentional weight loss and bloody expectoration.

Dysphagia: History Taking

Key discriminating questions: difficulty initiating swallowing (oropharyngeal gives choking at the beginning); changes in voice; reflux/PUD history; catching or sticking sensation (“is anything sticking in your chest, or is it difficult to start the swallow?” — to localise); and odynophagia (most important) — painful versus merely difficult swallowing.

Opening: “How can I help you?” Patients may say “I’m finding it difficult to swallow and I’m worried” or “food is sticking when I swallow”. Build rapport by responding to the statement: “Thank you for coming in for assessment. I’ll need to know more about this to make the best decision about what’s going on and provide the best management — are you okay to get started?” (Only say “I understand your concern” if the patient actually expresses concern.)

Explore the dysphagia (boxes):

  • Describe: What do you mean — hard to swallow, or painful? Hard to initiate? Any choking? Do you feel food sticking in the throat or the chest (oropharyngeal vs oesophageal)?
  • Timing: since when (one exam version 6 weeks, another 6 months). For 6 weeks: first time? For 6 months: on/off or continuous? Worsening?
  • Pattern: only solids (obstructive) or solids and liquids (motility, or severe late obstruction)?
  • Alleviating/aggravating: specific foods; how is it affecting your life?

General GI questions (for every GI case): nausea, vomiting, bloating, passing wind, constipation, diarrhoea, fever.

Differentials in order:

  1. Red flags. General cancer questions: weight loss (if positive: how many kg, over what period, intentional?), loss of appetite, lumps and bumps, tiredness, night sweats. Specific: oropharyngeal cancer — longstanding sore throat, ear pain, voice change; oesophageal cancer — ever vomited blood, dark stools; thyroid cancer — neck lump (weather preference screens thyroid dysfunction more than cancer); lung cancer — cough, coughing up blood.
  2. Infections: fever/chills, sore throat (only if not already asked — do not ask twice).
  3. Stricture: GORD — heartburn (if positive: taking any medication, is it helping?), bitter taste, increased saliva; PUD risk factors — smoking, spicy food, caffeine.
  4. Motility problems: neurological — MS (blurring of vision), stroke (weakness, numbness, pins and needles); autoimmune — rash, joint pain.
  5. Medications.
  6. Alcohol.

Also ask: past medical history (diabetes, stroke) and family history of stomach problems. No physical examination in this version.

Dysphagia: Diagnosis Versions

Version with red flags — diagnosis: oesophageal cancer. A normal PEFE does not rule out cancer — the patient has weight loss, difficulty swallowing and a long history of GORD. It could be a stricture, but red flags are present, so the top differential is oesophageal cancer and only endoscopy can exclude it. Say: “I was considering different conditions that cause difficulty swallowing. In your case I am concerned about the possibility of oesophageal cancer — a cancer in your food pipe.” Reasons: long-standing GORD with Mylanta no longer working and symptoms getting worse; red flags — loss of weight; swallowing difficulty with solids only, meaning something is blocking. Differentials: other cancers (oropharyngeal/throat, thyroid, lung), stricture (narrowing of the food pipe), infection, motility problems (MS, MG, neuropathy, systemic sclerosis, achalasia), medications.

Version with no red flags: dysphagia with long-standing GORD but no red flags — GORD alone does not cause dysphagia, so the diagnosis is peptic stricture secondary to long-standing GORD: “these are scars caused by long-term inflammation, narrowing the food pipe and blocking movement.” Differential: cancer (mouth/throat/oropharyngeal, oesophageal, thyroid, lung) and the same other differentials.

Key to passing: maintain the structure — explore/define the problem, differentiate oropharyngeal vs oesophageal and motility vs obstructive, screen red flags, present grouped differentials.

Haematemesis (new, June 2025)

New cases are dominated by history taking; differentials are woven into the history.

Differentials: oesophageal cancer, gastric cancer, severe oesophagitis, oesophageal varices (a complication — so ask about the underlying condition: portal hypertension and chronic liver disease), bleeding disorders, Mallory–Weiss tear. Others exist (e.g. vascular malformations of the oesophagus) — use them if time allows.

History:

  • Confirm haemodynamic stability first: ask the examiner for BP, pulse, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation and temperature before starting.
  • Opening: “How can I help you?” Patient: “I just vomited some blood; my partner and I panicked and came to the emergency department.”
  • Address the concern: “I understand your concern — you did the right thing coming to hospital. We’ll do a proper, comprehensive assessment, first to work out what’s going on, and we’ll take care of you. You are in safe hands. Are you happy to go through a few questions?”

Explore the complaint (vomiting blood):

  • Clarify the source: was it from your nose or mouth? Any nosebleeds (blood from morning epistaxis can be swallowed and then vomited)? Did you cough up blood? — distinguish epistaxis, haematemesis and haemoptysis.
  • Timing: acute — when exactly did it happen? First time vomiting blood?
  • Character of the blood: fresh/bright red or dark (coffee-ground)? Any clots? Amount — a few drops/streaks or a large amount?
  • Vomiting pattern: how many times did you vomit? A single vomit makes Mallory–Weiss unlikely (it follows recurrent severe vomiting/retching).
  • Symptoms of blood loss (red flags for anaemia/instability): dizziness? Tiredness?

Differentials in the history:

  1. Red flags (must be asked properly to pass). General cancer questions: loss of appetite, loss of weight (if positive: how many kg, over what time, intentional?), lumps and bumps, tiredness. Specific: oesophageal cancer — difficulty or pain on swallowing; gastric cancer/peptic source — abdominal pain, dark stools.
  2. GORD: history of heartburn, acidic/metallic/bitter taste, increased saliva.
  3. PUD: abdominal pain (if positive, explore with SIQORA); risk factors — medications, spicy food, alcohol, smoking, coffee.
  4. Oesophageal varices / chronic liver disease: yellow discolouration of skin, itchiness, dark urine, pale stools, alcohol history.
  5. Bleeding disorders: recent easy bruising, recurrent nosebleeds, menorrhagia in young women; risk factors — family history, anticoagulant medications (specifically apixaban, warfarin), past medical history, family history.

Diagnosis (this recall): with a history of ibuprofen use plus red flags, the first differential is gastric cancer, oesophageal cancer second. “I considered different conditions that can cause blood in your vomit. I found some concerning features that raise the possibility of gastric cancer — a cancer in your stomach.” Reasons: risk factor for stomach ulcer from the painkiller; red flags — loss of weight, loss of appetite, tiredness. Differential order: 2nd PUD; 3rd oesophageal cancer (“in your food pipe” — less likely as there is no dysphagia); 4th oesophagitis (inflammation of the food pipe from GORD); 5th varices (“dilated blood vessels in your food pipe” — less likely with no liver disease); 6th bleeding disorder; 7th Mallory–Weiss tear (“superficial tears in the food pipe triggered by vomiting”).

GORD Counselling: Handbook Scenario

This case comes frequently from the AMC handbook. In the handbook there is ulceration; in the exam there is no ulceration and normally no photo. All the key history points in the handbook feed the management plan — ask only those.

Patient script (from the handbook, cleaned from OCR): you consulted this doctor recently because of worsening heartburn (pain behind the lower breastbone) over recent months. Symptoms are worse in bed at night when recumbent, with occasional regurgitation of bitter fluid into the mouth and bouts of dry coughing. Relief is obtained by drinking a glass of milk or a dose of Mylanta (aluminium hydroxide and magnesium hydroxide). You are otherwise well. The doctor arranged endoscopic examination of your gullet and stomach as a day patient, and a breath test; you have returned to find out about treatment. The specialist found inflammation but nothing more serious. You have mild migraine three or four times a year. You were an active sportsman but not now, though you still go to the football. You should interrupt if there are confusing statements or inappropriate medical jargon. Your concern is to get rid of your symptoms; you have not been concerned about a serious cause until now — display some anxiety if the doctor emphasises the most serious complications (malignant change, oesophageal stricture or rupture). If the doctor’s approach is satisfactory, agree to follow advice about behavioural change; otherwise be resistant to advice about weight, smoking and alcohol.

Key history points: ask about weight (if asking ‘are you overweight?’ may offend, rephrase), hiatus hernia, smoking, alcohol, occasional aspirin use (for migraine — if you offer to discuss migraine alternatives, the patient will say it is not a significant problem; defer it: “I’ll talk about alternatives later; today I want to focus on other things”).

Key counselling facts:

  • Give the non-pharmacological management used for oesophagitis, PUD and GORD; dietary advice — but losing weight is the most important.
  • Oesophagitis is reversible, but if left untreated it can progress towards cancer.
  • Hiatus hernia does not cause oesophagitis, but it can worsen it. Most hiatus hernias cause no symptoms — make clear to the examiner it is not the cause.
  • Do not start a PPI immediately after the diagnostic endoscopy without assessing severity and risk factors.
  • Surgery (fundoplication) can be mentioned but is not an option at this stage; consider it if things worsen despite treatment (severe oesophagitis/GORD) or there is no improvement on follow-up.
  • Red flags for the patient to return: difficulty or pain on swallowing, loss of weight, worsening symptoms, dark stools.
  • The handbook mentions periodic follow-up endoscopy for oesophagitis; see corrections — current Australian practice reserves surveillance endoscopy for Barrett oesophagus.

GORD: Symptoms, Severity and Stepped Management

The main symptom of GORD is heartburn. Atypical symptoms: cough, laryngeal symptoms (hoarseness), dental erosion, sleep disturbance, chest pain.

Lifestyle and dietary advice (weight loss most important): eat smaller meals; eat slowly; avoid lying down after eating; avoid eating or drinking 2–3 hours before bed or vigorous exercise; positional therapy / elevate the head of the bed if nocturnal symptoms; stop smoking.

How to decide treatment intensity — frequency and severity: occasional reflux is mild; daily or severe symptoms (e.g. with cough) is severe GORD.

  • Mild/intermittent: over-the-counter antacids (liquid formulations work best). If inadequate, step up: antacid (e.g. Mylanta) → regular H2-receptor antagonist (famotidine is the agent used in Australia) → PPI at standard dose.
  • Severe symptoms, or severe mucosal disease (oesophagitis, ulceration): start a PPI from the beginning; with oesophagitis plus ulceration use a high-dose PPI (any agent — e.g. omeprazole/esomeprazole).
  • Typical symptoms without alarm features: a trial of PPI first; if it fails, refer for endoscopy. (In this handbook case endoscopy is already done.)

PPI practical points:

  • Take the PPI 30–60 minutes before meals (acid production is triggered by eating). Morning symptoms → before breakfast; evening symptoms → before the evening meal; sleep-related → at night before sleeping.
  • Ask the patient to return if ineffective — treatment is reviewed and stepped up.
  • Duration: without endoscopy and without red flags, give a PPI for 4–8 weeks, then plan maintenance.
  • PPIs are not risk-free — reconsider at every visit and use a step-down approach: titrate to the lowest effective dose; do not stop suddenly (rebound symptoms). To step down: halve the dose; if twice daily, make it once daily or alternate days; then on-demand use only when symptoms occur.
  • Long-term PPI risks: interstitial nephritis, pneumonia, hypomagnesaemia, increased fracture risk in older people, and other GI problems and infections (e.g. enteric infections).

Follow-up: review; if there is no improvement despite optimal therapy, consider fundoplication surgery.

H. pylori Testing and Eradication

  • First-line triple therapy: PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin. In Australia it comes as a single combination pack (Nexium Hp7) used for 7 days. If triple therapy fails, use quadruple therapy (H. pylori can be resistant). After treatment, confirm eradication with a test of cure.
  • If endoscopy has not been done, the urea breath test is the best test; it is also used to confirm eradication.
  • Indications for H. pylori testing: symptoms (e.g. dyspepsia) or concerns; a history of PUD definitely warrants testing, because H. pylori increases the risk of gastric cancer.
  • If H. pylori is positive in the GORD counselling case: manage with PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin, and repeat the urea breath test after treatment.

Explaining Endoscopy Results to the Patient (GORD counselling structure)

Open-ended start: “What’s been happening, Jenny?” — let the patient tell the story; reassure if any concern is expressed. Then: “I have your results with me.”

  1. Explain the endoscopy: “We passed a camera down your food pipe to take a look at your food pipe and your stomach.”
  2. Explain oesophagitis: “We did see some inflammation in your food pipe, which is called oesophagitis.”
  3. Explain the biopsy findings: “Although we found inflammation, there is no ulcer, and we also checked the biopsy samples for pre-cancerous change (Barrett oesophagus) and cancer — none was found.”
  4. Explain hiatus hernia (the one situation where you draw a diagram): “We did see a hiatus hernia — the upper part of your stomach slides up through the muscle dividing the chest and abdomen, called the diaphragm. Let me draw it for you. This is not the cause of the inflammation in your food pipe.”
  5. Explain H. pylori: “We also took samples for a bug called H. pylori, which can cause ulcers and cancers in the stomach. It was negative — you don’t have the bug.”

Focused history (1.5–2 minutes maximum — risk factors, no new complaint to explore): tell me more about your heartburn; how long; what treatments so far (and whether taken at the prescribed dose); risk factors — smoking, alcohol (frequency, amount), takeaway/fatty/spicy food, caffeinated drinks (coffee, tea), carbonated/fizzy drinks, medications (aspirin, NSAIDs, alendronate, doxycycline etc.), overweight, stress. One or two red flags can be asked (loss of appetite, dark stools) but watch the time — the investigations found no cancer.

Diagnosis (keep it short): “You have a condition called gastro-oesophageal reflux disease which has caused oesophagitis — inflammation in your food pipe. There is a gateway (valve) between the food pipe and the stomach, and in your case it is not functioning properly — it is loose — so some acid from your stomach moves up into the food pipe and irritates the inner lining.”

Complications to mention: ulcer in the stomach or food pipe; cancer if untreated; bleeding (especially with ulceration); narrowing (stricture); rupture of the food pipe.

Management (bullet points — no time for long explanations):

  1. Acid suppression: “We’ll start a medication to suppress your acid production — a proton pump inhibitor such as esomeprazole or pantoprazole.” (In the exam, step straight up to a PPI; no need to mention Mylanta/Gaviscon.)
  2. Lifestyle modification: avoid coffee, tea, fizzy drinks, spicy food, tomato products, fatty food, alcohol; stop smoking. Address culprit medications: if on an NSAID, stop it (use paracetamol, with codeine if needed); the patient taking aspirin for migraine should stop it and use paracetamol or a triptan instead. If a patient genuinely needs aspirin and clopidogrel post-MI, do not stop them — continue with a high-dose PPI. Exercise and lose weight.
  3. General advice: small, lighter meals; avoid drinking with meals; do not lie down immediately after eating; raise the head of the bed.
  4. Review/follow-up: arrange review; follow-up endoscopy only where indicated (see corrections — surveillance is for Barrett oesophagus).
  5. Red flags: “If your symptoms get worse, you lose weight, or you notice dark stools, come back to me.”

Reading material for the patient: Better Health Channel (Victorian government website); John Murtagh patient education sheets.

Final reminders: oesophagitis is not caused by the hernia; remember the non-pharmacological management; if H. pylori positive — PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin, then repeat the urea breath test.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The notes state 'In the handbook, it says 2 yearly endoscopy is done in pt with esophagitis with no metaplegia' ('metaplegia' is an OCR error for 'metaplasia'). Routine 2-yearly surveillance endoscopy for reflux oesophagitis without Barrett oesophagus is outdated advice.
Current Australian practice (GESA / Cancer Council Australia Barrett's guidelines, eTG) does not recommend routine surveillance endoscopy for uncomplicated reflux oesophagitis. Surveillance endoscopy is reserved for confirmed Barrett oesophagus (intestinal metaplasia): non-dysplastic Barrett is surveyed approximately every 3-5 years depending on segment length, with shorter intervals only for dysplasia. Repeat endoscopy for oesophagitis is otherwise done to confirm healing of severe (LA grade C/D) oesophagitis or if symptoms fail to respond.
✏️ In the endoscopy-results explanation the notes say: 'we also checked for cancerous changes, but didn't find any from the samples taken by biopsy. It's called barrets.' This conflates a normal biopsy with Barrett oesophagus.
Barrett oesophagus IS the pre-malignant change (intestinal metaplasia replacing the squamous lining). In this recall the biopsies showed NO Barrett oesophagus and no malignancy. The correct patient explanation is: 'We checked the samples for a pre-cancerous change called Barrett oesophagus and for cancer - neither was found.'
✏️ Somac is listed among antacids: 'These are used for reflux: Quickeze, mylanta, somac, gavisol.' Somac is pantoprazole, a proton pump inhibitor, not an antacid; 'gavisol' is a misspelling of Gaviscon (an alginate/antacid). The notes also spell famotidine as 'famotadin' and imply H2RA choice without noting availability.
Classify correctly for the exam: Quick-Eze and Mylanta are antacids, Gaviscon is an alginate/antacid, and Somac (pantoprazole) is a PPI. Famotidine is the H2-receptor antagonist available in Australia - ranitidine was withdrawn from the Australian market by the TGA in 2020 due to NDMA contamination, so do not name ranitidine in a management plan.
