Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (RLQ Abdominal Pain)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / 2.Abdominal pain case (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Case Stem (Online Exam Version)

Your next patient is a 25-year-old lady who presents to you complaining of abdominal pain.

  • Task: Take history (6 minutes)
  • Explain your diagnosis and differentials to the patient

This is a newer case in this cluster (started approximately 2 years ago). The stem gives a young lady with abdominal pain — no further information about duration or location. We must think about history-taking structure and critical differentials from the outset.

Opening Approach: Haemodynamic Stability, Introduction and Consent

Enter, ID check done, sanitise hands, sit down and introduce yourself.

  • Introduction: "Hi, my name is Amir, I'm the doctor for you today."
  • Haemodynamic stability: "Before starting the case, I want to make sure my patient is vitally/haemodynamically stable — can you please tell me the vitals of my patient?" (Examiner may respond: "Patient is stable, proceed." Thank the examiner for confirming stability.)
  • Open-ended question: "How can I help you? From the notes I can see you've been having pain — tell me more about your pain."

Patient's opening statement: "I've had this pain in the right lower side of my stomach. It has been going on for a couple of days and it's getting worse, which made me a little bit worried. I want you to check it out for me."

Acknowledgement and empathy: "Yes, that is stressful. I understand you've been having a tough time these last couple of days. I'm here to help you and I'm really sorry you've been experiencing this. To get a better understanding about the pain, we'll need to go through a few questions — is that okay with you?"

Pain relief: "Are you in any pain now?" → Yes. "Do you want some painkillers?" → Yes. "I'll arrange the nurse to give you some painkillers; in the meantime I'll ask you the questions."

Pain Exploration (SIQORAA)

  • Site: "Can you just reconfirm where the pain is?" → Right lower side of the stomach. Shift (key point): "Where did the pain start?" → "The pain started at the exact same side and is still there." "It didn't start around the belly button or anywhere else?" → No.
  • Intensity: "On a scale of 1–10, 10 being the worst pain you have experienced, how bad is your pain?" → 5/10.
  • Quality: "Is it a dull ache or sharp pain?" → Dull.
  • Onset: "When did the pain actually start?" → 3–5 days ago. "Is it on and off or continuous/constant?" → "It's always there, it just doesn't go away." "Is it getting worse?" → Yes.
  • Radiation: "Any radiation of your pain?" → No, it doesn't travel anywhere.
  • Alleviating/Aggravating factors: "Anything that makes your pain better or worse?" → No. "Any relationship to eating/food?" → No. "Any position that makes it better or worse?" → No.

At this point we know the patient has RLQ pain with no shift. Our differential groups are: 1. GI, 2. KUB (kidney–ureter–bladder), 3. Pelvic — it is very rare to be something else.

Differential Screening 1: GI Questions and Red Flags

  • Any fever? → Yes, feverish (+)
  • Any nausea, vomiting, bloating? → Nausea (+), no vomiting
  • Are you passing wind? Any constipation or diarrhoea?

Red flags (bowel cancer screen):

  • Any loss of weight? → No
  • Any loss of appetite? → No
  • Any lumps and bumps? → No
  • Feeling light-headed/dizzy? → No

Specific questions:

  • Blood in the stool? → No
  • Dark stool? → No
  • Changes in bowel motion? → No
  • Family history of cancers? → No
  • Previous abdominal surgery? → No
  • Regular medications? → No

Differential Screening 2: KUB Group

  • Dysuria: Any pain or burning sensation while passing urine? → No
  • Frequency: Are you passing urine frequently? → No
  • Blood: Any blood in your urine? → No

Differential Screening 3: Pelvic Group (Key Positive Findings)

  • Hernia: Any swelling or lumps in your groin? → No

For a female with lower abdominal pain — gynaecological symptoms:

  • Any vaginal bleeding? → No
  • Any vaginal discharge? → Yes (positive finding — probe further)

Discharge characteristics (Colour, Consistency, Volume, Odour):

  • Colour → yellowish
  • Consistency → a little bit watery, yellow
  • Odour → bad smell (+)

Period history: "When was your last menstrual period?" → 2 weeks ago.

Sexual history:

  • Are you sexually active? → Yes
  • Do you practise safe sex and use condoms? → No
  • Have you ever been diagnosed with an STI? → Yes/No
  • History of multiple partners? → Yes — "I usually have a few partners."

Once risk factors for STI are established, this is enough — the sex of the partners does not change the assessment. With extra time, rule out other differentials.

Other Differentials and Closure (SADMA)

Other DDx questions (if time):

  • Any rashes on your stomach? (shingles — plausible even in a young patient)
  • Any trauma/injury to your stomach?
  • Diabetic ketoacidosis screen (young patient): more thirsty (polydipsia)? → No; passing more urine (polyuria)? → No

Good closure statement (SADMA):

  • Do you smoke? → No
  • Do you drink alcohol? → Occasionally — a glass of wine every week
  • Any recreational drugs? → No
  • Any allergies?
  • Any past medical history or family history? → "No, I've been generally healthy."

Predominant Assessment Area and Key History Findings

This case is designed with a Predominant Assessment Area (PAA) of history taking — there is no PEFE task. Take history for the full 6 minutes; the examiner assesses history-taking skill and then the explanation of diagnosis and differentials. The examiner watches how you respond to the initial statement, and most importantly how you probe further — picking up positive findings and pursuing relevant detail to reach a diagnosis, while ruling out a good number of differentials.

History findings summary:

  • RLQ abdominal pain
  • No shift
  • No anorexia
  • Vaginal discharge: yellowish, offensive
  • Multiple partners; inconsistent condom use (other STI risk factors may be offered)

With all these findings, this is a case of pelvic inflammatory disease (PID).

Explaining the Diagnosis

Diagnosis: "Most likely you have a condition called pelvic inflammatory disease." (Say the full name, not the abbreviation "PID".)

Explanation: "This is an infection in the womb and the tubes, usually caused by a sexually transmitted infection, but not always."

Reasons:

  • Lower abdominal pain
  • Vaginal discharge that is characteristically yellowish and bad-smelling
  • Risk factors for STI: multiple sexual partners, previous STI diagnosis, no condom use
  • Supporting features if elicited: feverish (+), nausea (+/−)

Explaining the Differentials

  1. Appendicitis (top GI differential): "An inflammation in a part of your bowel — not likely because your pain has not shifted; in appendicitis the pain typically shifts from the middle to the right lower side."
  2. Ectopic pregnancy (obstetric): "A pregnancy abnormally formed in the tubes — less likely because your last menstrual period was 2 weeks ago, so it is a little too early."
  3. UTI (KUB): "An infection in your bladder — less likely because you have no pain or burning when passing urine."
  4. Hernia: "A sac in your groin — not likely because you have no groin swelling."

Other differentials on the list (mention if time): GI — bowel cancer, bowel obstruction, IBD, gastroenteritis; KUB — kidney stones, renal colic, urinary retention; O&G — ovarian cyst, ovarian torsion, ovarian cancer; others — diabetic ketoacidosis, shingles, trauma to intra-abdominal organs.

PID: Symptoms and Examination Findings

  • Symptoms: abdominal pain (mostly lower), fever, menorrhagia, vaginal discharge
  • Examination: tenderness and rebound tenderness, decreased bowel sounds, tender uterus and adnexa

Why the bimanual findings occur:

  • Cervical motion tenderness (CMT) positive — cervicitis
  • Tender uterus — endometritis
  • Tender tubes (adnexae) — salpingitis

Symptom considerations: pelvic pain may worsen with movement and intercourse; pain may refer to the right upper quadrant (perihepatitis). Vaginal/cervical discharge may be blood-stained or purulent. Intermenstrual, postcoital and heavy menstrual bleeding may indicate severe infection — but the absence of these symptoms does not exclude a diagnosis of PID. Fever, nausea and vomiting are quite common.

Diagnostic principles: PID has a wide clinical spectrum (may be asymptomatic). Examination is important for an accurate diagnosis (with exclusion of surgical emergencies). Rapid response to appropriate antibiotic treatment is highly predictive of PID. Instrumentation (e.g. IUD insertion, surgical procedures) is a recognised precipitant. The presence of an STI supports the diagnosis; in a substantial proportion of cases no organism is detected. Bimanual examination is necessary to elicit cervical motion tenderness and adnexal or uterine tenderness; pain on bimanual examination is a poor predictor of PID, however the absence of pain makes the diagnosis unlikely. Pelvic ultrasound may be normal or may show thickened, dilated fallopian tubes ± free fluid if the diagnosis is uncertain — transvaginal ultrasound is preferred.

Face-to-Face Exam Version (Long Stem, 19-Year-Old)

Your next patient is a 19-year-old lady who presents complaining of abdominal pain (stem includes: RLQ pain, started 1 week ago, vaginal discharge, bowels opened and normal, not on any medication).

  • Task: Take history for 3 minutes
  • Ask physical examination findings from the examiner — findings given only if asked specifically
  • Explain diagnosis
  • Immediate management

Three minutes of history means: come in and ask key points only — be selective.

Condensed history: confirm haemodynamic stability (BP, PR, RR, temperature) → introduction → open-ended question → address the concern → pain assessment (site already known; ask shift, intensity, quality, course, radiation, aggravating/relieving). About 2 minutes spent; less than 1 minute left for 5 key-point question groups:

  1. Female pelvic symptoms: vaginal bleeding? → No. Discharge characteristics: colour, watery or sticky, bad smelling?
  2. Period history: last menstrual period (needed before prescribing antibiotics and to assess pregnancy risk)
  3. Sexual history: sexually active (+), no condom use (+), multiple partners (+), previous STI ("maybe yes")
  4. PID symptoms: nausea, vomiting, fever, light-headedness
  5. GI red flag (if time): blood in stool?

PEFE (Face-to-Face Version)

  • General appearance: "I want to know the general appearance." → "As you can see."
  • Vital signs: already covered with haemodynamic stability (temperature is essential).
  • Abdominal examination (full structure even in a PID case):
    • Inspection: abdomen moving symmetrically with respiration? → Yes. Cullen sign/Grey Turner sign? → No. Distension? → No.
    • Superficial and deep palpation: tenderness → right lower quadrant tenderness. Rigidity? → No. Guarding? → No. Rebound tenderness? → No.
    • Percussion: tenderness? → Yes/No.
    • Auscultation: bowel sounds present? → Yes.
  • Inguinal area and DRE: inguinal area normal; rectal examination normal.
  • Pelvic examination (with patient consent and a chaperone present):
    • Inspection: rashes, redness, lumps, swelling, discharge or bleeding? → No.
    • Speculum: bleeding/discharge at the cervix? → No.
    • Bimanual: cervical motion tenderness? → Positive. Uterus size → normal. Uterine tenderness → Yes. Adnexal tenderness/mass → right adnexal tenderness.
  • Office tests: urine dipstick → normal; BSL if desired. A urine pregnancy test was not performed given LMP 2 weeks ago (see expert corrections — pregnancy should still be excluded before antibiotics).
  • Appendicitis special tests (Rovsing's/obturator) are low-yield here — they will be negative.

Immediate Management (Face-to-Face Version)

Explain the diagnosis with reasons from history plus PEFE (tender womb and tubes on palpation). Differentials in a few seconds only (appendicitis, ectopic pregnancy, hernia — "not likely in your case") because immediate management is a task.

1. Investigations:

  • Endocervical/vaginal swab for microscopy, culture and sensitivities and chlamydia and gonorrhoea NAAT (PCR)
  • Full STI screen: HIV, syphilis, hepatitis B
  • Bloods (least critical): FBE, ESR, CRP

2. Empirical treatment — do not wait for results; start today (outpatient regimen for mild–moderate PID, per current Therapeutic Guidelines):

  • Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM (in 2 mL of 1% lidocaine) or IV, as a single dose — covers gonorrhoea
  • Doxycycline 100 mg orally, 12-hourly for 14 days — covers chlamydia (chlamydia alone is treated for 1 week, but in PID treatment is 2 weeks)
  • Metronidazole 400 mg orally, 12-hourly for 14 days — covers anaerobes
  • For patients who are pregnant, breastfeeding, or likely to be non-adherent to doxycycline: azithromycin 1 g orally as a single dose, repeated 1 week later (in place of doxycycline)

3. Red flags / safety-netting: "If the pain gets worse, the fever gets worse, or you start getting drowsy, dizzy or vomiting, you must go to the emergency department, because then we need to give you IV antibiotics." Review when the swab results return. Advise safe sexual practice — regular partner, condom use.

Severe PID (inpatient empirical IV therapy, per Therapeutic Guidelines): ceftriaxone IV (higher dosing for septic shock/intensive care) PLUS azithromycin 500 mg IV daily PLUS metronidazole 500 mg IV 12-hourly — see eTG for modification and duration of therapy for severe PID.

Teaching Points

  • Build the basic structure first: good differential list → PEFE structure. All three stems in this cluster involve RLQ pain but the cases differ — the framework is the same.
  • Approach is better than memorising recalls. Cover key points specific to each case as more recalls are practised.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The transcript states doxycycline '100 mg BD (twice a week) x 14 days'. 'BD' means twice daily; 'twice a week' is a transcription error that, if followed, would be grossly sub-therapeutic.
Doxycycline 100 mg orally, 12-hourly (twice daily) for 14 days, per current Therapeutic Guidelines (eTG) for mild-to-moderate PID. The 14-day duration in the notes is correct.
✏️ Metronidazole is described as being given 'to treat normal flora'. This is conceptually wrong — normal flora do not require treatment.
Metronidazole 400 mg orally, 12-hourly for 14 days is included to cover anaerobic organisms (including bacterial vaginosis-associated anaerobes) implicated in polymicrobial PID, per eTG.
✏️ The notes state 'We don't do UPT with 2 weeks LMP' and reassure the patient 'you're not definitely pregnant' based on LMP alone.
Australian practice (eTG, RACGP) requires pregnancy to be excluded in any woman of reproductive age with lower abdominal pain before prescribing doxycycline (Category D) and before excluding ectopic pregnancy. LMP recall is unreliable; a urine β-hCG is quick, cheap and should be performed. In the AMC exam, omitting it is defensible only if the examiner explicitly confirms a recent LMP with regular cycles, but it remains best practice to test.
✏️ Recurrent OCR misspelling 'Gomorrhoea' for gonorrhoea, and 'Sulpingitis' for salpingitis, in the source PDF.
Corrected throughout the structured sections to 'gonorrhoea' and 'salpingitis' (Australian spelling).

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add an explicit reference to the Australian STI Management Guidelines for primary care (sti.guidelines.org.au, Australasian Sexual and Reproductive Health Alliance) for PID diagnosis, contact tracing and test-of-cure — contact tracing/partner notification is a scoring point in AMC counselling variants and is absent from the notes.
⭐ Add a partner management and follow-up paragraph: current sexual partners require testing and empirical treatment; abstain from sex until both patient and partner(s) complete treatment; repeat NAAT (test of re-infection) at 3 months — consistent with RACGP and Australian STI guidelines.
