Unwell After Overseas Travel with IV Drug Use (Hepatitis / HIV / Infective Endocarditis)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med workshop 2 - 2026 / CLASS 5 UNWELL (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: general_practice

Case Overview

A patient returns from a trip feeling progressively unwell and low in energy. The pivotal history finding is overseas heroin use by injection with needle sharing, which reframes the differential towards blood-borne viruses and their complications. The workshop runs three versions with the same opening: Version 1 - acute viral hepatitis (B or C), Version 2 - HIV seroconversion, and Version 3 - infective endocarditis. The correct diagnosis is driven by the examination card and additional positives.

Opening and Responding to Concern

Open-ended start ('How can I help you today?'), then acknowledge the concern. The patient later volunteers guilt and shame ('I feel guilty', 'it was my first time', 'I feel ashamed') - a psychological door that must be opened non-judgementally, allaying guilt and exploring mood, sleep and coping.

Exploring 'Unwell'

  • Clarify: low energy / sleepy / feverish (patient is low in energy, 'can hardly get out of bed')
  • Timing, aggravating and relieving factors, impact on daily life

Travel, Drug and Blood-borne Virus Exposure

  • Travel destination and duration; activities ('partied with friends')
  • Exposure screen (BIC): bushwalking, insect bites, contact with sick people/animals
  • Drug use overseas: heroin, injected, needles shared (major turning point)
  • Other blood-borne exposures: tattoos, piercings, transfusions, blood products
  • Food/water: street food, bottled water
  • Sexual history: never accept 'not currently' - ask 'have you ever been sexually active?', then partners, condom use, safe-sex practices
  • Pre-travel GP visit / vaccinations

Targeted Screening (BBV Complications)

HIV seroconversion: sore throat, rash, fever, chills, appetite loss, weight loss.

Hepatitis: abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, dark urine, pale stool, itchiness.

Infective endocarditis (most important IVDU complication): chest pain, shortness of breath, palpitations/racing heart; injection-site infection (redness, swelling, pain).

Complete with a malignancy screen (weight/appetite loss, night sweats, lumps, family history) and a rapid travel-infection screen (dengue, malaria, TB, gastroenteritis, atypical pneumonia).

Version-Specific Findings and Diagnoses

Version 1 - Hepatitis: abdominal pain, appetite loss, jaundice; exam - mild tender hepatomegaly with RUQ pain, urine dipstick bilirubin positive. Explanation: inflammation of the liver, most likely Hepatitis B or C given needle sharing.

Version 2 - HIV seroconversion: rash, sore throat, tiredness; exam - cervical lymphadenopathy, inflamed throat, aphthous ulcers. Needle sharing plus unsafe sex places HIV first.

Version 3 - Infective endocarditis: chest pain, palpitations; exam - murmur, raised JVP, bibasal crackles, peripheral oedema (features of heart failure). Explanation: infection of the inner lining of the heart following injecting, affecting cardiac function.

Differential Diagnosis

  • Blood-borne viruses: HIV, Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C
  • Cardiac: infective endocarditis and other causes of heart failure
  • Other infections: syphilis, EBV, CMV, toxoplasmosis
  • Travel-related: dengue, malaria, TB, gastroenteritis
  • Psychological: adjustment disorder, PTSD
  • Malignancy: leukaemia, lymphoma

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The examination card records a 'liver span 22cm'.
A liver span of 22 cm is physiologically implausible (normal mid-clavicular span is approximately 6-12 cm; even gross hepatomegaly is usually 15-20 cm). This is likely an OCR/transcription error and should be corrected to a realistic figure (e.g. 'liver edge palpable 4-5 cm below the costal margin, tender') to avoid teaching an inaccurate finding.
✏️ The infective endocarditis version presents left-heart failure signs (bibasal crackles, raised JVP, peripheral oedema, pansystolic murmur) without specifying valve involvement.
In injecting drug users, IE most commonly affects the right-sided (tricuspid) valve and typically produces septic pulmonary emboli (pleuritic chest pain, cough, dyspnoea) with a tricuspid pansystolic murmur, rather than classic left-sided signs. The teaching should flag right-sided IE and Staphylococcus aureus as the usual organism.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add explicit HIV testing and window-period counselling: use a 4th-generation HIV Ag/Ab combination assay, explain that a negative early test may need repeating at 6 weeks, and address confidentiality and consent for testing.
⭐ Add harm-reduction and public-health management: refer to a needle and syringe program, offer Hepatitis B vaccination and Hepatitis A/B/C serology, discuss opioid dependence treatment options, and note contact tracing/partner notification obligations for HIV and hepatitis.
⭐ For the endocarditis version, specify the diagnostic pathway - three sets of blood cultures from separate sites before antibiotics, transthoracic/transoesophageal echocardiography, and application of the modified Duke criteria.
⭐ Apply SOCRATES to the chest pain in the IE version to distinguish pleuritic (septic emboli) from ischaemic pain, and quantify severity.
