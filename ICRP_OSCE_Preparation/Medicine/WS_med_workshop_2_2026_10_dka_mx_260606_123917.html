Diabetic Ketoacidosis - New Type 1 Diabetes (Rural Management and Explanation)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med workshop 2 - 2026 / CLASS 5 UNWELL (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: general_practice

Case Overview

A 17-year-old girl brought to a rural emergency department by her parents, diagnosed with diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) as the first presentation of type 1 diabetes. She had been unwell for two weeks with polydipsia and weight loss, then collapsed on the farm. Tasks: explain the investigations, explain the diagnosis, explain immediate management, and outline long-term management of type 1 diabetes. The rural setting (approximately 400 km / 4 hours from a tertiary centre) is a deliberate design point affecting monitoring and transfer.

Investigations and Interpretation

  • Blood glucose: extremely high (e.g. 35 mmol/L)
  • Blood ketones: elevated (>=3 mmol/L is diagnostic; even >1 mmol/L is relevant)
  • Urine dipstick: ketones present; no leucocytes/nitrites
  • Venous blood gas (VBG): acidotic pH; a separate COPD case uses an ABG
  • Electrolytes: low sodium (dilutional) and a high-normal/high potassium

Biochemical triad for DKA (paediatric/RCH): hyperglycaemia (BGL >11 mmol/L), acidosis (venous pH <7.3 or bicarbonate <15 mmol/L), and ketosis (blood ketones >=3 mmol/L or ketonuria).

Explaining Investigations to the Parent

Sit down, build rapport, and ask the parent to summarise events first ('Can you tell me what has happened so far?'). Address the concern, then explain in a logical sequence:

  • Blood sugar is extremely high
  • When sugar is high the body burns fat for energy, producing ketones - seen in her blood and urine
  • Ketones make the blood acidic, shown on the VBG as a low pH
  • Blood salts checked - sodium low, potassium being monitored closely

Diagnosis

The condition is diabetic ketoacidosis, a complication of diabetes. Her body is not producing enough insulin (the hormone that lowers blood sugar), so the sugar rises very high and the body produces ketones. Reasons for the diagnosis: her symptoms (tiredness, increased urination, fainting) and the investigations. A hyperglycaemic emergency like this confirms a diagnosis of type 1 diabetes.

Immediate Management

  1. Correct dehydration: admit; start IV normal saline first (e.g. 10 mL/kg in children), commenced about one hour before insulin. Fluid alone lowers the glucose to a degree.
  2. Insulin: after the first hour, a rapid-acting insulin infusion (via pump), aiming to lower the glucose gradually to avoid complications; add glucose to the fluids as the BGL falls (aim BGL 5-10 mmol/L).
  3. Potassium: monitor closely - it may be high, normal or low. Add potassium chloride when serum potassium <5.5 mmol/L and the child is passing urine; withhold potassium if anuric or K >5.5 mmol/L. In this case potassium is 6 mmol/L, so potassium is not started initially.
  4. Monitor hourly: BGL, ketones, potassium, renal function, urine output, and for red-flag (cerebral oedema) symptoms.
  5. Identify and treat the precipitant: DKA in children is often triggered by infection - examine, check temperature, and treat any infection.

Complications and Rural Transfer

Cerebral injury/oedema is the key life-threatening complication, usually developing within the first 12 hours; early signs include headache, irritability, lethargy and vomiting after therapy starts, progressing to depressed consciousness and, late, bradycardia and rising blood pressure. If suspected, discuss immediately with a senior. Children have depleted total-body potassium regardless of the initial serum level.

Rural context: per RCH guidance, consult the paediatric team for all children with DKA and all newly diagnosed diabetes, and consider transfer when intensive monitoring is needed (age <2 years, coma, cardiovascular compromise, seizures, signs of cerebral oedema, or severe acidosis with pH <7.1 or HCO3 <5). Because close hourly biochemical monitoring is difficult rurally, stabilise locally then transfer to a tertiary centre.

Long-Term Management of Type 1 Diabetes

Pharmacological: discharge on insulin - multiple daily injections or an insulin pump.

Non-pharmacological: dietitian referral; healthy lifestyle and exercise advice; referral to a diabetes educator (what diabetes is, injection technique, preventing hypoglycaemia); written information; support groups (e.g. Diabetes Australia). Regular follow-up with BGL and HbA1c to adjust insulin. Because type 1 diabetes is autoimmune, screen for associated conditions (coeliac serology, thyroid autoimmunity, diabetes autoantibodies). Provide a sick-day management plan emphasising hydration, closer glucose monitoring, adjusting insulin, and seeking review if vomiting/unable to maintain intake.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The teaching interprets a venous pH below 7.4 as indicating the acidosis of DKA.
Normal venous pH is approximately 7.32-7.38, so a pH just under 7.4 is not diagnostic. The acidosis criterion for DKA is a venous pH <7.3 (with severity graded by pH: mild <7.3, moderate <7.2, severe <7.1). Teach the correct diagnostic threshold to avoid over-calling acidosis.
✏️ One (garbled) line gives the bicarbonate criterion for DKA as <18 mmol/L.
Under paediatric/RCH-ISPAD criteria used for this 17-year-old, the bicarbonate threshold for DKA is <15 mmol/L (venous pH <7.3 or bicarbonate <15 mmol/L). The <18 figure belongs to some adult definitions; state <15 mmol/L for consistency with the paediatric guideline referenced in the case.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ State the fluid strategy explicitly to prevent cerebral oedema: correct the estimated deficit slowly (over about 48 hours), avoid fluid boluses unless shocked, and start insulin only after the first hour of rehydration - and never give an IV insulin bolus in paediatric DKA.
⭐ Add the cerebral oedema treatment pathway: hourly neurological observations, and if suspected, immediate senior involvement plus hyperosmolar therapy (hypertonic saline or mannitol) and airway support - do not wait for imaging.
⭐ Add the precipitant work-up beyond infection: in established diabetes, insulin omission/non-adherence is a common trigger; document a septic screen (FBC, CRP, cultures, urinalysis) and only give antibiotics if infection is found rather than routinely.
⭐ For long-term care, specify the multidisciplinary paediatric diabetes team (paediatric endocrinologist, credentialed diabetes educator, dietitian, psychologist), baseline HbA1c, and structured education including hypoglycaemia recognition and glucagon for severe hypos.
