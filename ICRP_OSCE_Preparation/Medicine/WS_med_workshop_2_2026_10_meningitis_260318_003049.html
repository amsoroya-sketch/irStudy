Meningitis - History, Examination and Emergency Management

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med workshop 2 - 2026 / CLASS 1 HEADACHE (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: neurology

Opening

Confirm haemodynamic stability, use an open-ended question, and respond to the concern efficiently: "I'm concerned about your headache; I'll ask a few quick questions." Keep it focused — this is an acute case.

Pain Characteristics (SIQORAA)

  • Site: all over the head, always bilateral and forehead.
  • Intensity: 7-8/10.
  • Quality: dull.
  • Onset: started this morning, first episode, worsening.
  • Radiation: none.
  • Alleviating/aggravating: painkillers do not help.

A young patient with a sudden first-time headage plus a sexual history prompts the priority differentials: ICH/SAH, meningitis, migraine, brain tumour.

Differential-Directed Questions and Positive Findings

SAH: worst headache ever? sudden onset? — negative. ICH: trauma/head injury. Brain tumour: worse on coughing/sneezing; neurological deficit (weakness, nausea, blurred vision). Infection (positive): fever (yes), sore neck (yes), rash (no), nausea (yes)/vomiting (no), photophobia (yes). Plus dental infection, migraine, tension headache.

Physical Examination

  • General appearance: level of consciousness, rash.
  • Vital signs: temperature 38.5 °C, plus BP, pulse and respiratory rate.
  • Neurological: cranial nerve examination normal, fundoscopy normal, upper/lower limb examination normal.
  • Special/ENT: neck soreness positive, Kernig sign positive, Brudzinski sign positive.
  • Cervical spine: tenderness on palpation; assess movement.

Diagnosis and Explanation

Most likely diagnosis: meningitis — infection of the covering of the brain. Reasons: fever, nausea, photophobia and a sore/stiff neck. The meningitis headache is usually generalised, radiates to the neck, is constant and severe (occasionally abrupt in onset) and is aggravated by neck flexion, with positive Brudzinski and Kernig signs and fever plus neck stiffness. Differentials: brain tumour, other infection, ICH/SAH, migraine, tension headache, cluster headache.

Emergency Management

Call 000, transfer to the ED, admit. Investigations: CT brain, then lumbar puncture (CSF sample). CSF microscopy and culture are key to diagnosis and directed therapy; CSF lactate of 3.5 mmol/L or more suggests bacterial meningitis; Gram stain, pneumococcal antigen and PCR (for N. meningitidis and S. pneumoniae) assist, especially after antibiotics. Do not withhold treatment for a delay in investigations. Treatment: IV antibiotics, analgesia, fluids and steroids. In the community with suspected meningococcaemia (fever plus purpuric rash / sepsis with meningeal signs), give an immediate dose of ceftriaxone 2 g (child 1 month or older: 50 mg/kg up to 2 g) IV or IM, or benzylpenicillin 2.4 g (child: 60 mg/kg up to 2.4 g) IV or IM, ideally after blood cultures/lesion swabs but without delaying treatment. Consider a medical certificate.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The management line 'IV Abx, Painkillers, Fluid, Steroids' lists steroids without timing, and antibiotics generically.
Per Therapeutic Guidelines, empirical therapy for suspected bacterial meningitis in adults is ceftriaxone 2 g IV 12-hourly (add benzylpenicillin/amoxicillin if Listeria risk - age >50, immunocompromised, pregnant), with dexamethasone 10 mg IV given just before or with the first antibiotic dose (not after) to reduce neurological sequelae in pneumococcal meningitis. Do not delay antibiotics for CT or LP.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add the meningococcal public-health steps: this is a notifiable disease - notify the public health unit, arrange contact tracing and chemoprophylaxis (e.g. ciprofloxacin/rifampicin) for close contacts.
⭐ Include the caveat that Kernig and Brudzinski signs have low sensitivity in adults; their absence does not exclude meningitis, and non-blanching purpuric rash mandates immediate parenteral benzylpenicillin/ceftriaxone before transfer.
⭐ State CT-before-LP indications (reduced GCS, focal deficit, papilloedema, immunocompromise, seizure) and note LP is contraindicated if raised ICP is suspected.
⭐ For a rural district hospital (Young), emphasise give first-dose IV/IM antibiotics locally, call 000/retrieval, and transfer - do not wait for tertiary review to treat.
