Liver Metastasis - Breaking Bad News (SPIKES) & Palliative Management

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / class 5 liver cases (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Stem & Tasks

Your next patient is a 65-year-old lady complaining of dyspepsia and back pain. Investigations show microcytic hypochromic anaemia (low Hb, low MCV, low MCH) - suggesting iron-deficiency anaemia. A CT scan is provided.

Tasks: explain the investigation results to the patient; request further investigations.

Most common sites of colon cancer metastasis: liver, lungs, lymph nodes, peritoneum.

Explaining the Results

Introduce, then open question: "Jenny, I can see you've done a scan and blood tests. Can you tell me why you did them / what has happened so far?" → patient gives the complaint.

Blood test: "We did a Full Blood Count. We checked your red blood cells, which carry oxygen using haemoglobin. You have a low amount of haemoglobin, smaller red cells, and less haemoglobin in each cell than expected."

CT scan: "We've done a scan of your abdomen and checked your liver and gallbladder." Pick up the image: "I can see a number of circular lesions in your liver, which is abnormal and suggests some lumps/growths."

SPIKES - Breaking the News

  1. Warning phase: "Unfortunately, I don't have good news today."
  2. Offer support: "Would you like me to call anyone to be with you?"
  3. Drop the bomb: "We usually see this picture when a cancer has spread to your liver - we call this metastasis."
  4. Empathy: "I'm really sorry I had to give you this news today. I wish I had better news. The results are not what we were hoping for." Offer tissue, water and silence. "I'm here to support you. You're in safe hands. We can still provide effective treatments. How are you feeling now?"

Explaining the Diagnosis & Investigations

"Based on your dyspepsia and the iron-deficiency anaemia - meaning you may be bleeding from your stomach or bowel - I'm thinking about a bowel cancer or gastric cancer that has spread to your liver. I'm also concerned that your back pain may reflect spread to the bones and spine."

Investigations:

  1. Endoscopy and colonoscopy (camera via mouth and back passage; biopsy any ulcer/mass)
  2. PET scan / bone scan for other sites of spread
  3. Liver lesion biopsy if the source is uncertain
  4. MRI of the liver

Second Encounter - 'What is metastasis?' & Palliative Plan

Introduce, open question: "I went through your files and believe you had a tough day yesterday. Can you tell me more / how are you feeling today?" → "Metastasis means a cancer has spread to another part of the body, in your case most likely from the bowel cancer." Offer support, emotional check, empathy ("I understand this may be shocking news"), tissue/water/silence, and reassurance.

Focused colon-cancer history: when diagnosed; treatment; regular GP/specialist follow-up; complications (general cancer screen - LOA, weight loss, lumps/bumps, tiredness; other - cough, bone pain; liver metastasis - jaundice, itch, dark urine/pale stool); family/social support (end-of-life, stage IV).

Management:

  1. MDT approach - GP, oncologist, surgeon, psychologist, cancer nurse.
  2. Confirm the source - biopsy the lesion; special MRI as needed.
  3. Palliative care for symptom control - analgesia, antiemetics, chemotherapy/radiotherapy or surgery to control symptoms.
  4. Advance Care Directive and appointing a medical decision-maker while capacity is retained.
  5. Refer to Cancer Council / cancer nurses for support.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ IDA is inferred from the FBC and the patient is moved straight to breaking bad news of metastatic cancer.
Iron-deficiency should be confirmed with iron studies (ferritin, transferrin saturation) before it is used to build the malignancy argument; and although the metastatic picture is strong, the definitive cancer diagnosis is not yet histologically confirmed, so language should acknowledge that biopsy/endoscopy will confirm it. New IDA in a person over 50 nonetheless mandates urgent GI investigation.
✏️ The palliative plan lists chemotherapy, radiotherapy and surgery generically to 'control symptoms'.
Per eviQ/Australian oncology practice, systemic therapy for metastatic colorectal cancer is biomarker-directed (RAS/BRAF, MSI) and an oligometastatic liver deposit may be resectable with curative intent; radiotherapy has little role in colon (as opposed to rectal) primaries. The plan should be MDT-directed rather than framed as uniformly palliative.
✏️ SPIKES 'Perception' and 'Invitation' steps are not explicit before delivering the news.
Add explicit Perception ('What is your understanding of the tests so far?') and Invitation ('How much detail would you like?') before knowledge delivery, in line with the current Australian communication standard.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add can't-miss red flags: back pain in metastatic cancer requires screening for spinal cord/cauda equina compression (leg weakness, saddle anaesthesia, bladder/bowel dysfunction) - an oncological emergency needing urgent MRI and specialist referral.
⭐ Add a Strategy/Summary and safety-net step to complete SPIKES: written information, a contact number, a follow-up appointment, offer of support person/interpreter, and chaplaincy/psychology referral.
⭐ Specify the Australian palliative/analgesia ladder (regular paracetamol +/- opioids per eTG palliative care) rather than a generic 'painkillers', and add antiemetic choice appropriate to cause.
