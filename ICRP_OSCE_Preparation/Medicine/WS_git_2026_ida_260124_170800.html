Iron Deficiency Anaemia: Investigations, Sinister Causes and Management

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / class 5 liver cases (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Stem and Investigation Summary

A 62-year-old woman presented to the practice 2 days ago and saw a colleague for tiredness. She is a non-smoker, drinks alcohol rarely, and her last menstrual period was about 2 years ago (later stated ~1 year ago). On examination she was pale, with the rest of the examination normal.

Investigations: low haemoglobin, low MCV, low MCH, low MCHC and low ferritin, with anisocytosis and poikilocytosis - a microcytic hypochromic picture confirming iron deficiency anaemia (IDA).

Task: explain the investigations to the patient; take a focused further history; explain diagnosis and differentials; explain a management plan.

Explaining the Investigations (Plain Language)

  • Red cells carry oxygen using haemoglobin (Hb); your Hb is low.
  • MCV - the size of your red cells - is reduced (small cells).
  • MCH/MCHC - the amount of Hb in each cell - is reduced (pale cells).
  • Anisocytosis - your red cells are of differing sizes.
  • Poikilocytosis - your red cells are abnormally shaped (not the normal donut shape).
  • Ferritin - your iron store - is low, confirming depleted iron.

Together these confirm iron deficiency anaemia.

Focused History - Causes of IDA

Because IDA is already established, the history targets its cause rather than a generic tiredness screen.

1. Decreased intake: diet quality, red-meat intake, vegetarian diet.

2. Blood loss:

  • Recent surgery or blood donation
  • Menstrual: LMP, prior heavy periods
  • GI blood loss: PUD (abdominal pain, vomiting, NSAID/ibuprofen use), gastric or bowel cancer

PUD / GI malignancy screen: abdominal pain, vomiting, medications (NSAIDs); general cancer questions (weight loss - positive, 5 kg over a few months; appetite loss; lumps); specific questions - dark (melaena) stools (positive), blood in stool, change in bowel habit, family history of bowel cancer, and (age over 50) bowel cancer screening status.

3. Malabsorption: chronic diarrhoea/loose stools; coeliac disease (greasy stools that float/stick, hard to flush); IBD (fresh blood in stool); extra-intestinal features (joint pain, blurred vision, rashes, mouth ulcers).

Positive findings: weight loss 5 kg and melaena.

Diagnosis, Differentials and Management

Diagnosis: IDA with concern for an underlying gastric or bowel malignancy causing occult bleeding, given unintentional weight loss and melaena. Dark (rather than fresh) stools suggest a more proximal (upper GI) source, so gastric cancer is slightly favoured, though both remain on the list. IDA is always a red flag when accompanied by chronic abdominal pain or blood in the stool.

Differentials for IDA: reduced intake (vegetarian/low red meat); increased loss (malignancy, surgery, heavy periods, PUD); malabsorption (coeliac disease, IBD).

Management:

  • First and foremost: gastroscopy and colonoscopy to identify the bleeding source, with biopsy of any ulcer/lesion; refer to a gastroenterologist/GI specialist.
  • Treat the underlying cause once identified.
  • Replace iron (oral iron, or IV iron infusion), then follow up ferritin and Hb to confirm response.

Teaching point: prioritising iron supplementation over endoscopy/colonoscopy is a failing approach in this age group; identifying the source comes first.

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: history-taking-expert (Australian clinical supervisor) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The work-up jumps from FBC to endoscopy without confirming iron studies or excluding coeliac disease serologically.
Confirm iron deficiency with full iron studies (ferritin, transferrin saturation, serum iron/TIBC) - ferritin can be falsely normal if there is coexisting inflammation. In an adult with IDA, also request coeliac serology (tissue transglutaminase IgA with total IgA) per Australian guidance before or alongside endoscopy.
✏️ IV iron is offered generically without current Australian product/eligibility detail.
In Australia, ferric carboxymaltose (e.g. Ferinject) is the usual single-dose IV iron and is PBS-subsidised for eligible patients (including where oral iron is not tolerated or ineffective). Oral iron is first-line where tolerated; advise alternate-day dosing to improve absorption and reduce side effects.
✏️ A minor internal inconsistency in the LMP (stated as both 2 years and 1 year ago).
This is a transcription inconsistency; the clinically relevant point is that the patient is postmenopausal, so a gynaecological source of blood loss is unlikely and GI evaluation is mandatory. Clarify LMP in the stem.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add the explicit Australian red-flag rule: IDA in a postmenopausal woman or any man is a GI malignancy until proven otherwise and mandates urgent (two-week) upper and lower endoscopy - reinforce this as the key learning point.
⭐ Add NSAID counselling: if ibuprofen is contributing to PUD/GI bleeding, stop the NSAID, consider a PPI, and review analgesia (paracetamol-based) - relevant given AMC's fondness for the NSAID-induced ulcer twist.
⭐ Add dietary and follow-up advice: iron-rich diet counselling, recheck Hb at 2-4 weeks and ferritin at 3 months, and continue iron for ~3 months after Hb normalises to replenish stores.
⭐ Structure improvement: add a brief blood-film/indices interpretation box (microcytic hypochromic; differentiate from thalassaemia trait using Mentzer index and haemoglobinopathy screen if ferritin is normal).
⭐ Add metadata linking to bowel-cancer screening (National Bowel Cancer Screening Program) and coeliac-disease pathways.
