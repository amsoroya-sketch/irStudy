Epigastric Pain (Acute Pancreatitis)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / 2.Abdominal pain case (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Case Stem (2025 — Most Common Acute Abdomen Case of 2025)

Your next patient is a 25-year-old man who presents to the emergency department complaining of upper abdominal pain.

  • Vitals: BP 100/70, PR 92, SpO₂ 97%, Temp 37.3 °C
  • Task: Take history for 5 minutes
  • Explain your diagnosis and differentials
  • Request investigations

The vitals are in the stem, so do not ask about haemodynamic stability again — the patient is stable. In an upper abdominal pain case, do the entire upper-abdomen differential pack regardless of whether the pain is left, right or midline.

Non-Verbal Clues

  • On entering, the role-player is holding a vomit bag → prompt the questions: "Do you have nausea?" → Yes. "Have you vomited?" → Yes.
  • The patient is sitting leaning forward in a stooped posture throughout the history — patients with pancreatitis are more comfortable leaning forward. Leaning forward + upper abdominal pain → think pancreatitis.

Opening the Consultation

  • Introduction: "Hi, my name is Amir."
  • Open-ended question: "How can I help you?"
  • The patient complains, is distressed and worried. Address the concern — not only in words but in tone of voice and body language: reassure that you will ask a few questions to work out what is happening.

Pain Assessment (SIQORAA)

  • Site: "Can you reconfirm where exactly you have the pain?" (patients usually point) → epigastric area.
  • Intensity: 5/10.
  • Quality: sharp or dull (either answer — not discriminating here). In the 2025 recall the pain was described as stabbing.
  • Onset/time: a couple of hours ago. First episode? — especially important in upper abdominal pain, because gallbladder disease may present as long-standing biliary colic before suddenly becoming cholecystitis → "Yes, first time." Constant, getting worse.
  • ** Radiation: "Is your pain radiating anywhere?" → Yes — to the back. (In 2025 the patient also said "to my chest" — if ACS and pneumonia are then not ruled out, the candidate is in trouble.)
  • Alleviating/aggravating: nothing helps. "Is there any position that makes your pain better — does it get better when you lean forward? I can see you're leaning forward." → "Yes, the pain gets better when I lean forward and worse when I lie down." Food-related? → No.

Working diagnosis: acute pancreatitis.

Differential Framework for Upper Abdominal Pain

  • GI general questions
  • Red flags
  • Pancreatitis (and its causes)
  • Liver and gallbladder
  • PUD / GORD
  • ACS
  • Pneumonia (lower lobe)

GI General Questions and Red Flags

  • Nausea? → Yes (+++)
  • Vomited? → Yes (+++) — probe: how many times (3/5/10)? and for a patient vomiting non-stop, the key question: "Is it green or not?" (looking for bilious vomiting/obstruction)
  • Bloating, passing wind, diarrhoea, constipation, fever — screen each (diarrhoea/constipation/fever also serve to rule out infective causes)

Red flags (cancer questions): weight loss; appetite loss; lumps and bumps; tired/dizzy (acute → dizzy; chronic → tired).

Specific questions: fresh blood in stool (cancer; obstruction with necrosis); dark stool (proximal GI bleeding); recent bowel-habit change; family history of bowel cancer.

Pancreatitis Cause Questions

  • Alcohol: "Do you drink alcohol?" (a "never" ends this line). New-case answer: "Sometimes/rarely/occasionally." → "Any recent episode of heavy/binge drinking?" → "Now that you say it — I was drinking last night." → "How much?" → 5–6 shots (tequila/whisky — a large binge). (Older versions: 2018 — "Yes, 5–6 glasses last night"; 2019 — "rarely, but binge last night".)
  • Trauma: any recent injury to the abdomen?
  • Lipids: "Any history of high fats in your blood?" → "Yes, a doctor told me I have high fat in the blood."
  • Medications: regular medications? (several drugs cause pancreatitis — e.g. diuretics, tetracyclines)
  • Previous surgery?

Cancer and infection have effectively been ruled out through the earlier questions (ask fever/cancer questions now if not already done).

Aetiology reminders: past history of alcohol excess is important; gallstones and gallbladder disease must be ruled out; infection (e.g. mumps) can cause pancreatitis; hyperlipidaemia — specifically high triglycerides — can cause pancreatitis; some medications cause pancreatitis.

Liver, Gallbladder, GORD/PUD, ACS and Pneumonia Questions

Liver questions: yellowish discolouration of the skin (jaundice)? dark urine or pale stools? travel history?

Gallbladder questions: previous episodes of pain after large fatty meals? (screening for gallstones)

GORD/PUD questions:

  • Heartburn (screening, non-specific): burning sensation in the chest?
  • Bitter/acidic (metallic) taste in the mouth?
  • Water brash — sudden increase of saliva in the mouth (acid-triggered salivation in the oesophagus)?
  • Risk factors (shared with red flags/PUD; alcohol and medications already covered): caffeine/tea? spicy food?

ACS and lower-lobe pneumonia questions: any chest pain? shortness of breath? cough?

History Findings Summary

Older version findings: epigastric pain; better leaning forward, worse lying down; vomiting a few times; nausea (+); "drinks alcohol, not that often"; binge alcohol last night.

2025 findings: epigastric pain; stabbing; radiating to the back; history of hypercholesterolaemia (see expert corrections — the pancreatitis-causing lipid abnormality is hypertriglyceridaemia); pain better on leaning forward; binge drinking last night.

Acute Pancreatitis Reference Notes

Clinical features: sudden (may be steady) onset of severe epigastric pain lasting hours to a day or so; nausea and vomiting; sweating and weakness. Diagnostic pattern: severe pain + nausea and vomiting + relative lack of abdominal signs → acute pancreatitis. There may be a history of previous attacks or of gallstones/alcohol.

Signs: the patient is weak, pale, sweating and anxious; relative lack of guarding, rigidity or rebound; bowel sounds may be reduced/absent if ileus; ± abdominal distension; fever, tachycardia, ± shock (septic features may occur). Nothing highly specific — mostly the general findings of an acute abdomen.

Diagnosis/investigations: serum lipase — the most sensitive and specific initial test, preferred over amylase; CRP elevated; glucose and calcium; blood gases (PaO₂ — pulmonary complications); LFTs (obstructive pattern?); plain X-ray (sentinel loop); CT scan (especially for complications); ultrasound better for detecting cysts and unsuspected gallstones.

Explaining the Diagnosis

Diagnosis: "You have a condition called acute pancreatitis."

Explanation: "The pancreas is an organ in your upper tummy that helps digest food. In your case the pancreas is inflamed — most likely due to the binge drinking last night."

Reasons:

  • Site — the middle upper abdomen is where the pancreas sits
  • Character — radiation to the back; better on leaning forward
  • Severe nausea and vomiting (+++)
  • Risk factors — alcohol binge; high blood fats (add any other elicited risk factors)

Explaining the Differentials

Anatomical sweep of the upper abdomen:

  • Pancreas: pancreatitis; pancreatic cancer
  • Right side (liver/GB): hepatitis, cholecystitis, cholangitis, hepatocellular carcinoma
  • Left side/stomach: GORD, PUD
  • Above the diaphragm: IHD/ACS, lower-lobe pneumonia

Patient-facing wording: "I was also thinking about a possible cancer in the pancreas; problems in your liver and gallbladder — hepatitis or cholecystitis, which are inflammation of the liver and gallbladder — but you have no yellow discolouration of the skin and no previous episodes of pain; reflux or a peptic ulcer irritating the stomach or food pipe — not likely because you don't have those features; and I wanted to make sure there is no heart problem or lung infection — but you have no cough, no shortness of breath and no fever, so those are unlikely."

Requesting Investigations

  1. Serum lipase — the first, most sensitive and most specific test (better than amylase)
  2. LFTs
  3. Other bloods: FBE, ESR, CRP, VBG, UEC, BSL, lipid profile, serum calcium
  4. Imaging: ultrasound first — to assess the pancreas and, importantly, the gallbladder (gallstone aetiology). Further specialised imaging (e.g. CT) as indicated — but if one imaging test is named, name the ultrasound.

Very Old Version (Results-Based Stem)

Your next patient is a 25-year-old lady presenting to the ED with upper abdominal pain. Investigations already done:

  • WBC 10,000 (raised)
  • Lipase 1200 — high
  • ECG normal
  • UEC normal
  • LFTs: mildly raised AST and ALT (up to ~2×)

Tasks: history taking (same structure + pancreatitis key points and risk factors); explain the results (lipase, WBC, liver enzymes); explain the diagnosis — pancreatitis. (Covered in detail in the companion case "Pancreatitis version 2".)

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: history-taking-expert (Australian clinical supervisor) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The 2025 recall stem cites 'history of hypercholesterolemia' as the lipid risk factor for pancreatitis, and the patient explanation uses 'high fats in your blood' loosely.
The lipid abnormality that causes acute pancreatitis is severe hypertriglyceridaemia (typically >11.3 mmol/L), not hypercholesterolaemia per se — the source notes themselves clarify '(High TG)'. When requesting a lipid profile, candidates should state they are specifically looking for triglycerides, reported in mmol/L.
✏️ US spelling 'hypercholesterolemia' and OCR artifacts (e.g. 'GORD' rendered as 'GoRD/GORD' inconsistently, garbled handwritten shorthand) in the source.
Australian spelling used throughout the structured sections (hypercholesterolaemia, hyperlipidaemia, oesophagus); non-clinical OCR noise removed without altering clinical content.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add the biochemical diagnostic criterion explicitly: lipase (or amylase) greater than 3 times the upper limit of normal supports the diagnosis of acute pancreatitis — this converts 'lipase is the best test' into an examinable threshold and matches the companion results-based station (lipase 1200).
⭐ Add a severity-assessment line (per Australian ED practice): acute pancreatitis requires admission, IV fluid resuscitation, analgesia, antiemetics and early severity stratification; a note that ALT >3x the upper limit of normal strongly suggests a gallstone aetiology and mandates early biliary ultrasound.
⭐ Add an alcohol brief-intervention hook for the binge-drinking aetiology: quantify in standard drinks and reference the NHMRC guideline (no more than 10 standard drinks/week and no more than 4 on any one day), with brief advice and follow-up — a frequent AMC add-on task in ED alcohol-related presentations.
⭐ Add the I GET SMASHED mnemonic (idiopathic, gallstones, ethanol, trauma, steroids, mumps, autoimmune, scorpion sting, hypertriglyceridaemia/hypercalcaemia, ERCP, drugs) as revision metadata for the aetiology questions the station tests.
⭐ Cross-link the non-verbal-clues section (vomit bag, leaning-forward posture) to the irStudy OSCE communication module — this station is the clearest example in the bundle of examiners rewarding observation of the role-player.
