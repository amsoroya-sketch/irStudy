Traumatic Abdominal Pain — 16-Year-Old Boy after Bicycle Fall (LUQ Injury)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / 2.Abdominal pain case (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Station Stem and Tasks

Left upper quadrant pain — a very famous site for physical examination in both the online and face-to-face exams. The management task points toward the immediate and urgent investigations to run in the ED.

Your next patient is a 16-year-old boy who has presented to the ED after he fell from his bike. He is complaining of pain in his left upper quadrant.

  • Task 1: Take history for 5 minutes.
  • Task 2: Explain your diagnosis and differentials to the patient.
  • Task 3: Explain your further management.

This is not a new case — it is quite famous. In the face-to-face exam version there is blood in the urine, so it plays as a kidney injury; the online version leans toward a splenic injury.

History: Opening

  • Haemodynamic stability — mandatory in any traumatic abdominal patient, even without abdominal pain.
  • Open-ended question: the patient answers, "Yes, I just had a fall. I was riding my bike, I nearly hit a car, I tried to steer away, I fell, and I've got pain in my stomach."
  • Address the concern (be kind to the teenager): "I understand this can be concerning — you're stressed and you're scared. Let me ask you a few questions; we'll do some investigations, I'll find out what's happening and make sure everything is okay."

Pain Assessment (SIQORAA, Adjusted to Context)

The complaint is still abdominal pain, so assess it — adjusting the questions to a hyperacute setting:

  • Site: where exactly is the pain?
  • Intensity: rate the pain.
  • Quality.
  • Onset: the fall just happened, so "since when?" is not useful; instead ask, "Is the pain on and off or constant? Is it gradually getting worse?"
  • Radiation: does the pain radiate anywhere?
  • Aggravating/alleviating factors: food and bowel motion are unlikely to be relevant here, but position is: "Have you noticed any position that makes your pain better or worse?"

Explore the Incident (Key Points)

  • "Can you describe the fall? Was it a direct blow to your stomach, or did you land on your side or chest wall? Did you hit the handlebars? What happened exactly?"
  • "Did you injure any other part of your body?" (e.g. the classic patient who fell and also has wrist pain)
  • Head injury: "Did you hit your head anywhere?" — if there is a head injury, that takes priority over the abdomen.
  • Red flag: "Did you lose consciousness?"

System-by-System Injury Screen

Concept: in trauma we are ruling out injuries, not diseases — we are not looking for cancer, IBD or UTI. The high-risk solid organs in the LUQ region are the spleen and kidney; also screen GI, respiratory and cardiovascular. General questions can be skipped in this case if time is short.

  1. Spleen (laceration/rupture): "Are you feeling dizzy?" (a dizzy patient matters — possible active bleeding); loss of consciousness already asked; "Has anyone told you that you look pale?" (screening for the pale, bleeding patient).
  2. GI: "Any nausea? Have you vomited any blood? Any blood in your stools?" (colonic injury can give fresh blood; melaena would not have had time to develop within an hour).
  3. KUB: "Any blood in your urine? Are you passing any clots? Any difficulty passing urine?" (the exact site of impact is still unknown).
  4. Respiratory (rib fracture, lung contusion, pneumothorax): "Any shortness of breath right now? Any cough? Any difficulty breathing?"
  5. CVS (upper-left chest trauma — think cardiac tamponade): "Any chest pain?" (also occurs with rib fracture); "Is your heart racing?" (a bleeding patient will be tachycardic; a cardiac problem may present as palpitations).
  6. Past medical history: "Any past medical history I should be aware of?"
  7. Medications: "Are you taking any medications regularly?" — specifically screening for anticoagulants such as warfarin.

Interpreting the Findings

  • If the patient reports blood in the urine → concern for kidney injury (this is the finding in the face-to-face PE version of the case).
  • If the patient is dizzy, lost consciousness and has abdominal pain → the picture is undifferentiated; suspect significant intra-abdominal injury.
  • If nothing positive is found, wrap up with an honest summary of the concerns (below).

Explaining Diagnosis and Differentials to the Patient

"In your case, I'm concerned about an injury to your tummy. The two main organs I'm worried about are your kidney and your spleen — these are solid organs at higher risk."

Differentials:

  • Rib fractures and lung injury (lung contusion)
  • Injury to the stomach and bowels — rare, but possible with significant trauma
  • Soft tissue injury
  • Cardiac injury, including a condition called tamponade

Management in the ED

With no specific diagnosis yet, management is investigation-led:

  1. Imaging:
    • Chest X-ray — for the lungs and ribs.
    • FAST (Focused Assessment with Sonography in Trauma) — a rapid bedside ultrasound of the abdomen to identify free fluid/possible injury to the organs discussed.
    • CT scan — if any abnormality is found on FAST (or clinical concern persists), proceed to CT.
  2. Bloods:
    • Full blood examination (FBE) — to help assess bleeding.
    • Blood group and cross-match — prepare blood for the patient.
  3. Urine dipstick: looking for blood, to screen for injury to the kidneys or urinary system.

Disposition: admit and observe closely with regular vital signs; give analgesia and consider IV fluids. Further management depends on findings. If a significant injury is identified — e.g. splenic laceration/rupture or major kidney injury with massive bleeding — proceed to urgent surgery (see expert review regarding non-operative management of stable patients).

✏️ Corrections

✏️ FAST is expanded in the source as 'Focused Assessment Sono for Trauma'.
The correct expansion is 'Focused Assessment with Sonography in Trauma' (FAST). In significant blunt chest/abdominal trauma, eFAST (extended FAST, adding lung views for pneumothorax/haemothorax) is now standard in Australian EDs.
✏️ The notes state that any splenic laceration/rupture or kidney injury with bleeding means 'we need to do urgent surgery'.
Current Australian and international trauma practice manages most haemodynamically STABLE splenic and renal injuries non-operatively (observation, serial examination, +/- angioembolisation), graded by CT (AAST grading). Urgent laparotomy is reserved for haemodynamic instability or ongoing bleeding despite resuscitation. Non-operative splenic preservation is particularly important in a 16-year-old to avoid overwhelming post-splenectomy infection; if splenectomy is required, post-splenectomy vaccination (pneumococcal, meningococcal, Hib) and antibiotic prophylaxis are mandatory.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add referred left shoulder-tip pain (Kehr's sign) to the splenic injury screen — a high-yield, examiner-expected question for splenic rupture with diaphragmatic irritation that is missing from the current question set.
⭐ Add a primary-survey framing sentence for the management task: in an Australian ED this patient is approached with ABCDE/EMST (Early Management of Severe Trauma) principles, with trauma-call activation criteria; history taking runs in parallel with resuscitation, not before it.
⭐ For the head-injury limb of the history, reference structured observation: conscious state and neurological observation charting (GCS) per standard Australian head-injury monitoring, and note that loss of consciousness or vomiting after head strike triggers a CT-brain decision pathway.
⭐ Add an adolescent-specific note: for a 16-year-old, consent/parental involvement, HEADSS context if relevant, and mandatory consideration of non-accidental injury if the mechanism is inconsistent — plus tetanus status check for any abrasions.
⭐ Metadata addition: tag the two exam variants explicitly (face-to-face variant = haematuria/renal injury with PE focus; online variant = splenic injury with history + management focus) so the platform can surface the right variant by exam format.
