Telehealth Consultation: Repeated Vomiting in a 60-Year-Old Man (Bowel Obstruction / Acute Kidney Injury)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / CLASS 4 MID GI CASE (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Case Stem and Station Format

This is a telehealth (telephone) station. A 60-year-old man has booked a telephone appointment with you at the general practice. He has called requesting a script for vomiting. Your tasks: take a history from the patient (approximately six minutes), then explain your management plan (a diagnosis is implied within the management even if not a separate task).

Station logistics: when you enter the room the examiner ID-checks you (hand sanitising may or may not be requested), then leaves and directs you to a telephone in the room. You pick up the telephone and the patient is on the line. Several AMC stations use this format (an ophthalmology case, some obstetrics nurse-call cases, and an ISBAR handover case), so do not be surprised to find an empty room with a telephone.

Differential Diagnosis: Repeated Vomiting in an Older Man

Additional context: he has not passed stools for three days, has not passed urine for two days, and is on metformin and blood pressure medication. The case has been recalled as bowel obstruction, but build a full differential list:

  • GI group (top of the list):
    • Bowel obstruction — causes include tumours, hernias, adhesion bands, and twisting (volvulus). Red-flag questions for malignancy still apply here.
    • Gastroenteritis (profuse vomiting).
    • Pancreatitis, cholecystitis.
  • Infections: pyelonephritis, hepatitis, even reflux-associated vomiting; otitis media (a commonly missed cause of vomiting in adults as well as children); COVID-19, influenza, EBV and other viral illnesses.
  • Neurological: meningitis (important to rule out), brain tumour / raised intracranial pressure; consider intracranial haemorrhage after a fall (e.g. a fall six hours earlier causing an intracranial bleed presenting with vomiting).
  • Cardiovascular: the patient is diabetic — a strong cardiovascular risk factor — so consider myocardial infarction and ask specifically about chest pain.
  • Metabolic: diabetic ketoacidosis; uraemia / acute kidney injury (AKI is the formal term). Note the classic AMC electrolyte scenario: a patient on perindopril (ACE inhibitor) plus a thiazide who then takes an NSAID — the triple whammy — developing AKI and hyperkalaemia fits this presentation pattern well. AMC has previously used two hyperkalaemia cases: one where the GP increased the ACE inhibitor dose, and one triple-whammy case.

Opening the Call: Identity Check and Rapport

Pick up the phone and introduce yourself: "Hi, my name is [doctor], I'm the GP. Who am I talking to?" In telehealth you must confirm who you are speaking to — even if the stem names the patient, it could be a family member on the line. A full three-point ID check (date of birth, address) is less useful here because you have nothing to verify it against, so at minimum establish the caller's identity.

The patient is played as grumpy: "Doctor, I've been vomiting for two days, it just doesn't stop. I just need a script for vomiting tablets or I'll hang up." Respond to the concern first: "I understand your concern — this has been a very distressing situation, vomiting for a number of days. I want to reassure you, I'm here to help you, but I need to ask a few questions to better understand what's going on so I can make a proper management plan for you. Is that okay with you?"

Exploring the Vomiting (CCVO)

Timing: When did the vomiting start? How many times have you vomited (once or twice vs ten times matters)? Is it getting worse over time?

Characteristics — use the CCVO mnemonic (Colour, Content, Volume, Odour — usable for any fluid: vomiting, diarrhoea, discharge):

  • Colour: "Can you describe the colour of the vomit?" — green (bilious) vomit points strongly towards obstruction.
  • Content: any blood or clots in the vomit.
  • Volume: optional — small vs large volume.
  • Odour: optional — a faeculent smell can be asked about but rarely changes management here.

Alleviating and aggravating factors: "Is there anything that makes your nausea or vomiting better or worse?" The key specific question: is it worse after eating (e.g. after a large meal)?

General GI Questions

  • Bloating and passing wind: "Are you feeling bloated? Are you passing wind?" — in obstruction expect "no wind" (obstipation).
  • Constipation: the patient replies he has not opened his bowels for three days.
  • Diarrhoea (now or preceding the illness) — low yield here but reasonable to tick off.
  • Fever — do not forget this.

Abdominal pain is deferred: if pain is present, explore it fully (site, intensity, quality, onset, radiation, aggravating/alleviating factors).

Ruling Out Differentials Systematically

Group 1 — GI:

  • Obstruction / malignancy red flags: recent weight loss; change in appetite; lumps and bumps; fatigue; blood in the stools (before the three days of no stools); any recent change in bowel habit.
  • Hernia: "Have you noticed any lumps or swelling in your groin?"
  • Pancreatitis: if there is abdominal pain, explore it fully; "Is it better when you lean forward?"; "Any recent binge or excessive alcohol drinking?"
  • Cholecystitis: "Did the vomiting start after a large fatty meal?"

Group 2 — Infections: fever ("Have you been feeling feverish or hot?"); recent travel (a returned traveller changes the differential list entirely); flu-like symptoms (covers COVID-19, influenza, EBV); pyelonephritis — one urinary question ("Any pain passing urine?") — and this is where the patient reveals he has passed no urine for two days, making frequency questions redundant.

Group 3 — Neurological: headaches; fever (again); rashes (meningococcal — could equally sit under infections); photophobia ("Are you sensitive to light?"); neck stiffness ("sore neck"); neurological deficits for brain tumour or meningitis — vision problems, weakness, numbness; recent head injury or falls (particularly relevant in an older diabetic, hypertensive patient — think postural hypotension, hypoglycaemia as fall causes).

Group 4 — Cardiac: chest pain, shortness of breath, dizziness.

Group 5 — Metabolic / medications: confirm he is diabetic and on antihypertensive medication. Ask about recent medication changes or any new medications (thinking of the ACE-inhibitor dose-increase and triple-whammy hyperkalaemia patterns). Rather than a generic compliance question, ask: "Were you taking your medications in the last few days?" — because with vomiting and dehydration he should have been following a sick day management plan.

Sick Day Rules (Why the Medication Question Matters)

For patients with diabetes, sick day management plans advise: if you are on metformin or an SGLT2 inhibitor and you become dehydrated (vomiting, diarrhoea), stop the medication. CKD guidelines similarly list medications to withhold during dehydration, including ACE inhibitors. Rationale: metformin accumulation can cause lactic acidosis in renal impairment, and ACE inhibitors reduce renal perfusion pressure in an already dehydrated, stressed kidney — compounding the acute kidney injury.

Note on the phone recall: candidates often could not catch the antihypertensive name. Expect an ACE inhibitor ("-pril": captopril, enalapril, ramipril, perindopril), an ARB ("-sartan": losartan, valsartan, candesartan), a calcium channel blocker (typically amlodipine), or a thiazide.

Case Findings

  • Vomiting multiple times; unable to eat anything at all; unable to keep anything down (loss of appetite).
  • Bowels not opened for three days.
  • No urine passed for two days.
  • No blood in the vomit.
  • No previous surgeries.
  • Known diabetic and hypertensive; on metformin plus an antihypertensive (likely ACE inhibitor, ARB, calcium channel blocker or thiazide — the exact name was not clearly recalled).

Provisional diagnoses: bowel obstruction and acute kidney injury.

Do Not Escalate Prematurely — But Assess Social Supports

Do not call the ambulance mid-history ("premature call"). Complete a proper history first — if you call an ambulance without knowing what you are dealing with, your handover is just "vomiting". Finish the history, then escalate as part of management.

This is also a mini geriatric case: everyone agrees this patient needs hospital, so assess supports — "Are you alone at home? Do you have anyone with you? Can anyone transfer you if needed?"

Management: Explaining the Concerns and Arranging Transfer

Acknowledge the limits of telehealth: "There are some limitations in telehealth — I cannot examine you, which limits my ability to make a definite diagnosis." Then explain your two major concerns:

  1. You have not opened your bowels for three days — something may be obstructing or blocking your bowels: possibly faecal impaction (common in older people — hard stools blocking the bowel), or a tumour, scarring/adhesions from previous surgery, twisting of the bowel (volvulus), or a hernia.
  2. You have not passed urine for two days — this means either you are severely dehydrated from fluid loss, or your kidneys have shut down, which can be contributed to by the medications you are taking.

Add that other conditions were considered (problems in the brain, problems in the heart) and that he needs to be seen in person, examined, and investigated. Expect role-play resistance ("I don't want to go to hospital, just give me the script") — use a patient-centred approach and give enough information for an informed decision: "For these reasons I think it is not safe to stay home and take medications — I want you to go to the emergency department."

How to get there: call triple zero (000). If the patient says he cannot manage the call: "If you give me consent, I can call them, inform them about your problem, give them your address, name and details, and they will come and pick you up."

Wrap up with empathy and justification: "I know you only wanted a script from today's consultation, but I'm asking you to go to hospital because I want you to be safe and to make sure we don't miss a serious condition that could be dangerous without timely treatment."

Key Teaching Points

  • Approach the case broadly rather than anchoring on "obstruction" — the anuria may be even more important than the constipation; both are red flags acting together.
  • The findings that would clinch obstruction: green (bilious) vomit and absolute constipation with no flatus (obstipation). If you are thinking obstruction, your key question is "Are you passing gas?" — you need a "no" to support the diagnosis.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The transcript lists 'postural hypertension' as a cause of falls in an elderly diabetic hypertensive patient.
This should be postural (orthostatic) HYPOTENSION — a drop in blood pressure on standing (systolic fall >=20 mmHg), common in older patients on antihypertensives and in diabetic autonomic neuropathy, and a leading contributor to falls. Corrected in the structured sections.
✏️ The transcript states 'If it's green, you do have obstruction' — presenting bilious vomiting as diagnostic of bowel obstruction.
Bilious (green) vomiting is suggestive, not diagnostic, of mechanical obstruction distal to the ampulla of Vater. It can also occur with any prolonged or severe vomiting from other causes. The correct teaching point is that bilious vomiting plus obstipation (no stool AND no flatus) strongly supports obstruction, and the diagnosis is confirmed with imaging (erect/supine abdominal X-ray or CT abdomen) after clinical assessment.
✏️ The transcript loosely equates 'uraemia' with acute kidney injury ('you can call it uraemia too').
In current Australian usage these are not interchangeable: acute kidney injury (AKI) is defined by KDIGO criteria (rise in serum creatinine >=26.5 micromol/L within 48 hours, or >=1.5x baseline within 7 days, or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/hr for 6 hours). Uraemia refers to the symptomatic clinical syndrome of retained nitrogenous waste (nausea, anorexia, confusion, pericarditis) and is a complication of severe renal failure, not a synonym for AKI.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add explicit safety-netting and telehealth documentation guidance per RACGP telehealth standards: document patient identity confirmation, verbal consent to a telephone consultation, the limitations discussed (no physical examination), the escalation advice given (000), and a contingency plan if the patient deteriorates or declines transfer — this is expected in Australian telehealth practice and is examinable.
⭐ Add the formal sick day medication list using the SADMANS mnemonic (Sulfonylureas, ACE inhibitors, Diuretics, Metformin, ARBs, NSAIDs, SGLT2 inhibitors) — medications to withhold during acute dehydrating illness. This consolidates the case's scattered teaching on metformin/ACE-inhibitor cessation and aligns with eTG and Australian Diabetes Society sick day guidance.
⭐ Add the 'triple whammy' definition as a boxed key fact: the combination of ACE inhibitor (or ARB) + diuretic + NSAID is a leading preventable cause of drug-induced AKI in Australian general practice, and ask specifically about over-the-counter NSAID use (e.g. ibuprofen bought without prescription), which patients frequently omit unless directly asked.
⭐ Add expected investigations on hospital arrival to strengthen the management discussion: UEC (urea, creatinine, potassium in mmol/L), venous blood gas, blood glucose and ketones (metformin user — exclude DKA/lactic acidosis), ECG (hyperkalaemia changes: peaked T waves, widened QRS), and abdominal imaging for obstruction.
