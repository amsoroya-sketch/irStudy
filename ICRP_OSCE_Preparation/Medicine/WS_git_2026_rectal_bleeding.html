Rectal Bleeding

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / class 7 Lower GI (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Overview

Rectal bleeding stations are typically haemorrhoids, but from 2025 the AMC has added varied and multiple findings to prevent candidates relying on recalls. The examiner wants a full differential worked through, not just haemorrhoid risk factors. Anal fissure is painful; haemorrhoids are usually painless.

Differential Diagnosis

  • Red flags: bowel cancer, anal cancer
  • Haemorrhoids; anal fissure (fissure painful, haemorrhoid painless)
  • Inflammatory bowel disease
  • Gastroenteritis (e.g. salmonella, E. coli)
  • Proctitis
  • Acute abdomen: diverticulitis, mesenteric ischaemia, appendicitis
  • Bleeding disorders
  • Anticoagulants / blood thinners
  • Polyps
  • Pseudomembranous colitis (if recent antibiotics)

History Taking

1. Introduction and open questions - patient often states "I've noticed blood in my stool." Respond to the concern.

2. Explore the complaint:

  • Timing: When did the bleeding start? First time? On and off or every time you open your bowels? Getting worse?
  • CCVO: Colour (bright/fresh red vs dark red), character (mixed with stool, on the stool surface, or only on the paper), volume (few drops vs large amount).
  • Alleviating/aggravating: constipation, straining.
  • General GI: abdominal pain, nausea/vomiting, bloating, constipation, diarrhoea, fever, dizziness, haematemesis.

3. Differential-directed questions:

  • Cancer: weight loss, appetite loss, lumps/bumps, fatigue; change in bowel habit; family history of bowel cancer; age >50 - bowel cancer screening.
  • Fissure: pain on defecation, constipation.
  • Haemorrhoids: lump at back passage - can you push it back in?
  • IBD: joint pain, blurred vision, rashes.
  • Gastroenteritis: travel, sick contacts.
  • Medications: anticoagulants; family history of bleeding disorder.
  • Proctitis: sexual history, safe sex, anal discharge.

Do not fall into the trap of assuming haemorrhoids and only asking risk factors (heavy lifting, chronic cough, chronic constipation) - actively exclude the other differentials.

Physical Examination

General appearance (pallor, cachexia, level of consciousness) and vital signs.

Abdominal examination (core): inspection (movement with respiration, distension); palpation (masses, tenderness - bowel-cancer mass, hepatomegaly in liver disease); auscultation; inguinal area.

Rectal examination (consent + chaperone): inspect for visible fissure (do not perform digital exam if a painful fissure is seen), skin tags (repeated fissures), redness/rashes, lumps/swellings (haemorrhoids, prolapse, perianal abscess), discharge and bleeding. If the patient is not in pain, proceed to digital examination for masses (haemorrhoids, cancer) and tenderness.

Office test - proctoscopy/anoscopy: often done in rural settings. Some stations report boggy masses on proctoscopy that were not felt on digital exam, so always ask for proctoscopy findings.

Diagnosis, Explanation &amp; Investigations

Haemorrhoids explanation: "In the back passage there are some blood vessels; when they become swollen we call them haemorrhoids. I'm making this diagnosis because you felt a lump / I saw a lump on examination, and you have no red flags." Always still list the differentials, led by cancer.

Version 2 (anal fissure): some patients refuse DRE due to pain - this usually indicates anal fissure. Explain: "a small, superficial tear in the back passage, usually caused by constipation, that causes bleeding." Then cover the remaining differentials.

Investigations: colonoscopy to exclude proximal pathology and confirm there are no problems elsewhere in the bowel (see correction re: diagnosing haemorrhoids).

Counselling - Family History &amp; Colorectal Cancer Screening

If the patient has a first-degree relative with colorectal cancer, counsel on their increased risk and screening. In the recall scenario the father's cancer was diagnosed at 52 years; the notes advise colonoscopy every 5 years starting from age 42 (10 years before the relative's diagnosis, or 50, whichever is earlier). Provide clear follow-up advice (see correction for current Australian risk categories and the National Bowel Cancer Screening Program).

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The notes list colonoscopy as the 'key investigation to diagnose haemorrhoids'. Colonoscopy does not diagnose haemorrhoids; haemorrhoids/anorectal lesions are best seen on inspection, anoscopy/proctoscopy or flexible sigmoidoscopy.
Diagnose haemorrhoids clinically with perianal inspection, DRE and anoscopy/proctoscopy. Colonoscopy is indicated to EXCLUDE proximal pathology (colorectal cancer, polyps, IBD) - especially with red flags, age >=45-50, altered bowel habit, iron-deficiency anaemia, or blood mixed within the stool. Do not attribute bleeding to haemorrhoids until significant pathology has been excluded.
✏️ The counselling section cites 'Uptodate' and gives only a single moderate-risk colonoscopy rule; it omits the current Australian population screening pathway and risk categorisation.
Use Australian guidance (Cancer Council Australia / NHMRC colorectal cancer guidelines and the National Bowel Cancer Screening Program). Near-average/average risk: immunochemical FOBT (iFOBT) every 2 years from age 45 to 74. Moderately increased risk (e.g. one first-degree relative diagnosed <55, or two first-degree relatives): colonoscopy every 5 years starting at age 40 (or 10 years younger than the youngest affected relative's diagnosis, whichever is earlier) - consistent with the case's father-at-52 -> start-at-42 example. High risk / suspected familial syndromes: refer to a genetics/high-risk clinic.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add a haemodynamic red-flag and resuscitation step: if bleeding is large-volume with dizziness, tachycardia or pallor, assess for haemodynamic compromise, gain IV access, take FBE/coagulation/group & hold, and escalate - lower-GI bleeding can be life-threatening.
⭐ Add characterisation that localises the source: bright-red blood on the paper/coating the stool suggests anorectal (haemorrhoids, fissure); blood mixed through the stool or with mucus/altered bowel habit suggests colorectal/IBD; melaena suggests an upper-GI source and warrants a different pathway.
⭐ Add specific management for confirmed benign causes per eTG/RACGP: haemorrhoids - increase fibre and fluids, stool softeners, topical agents, and refer for banding/surgery if persistent; chronic anal fissure - topical GTN or diltiazem.
⭐ Add medication and bleeding-risk review (anticoagulants, antiplatelets, NSAIDs) and a coagulation screen where relevant, plus documentation of the last iFOBT and family history for the screening decision.
