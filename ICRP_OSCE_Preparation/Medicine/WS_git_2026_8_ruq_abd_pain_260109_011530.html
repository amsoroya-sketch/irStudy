Right Upper Quadrant Pain: Biliary Colic vs Cholecystitis vs Cholangitis

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / 2.Abdominal pain case (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Case Overview and Learning Objective

This is a high-yield teaching cluster that carries one of the highest AMC failure rates because of diagnostic confusion between three overlapping right upper quadrant (RUQ) biliary presentations. Review of recalls shows candidates repeatedly muddle these conditions.

The aim is to build a single reproducible RUQ structure that reliably differentiates:

  • Biliary colic
  • Acute cholecystitis
  • Acute cholangitis

Because the pain is on the right, the differential list is re-ordered to consider liver and gallbladder first, then pancreas, then contralateral and thoracic causes.

Presenting Scenario and Task

Stem: Your next patient is a 52-year-old woman presenting with pain in the region of the liver/gallbladder.

Tasks:

  • Take a focused history for 4 minutes.
  • Ask the examiner for physical examination findings (findings given only if requested specifically).
  • Explain the diagnosis and differentials to the patient.

Differentials to Exclude

For upper abdominal / RUQ pain the differentials to actively exclude are:

  • Liver / gallbladder disease (biliary colic, cholecystitis, cholangitis, hepatitis, hepatocellular carcinoma)
  • Pancreatitis
  • Peptic ulcer disease (PUD)
  • Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and lower-lobe pneumonia (thoracic causes referred to upper abdomen)

Differentiating the Three Biliary Conditions

ConditionPathologyKey clinical clues
Biliary colicGallstone intermittently obstructing the cystic duct/biliary tree without established inflammationPost-prandial (large/fatty meal) severe constant RUQ/epigastric pain lasting 20 min to several hours, radiating to right shoulder tip/scapula; often wakes patient 2-3 am; normal examination; afebrile; no jaundice
Acute cholecystitisPersistent cystic duct obstruction with gallbladder inflammation and secondary infection (>90% associated with gallstones)Continuous RUQ pain, tenderness, guarding, positive Murphy sign, +/- fever; pain may worsen on breathing; jaundice in ~15%
Acute cholangitisBiliary obstruction (stone, tumour or stricture) with ascending bacterial infection of the biliary treeCharcot triad: RUQ pain + fever/rigors + jaundice. A lethal condition; older patients may present with circulatory collapse and Gram-negative septicaemia - urgent referral required

Rule of thumb: severe RUQ pain + fever + jaundice is cholangitis until proven otherwise.

Clinical Features (Reference Detail)

Biliary colic: acute-onset severe pain, often nocturnal, constant (not truly colicky), lasting 20 minutes to 2-6 hours, maximal in RUQ or epigastrium, radiating to the tip of the right shoulder or scapula. Builds to a crescendo over ~20 minutes; may be associated with nausea and considerable retching; often precipitated by a fatty meal; frequently a prior history of biliary pain.

Acute cholecystitis: gallstones present in over 90% of cases with a usual past history of biliary pain; attack often precipitated by a large fatty meal. Causative organisms are aerobic bowel flora (e.g. E. coli, Klebsiella species, Enterococcus faecalis). Signs: patient lies still, muscle guarding, rebound tenderness; jaundice in approximately 15%.

Investigations for cholecystitis: ultrasound (first line in Australian practice), HIDA scan can demonstrate an obstructed cystic duct, and WCC and CRP may be elevated.

Causes of cholangitis: (1) gallstones in the ducts, (2) cancers obstructing the duct, (3) post-surgical strictures.

Structured History

Haemodynamic stability first: before starting, confirm the patient is vitally/haemodynamically stable - ask the examiner for BP, PR, RR and temperature (keep alert for fever).

Introduction and rapport: sit down, introduce yourself, use an open question ('Tell me more about the pain'), acknowledge concerns and offer analgesia if the patient is in pain.

Pain assessment (SIQORAA):

  • Site - reconfirm location (RUQ). Use the site to re-order the differential list.
  • Intensity - 0-10 scale.
  • Quality - dull ache vs stabbing.
  • Onset - timing, first episode vs recurrent, constant vs intermittent, progression.
  • Radiation - to shoulder tip/scapula or back (classic for biliary pain).
  • Alleviating / Aggravating factors - relationship to fatty meals, position, movement.

System-Specific and Red-Flag Questions

General GI: nausea, vomiting, bloating, flatus, diarrhoea, constipation, fever.

Liver/GB red flags (malignancy screen): weight loss, appetite loss, lumps/bumps, fatigue/dizziness, fresh blood in stool, family history of bowel or liver cancer (relevant given liver metastasis presentations).

Hepatitis screen: jaundice (yellowing), dark urine, pale stool, travel history.

Gallbladder: previous episodes of pain after large fatty meals; cholangitis - fever/rigors; previous abdominal surgery (stricture risk); regular medications (hepatotoxicity).

Pancreas: alcohol use / recent binge drinking; abdominal trauma.

GORD / PUD: acid/bitter taste, heartburn, spicy food, coffee/tea intake.

ACS / pneumonia: chest pain, shortness of breath, cough.

Closure (SADMA): Smoking, Alcohol, Drugs, Medications, Allergies, plus past and family history.

Physical Examination (PEFE)

General appearance: jaundice, pallor, dehydration, level of consciousness.

Vital signs: temperature especially (fever), plus BP, RR, PR.

Abdominal examination:

  • Inspection - symmetrical movement with respiration, Cullen/Grey-Turner signs, distension, scars.
  • Palpation - superficial and deep for tenderness (RUQ), rigidity, guarding, rebound tenderness, masses, hepatomegaly/splenomegaly.
  • Percussion - tenderness.
  • Auscultation - bowel sounds present.

Complete the structure: inguinal examination and DRE (acute abdomen), then focused CVS and respiratory examination.

Special test: Murphy sign - ask the patient to breathe out, press deeply in the RUQ, then ask them to breathe in; positive if the patient gasps and stops inspiration due to pain.

Office test: urine dipstick (bilirubin positive is a concerning finding); ECG if indicated.

Worked Scenarios

Version 1 - Biliary colic (52F/45M): intermittent RUQ dull ache 5/10 over a few months, radiating to shoulder tip, worse after large fatty meals, no active pain now, no N/V, no red flags, no jaundice. Examination normal, afebrile, Murphy negative, dipstick normal. Diagnosis: biliary colic - gallstone(s) in the gallbladder.

Cholangitis (45M): RUQ pain 5/10, jaundice, dark urine/pale stool, recurrent fever with chills; alcohol 3 months ago. Examination: jaundice, temp 39C, RUQ tenderness, Murphy positive. Diagnosis: cholangitis - the specific triad (RUQ pain + fever + jaundice) points to biliary infection, most likely from a ductal stone.

Cholecystitis (40M): sudden RUQ pain radiating to back, nausea without vomiting, NO jaundice, NO fever on history. Examination: temp 38.5C, RUQ tenderness, rebound/guarding, Murphy positive, no jaundice. Diagnosis: cholecystitis (fewer than the three cholangitis features).

Cholecystitis with management (40F, 24 h RUQ pain, prior self-resolving episodes): tasks are examination, investigations, diagnosis with possible causes, and acute management. Investigations: LFTs, ultrasound (in ED, CT if urgent imaging needed), lipase, FBE, CRP, UEC. Management: admit, analgesia, IV fluids, IV antibiotics, and surgical review for laparoscopic cholecystectomy (ideally within the first 72 hours of symptom onset).

Summary Decision Rule

  • Biliary colic: not in pain now + longstanding/recurrent + linked to fatty meals + shoulder-tip radiation.
  • Cholecystitis: pain now + nausea/vomiting + no jaundice + no or minimal fever + positive Murphy.
  • Cholangitis: RUQ pain + fever + jaundice (whatever the cause - stone, cancer or stricture).

RUQ differentials also include hepatitis and hepatocellular carcinoma; epigastric - pancreatitis/pancreatic cancer; left upper quadrant - PUD/GORD; and above - ACS/IHD and lower-lobe pneumonia.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The antibiotic guidance quoted (amoxicillin/ampicillin 1 g IV 6-hourly plus gentamicin 4-6 mg/kg IV daily) reflects an older Murtagh figure and understates gentamicin dosing.
Per current Therapeutic Guidelines (eTG), empirical therapy for biliary sepsis (acute cholecystitis/cholangitis) is amoxicillin (or ampicillin) 2 g IV 6-hourly PLUS gentamicin dosed 5-7 mg/kg IV as a single daily dose (adjusted for renal function/age); add metronidazole if there is a biliary-enteric anastomosis or in severe/complicated sepsis. Step down to oral amoxicillin+clavulanate once afebrile and improving.
✏️ The note states biliary/gallbladder pain 'always radiates to the shoulder', which is an over-generalisation.
Biliary pain commonly radiates to the tip of the right shoulder, right scapula or interscapular back, but radiation is not universal; its absence does not exclude a biliary cause.
✏️ First-line biliary imaging is not stated explicitly; HIDA is emphasised.
In Australian practice, transabdominal ultrasound is the first-line investigation for suspected gallstone disease/cholecystitis (stones, wall thickening, pericholecystic fluid, sonographic Murphy sign). HIDA/cholescintigraphy is a second-line test reserved for equivocal cases; CT or MRCP is used when ductal obstruction/cholangitis is suspected.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add an explicit escalation red flag for cholangitis: progression from Charcot triad to Reynolds pentad (adding hypotension and altered mental status) signals suppurative cholangitis/septic shock - activate the sepsis pathway, take blood cultures, give IV antibiotics within 1 hour and arrange urgent biliary drainage (ERCP) and surgical/gastroenterology review.
⭐ Add ACS as a mandatory safety differential for upper-abdominal/RUQ pain (inferior MI can present as epigastric/RUQ discomfort with nausea and sweating) - document an ECG and consider troponin in at-risk patients before anchoring on a biliary diagnosis.
⭐ Reference specific Australian resources: eTG 'Biliary sepsis / acute cholecystitis and cholangitis' for antibiotics, and the RACGP/Murtagh approach to gallstone disease. Add investigation set (FBE, CRP, LFTs, lipase, UEC, ultrasound) to the metadata.
⭐ In young women of reproductive age with RUQ pain, add a urine/serum beta-hCG and consider Fitz-Hugh-Curtis (perihepatitis) and pregnancy-related causes; document the pregnancy test as a safety-net item.
⭐ Tag the case with the sonographic Murphy sign and offer analgesia (paracetamol first-line; consider parenteral opioid for severe biliary pain) during history taking rather than deferring pain relief.
