Unwell with Nasal Symptoms (Acute Allergic Rhinosinusitis + Asthma Exacerbation)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med workshop 2 - 2026 / CLASS 5 UNWELL (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: general_practice

Case Overview

A 30-year-old man presents unwell for a few days, no lead point given. Task: history taking only (6 minutes), then explain diagnosis and differentials - no physical examination. Once the complaint declares itself as a stuffy nose and watery eyes, the structure pivots to an ENT-focused history. Two diagnoses to present: acute rhinosinusitis (most likely allergic, viral trigger possible) and a concurrent asthma exacerbation.

Opening and Exploring the Complaint

  • Introduce, open-ended question, acknowledge concern, request permission
  • Define 'unwell' - patient reports stuffy nose and watery eyes (a respiratory-tract issue)
  • Timing (a few days, constant, worsening), aggravating/relieving factors, effect on life

Characterising the Nasal Symptom (CCVO)

  • Pattern: one or both nostrils / eyes
  • Colour: clear vs yellow/green; Consistency: watery vs thick
  • Volume; Odour; Content (blood - flags nasal malignancy)

Findings: clear, watery discharge - pointing away from bacterial infection and towards viral/allergic rhinitis.

ENT Questions and Red Flags

Standard ENT sequence: fever/chills (no fever), ear pain/discharge, sore throat, facial pain/pressure (positive - maxillary sinus, points to cheek), post-nasal drip, neck lumps.

Red-flag questions: rash (EBV/mononucleosis), drooling/dysphagia (epiglottitis, quinsy), voice change (bacterial tracheitis/airway infection), headache/neck stiffness (sinusitis spreading to meningitis), eye symptoms - vision change, discharge, eyelid swelling (orbital cellulitis, a complication of sinusitis requiring urgent CT).

Lower Respiratory and Atopic Cluster

LRT/pneumonia: cough (yes, dry), shortness of breath, wheeze (yes). First-time wheeze? No - childhood asthma, gave up hockey, later improved; used sister's Ventolin with relief (reversible obstruction).

Allergic/atopic: sneezing, itchy eyes/nose, watery eyes, itchy rashes (eczema screen). Family history: mother hay fever, sister asthma; patient allergic to pollen. Triggers: dust exposure at work, pets, carpets, household smoking (partner smokes).

Closing Differentials

  • Eye questions to tick off orbital cellulitis
  • Travel history (negative)
  • Sexual history - gonococcal/chlamydial pharyngitis screen
  • Malignancy red flags - weight loss, night sweats, persistent unilateral symptoms, blood in discharge
  • Past history, family history, medications, allergies

Diagnosis, Reasons and Differentials

Diagnosis: acute rhinosinusitis - inflammation of the nasal lining (rhinitis) and paranasal sinus (sinusitis); explain all three types (viral, bacterial, allergic) and that it is most likely allergic. Second diagnosis: asthma exacerbation (cough, wheeze, Ventolin helped).

Reasons: rhinorrhoea + watery eyes + maxillary facial pain; strong atopic family history; personal asthma/pollen allergy; trigger exposure; no fever (bacterial less likely).

Differentials: viral (EBV) and bacterial (Group A Strep) pharyngitis/tonsillitis; serious ENT infections (epiglottitis, bacterial tracheitis, quinsy, gonococcal pharyngitis); pneumonia, TB, atypical pneumonia; orbital cellulitis; travel-related infection; malignancy; and meningitis/sepsis if spreading infection.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The diagnostic criteria are summarised as 'any 2 or more of 4 symptoms' for rhinosinusitis.
Under the accepted (EPOS) definition, acute rhinosinusitis requires two or more symptoms, one of which must be nasal blockage/obstruction OR nasal discharge (anterior or posterior), with or without facial pain/pressure and reduction/loss of smell. The simplified 'any 2 of 4' should specify that one of the two must be blockage or discharge.
✏️ The patient's wheeze was self-treated with a family member's (sister's) Ventolin, and the case accepts this as confirmatory without addressing it.
Using another person's prescribed reliever is not appropriate practice; management should include prescribing the patient his own short-acting beta-agonist with a spacer and a written asthma action plan, rather than endorsing borrowing medication.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add a management section: most acute rhinosinusitis is viral/allergic and self-limiting - symptomatic care (analgesia, saline nasal irrigation) with intranasal corticosteroid for the allergic component; per eTG, antibiotics are reserved for severe or persistent bacterial features, and any orbital/intracranial red flag warrants urgent CT, IV antibiotics and ENT referral.
⭐ Add allergic rhinitis management and allergen avoidance: intranasal corticosteroid +/- non-sedating antihistamine, and modifiable triggers - reduce pet/carpet/dust-mite exposure and address household smoking (offer the partner smoking-cessation support).
⭐ Add asthma exacerbation management: prescribe the patient's own reliever with spacer, assess control/severity (spirometry when stable), consider an inhaled preventer given recurrent symptoms, and provide a written asthma action plan.
⭐ Consider allergy testing (skin-prick or specific IgE) given the strong atopic history to guide avoidance and treatment.
