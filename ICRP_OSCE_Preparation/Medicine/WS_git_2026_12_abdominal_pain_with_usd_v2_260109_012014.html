Mittelschmerz — Right-Sided Abdominal Pain with Ultrasound Report

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / 2.Abdominal pain case (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Station Stem and Tasks (Version 1)

Third version of the lower quadrant abdominal pain case. Your next patient is a young (24-year-old) lady who presents to you complaining of right-sided abdominal pain.

  • Task 1: Take history for 5 minutes.
  • Task 2: At 5 minutes, investigations will appear on the screen (in the face-to-face exam, they are given on a card to read).
  • Task 3: Explain the investigation results.
  • Task 4: Explain your diagnosis and management.

Note: the stem says "right sided" but does not specify upper or lower quadrant.

History

  • Haemodynamic stability first.
  • Open-ended question: "Tell me more about the pain." Patient: "I've been having this mild pain in the lower stomach, in the right lower quadrant. I'm very worried about my gallbladder — my mother had surgery for gallstones, and I am very concerned about surgery."

Design point: anyone tempted to skip the liver and gallbladder questions because the pain is RLQ should note that the designer deliberately wrote only "right sided abdominal pain" — and the patient's concern gives a very good reason to still ask liver and gallbladder questions.

  • Explore the pain: Site — RLQ. Alleviating/aggravating — "It gets better with taking Panadol and resting." First episode? — "No, I've had this type of pain before."
  • General questions, GI DDx, KUB DDx.
  • Pelvic and hernia questions: "When was your last menstrual period?" — 14 days ago. "Are your periods regular?"
  • Liver and gallbladder questions — still asked despite the RLQ site, given the patient's concern.

History findings summary: mild pain in the lower stomach; relieved with rest; previous history of similar pain; LMP 14 days ago.

Provisional Diagnosis Before Investigations

Mild pain + relieved by Panadol (paracetamol) and rest + has happened before + occurring at a specific point of the cycle (mid-cycle) = by this stage the provisional diagnosis is Mittelschmerz, pending the investigations.

Ultrasound Report

  • Ruptured follicle seen in the right ovary
  • Fluid in the pouch of Douglas
  • Few gallstones seen; no inflammation of the gallbladder, no increase in wall thickness (a deliberate confuser)
  • Appendix not visible
  • No history of appendicectomy

The diagnosis remains Mittelschmerz.

On the value and limits of ultrasound for the appendix (per UpToDate, as quoted in the workshop): ultrasound can be performed at the bedside and avoids CT radiation, but it has lower diagnostic accuracy than CT or MRI; visualisation depends on patient body habitus, discomfort and operator factors, and rates of indeterminate examinations are high — 50–85% of normal appendices are not visualised. Graded compression can cause significant discomfort in patients with appendicitis.

  • Can this be appendicitis? — No.
  • Can this be cholecystitis or biliary colic? — No.

Mittelschmerz — Pathophysiology

  • Caused by follicular enlargement prior to ovulation, or by normal follicular bleeding at ovulation.
  • Pain may occur before ovulation due to local effects of the enlarging ovary, and at the time of follicular rupture due to peritoneal irritation from blood and follicular fluid.
  • Typically very mild pain, can be recurrent, occurring mid-cycle (around day 14).
  • The fluid in the pouch of Douglas is that same blood/follicular fluid; the ruptured follicle is seen in the right ovary.

Explaining the Investigation Results (Lay Language)

Explaining the investigations is the hardest task in this case. Because "follicular rupture" cannot be said before explaining what is happening, build it up in lay terms:

  • "In each of your menstrual cycles, one of the ovaries releases an egg cell into the tubes."
  • "The egg cell grows in a sac called a follicle in the ovaries during the first 2 weeks of your cycle."
  • "Around day 14 (mid-cycle), this follicle ruptures, causing some bleeding, and releases fluid into your tummy — this causes the pain."
  • "I can see some stones in your gallbladder, but they are not blocking the tubes, there is no inflammation, and the wall thickness is fine."
  • "We were not able to see the appendix — that is not an uncommon finding on ultrasound."

Diagnosis, Reasons and Addressing the Concern

Diagnosis: "Most likely you have a condition called Mittelschmerz, or mid-cycle pain, caused by the release of the egg cell / rupture of the follicle."

Reasons:

  • Timing: happening exactly on day 14 of the cycle.
  • Recurrent: the same type of pain has happened in previous cycles.
  • Character: mild, settles with rest and Panadol (paracetamol).
  • Other conditions were ruled out on history.

Address the concern before the DDx in this case: "I don't think this pain is because of the stones in your gallbladder." Reasons to reassure: no inflammation of the gallbladder, no wall thickening, and the site of the pain is not suggestive of gallstone pain.

DDx: even when a DDx task is not listed, spend 5–10 seconds mentioning a few other conditions considered.

Management / Counselling

Conservative treatment (for the acute episode):

  • Simple analgesics (paracetamol/Panadol; an NSAID such as ibuprofen/Nurofen may be used)
  • Rest
  • Heat pack applied to the lower abdomen
  • Reassurance — expected to settle by itself; it is a benign, normal part of ovulation

Safety-net red flags — advise review/ED if:

  • Pain is getting worse
  • Unusual vaginal bleeding
  • Fever

Recurrent/problematic Mittelschmerz: the combined oral contraceptive pill (OCP) may help by suppressing ovulation. There is no strong guideline evidence for this ("it's a may"); when the exam asks for management, it means management of the acute episode, not long-term OCP planning.

Version 2 of the Station (Appendicitis Concern)

Stem: a 24-year-old lady presents complaining of right-sided abdominal pain.

  • Take history for 4 minutes (same structure)
  • Investigations given on a card
  • Explain your diagnosis and differentials

History findings: LMP 14 days ago; pain sharp; lasts a few seconds — the same characteristics expected in Mittelschmerz. The only difference: the patient is worried about appendicitis, not the gallbladder. The ultrasound report is identical (ruptured right ovarian follicle, fluid in the pouch of Douglas, incidental gallstones with a normal gallbladder, appendix not visualised, no history of appendicectomy).

Why it is not appendicitis (better context because the appendix cannot be seen):

  • No shift of pain from the umbilicus to the RLQ
  • No anorexia (appetite normal), no nausea or vomiting, no fever
  • None of the typical features of appendicitis
  • Not seeing the appendix on ultrasound is a very common finding and does not mean the patient has appendicitis

Counselling task (replaces explain-investigations): "This is a benign condition — a normal change that happens in the ovaries and follicle. When you get the pain, rest and take simple painkillers such as paracetamol (Panadol) or ibuprofen (Nurofen); you can put a heat pack on your lower stomach. I'll give you red flags to watch for, but I want to reassure you this is not a serious condition — it is part of the normal process of ovulation. If it recurs frequently and is troublesome, we have the option of starting the OCP, which may help control it."

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: history-taking-expert (Australian clinical supervisor) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The counselling script lists painkillers as 'Panadol, Paracetamol, Neurofen' — Panadol IS paracetamol (listing both is redundant and confusing), and 'Neurofen' is a misspelling of the brand Nurofen (ibuprofen).
Advise 'simple analgesia such as paracetamol (brand name Panadol) or an NSAID such as ibuprofen (brand name Nurofen)'. Murtagh recommends simple analgesics (aspirin or paracetamol) and hot-water-bottle comfort for mittelschmerz; use generic drug names first per Australian prescribing convention.
✏️ In the DDx dialogue the female patient is addressed as 'James' ('Look, James, I understand you're concerned about Appendicitis') — an internal inconsistency, as the stem describes a 24-year-old lady.
Use a consistent female role-player name throughout (e.g. 'Look, Jenna...'). Addressing the patient by the wrong name in an AMC station would also cost rapport/communication marks.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add a mandatory teaching point: in ANY woman of reproductive age with lower abdominal pain, ectopic pregnancy must be actively excluded — ask about missed periods, sexual activity and contraception, and document a negative pregnancy test (beta-hCG) before settling on Mittelschmerz. The current notes rely on LMP 14 days ago alone, which is insufficient in real practice.
⭐ Add ovarian torsion and ruptured ovarian cyst to the explicit differential list with their discriminating features (sudden severe unilateral pain, vomiting, adnexal tenderness/mass) — torsion is the must-not-miss mimic of mid-cycle pain and is time-critical (surgical emergency).
⭐ Strengthen the safety-netting script to Australian standard: specify that severe worsening pain, fainting/dizziness, fever, or heavy vaginal bleeding warrants immediate ED presentation (call 000 if collapse), rather than the current soft 'maybe it might be better to go to the ED'.
⭐ Add a reference anchor for the OCP point: Murtagh and RACGP resources note that ovulation suppression with the combined pill can prevent recurrent mittelschmerz — this converts Dr Amir's 'no references' remark into a citable teaching point while retaining the caveat that acute-episode management is what the exam asks for.
