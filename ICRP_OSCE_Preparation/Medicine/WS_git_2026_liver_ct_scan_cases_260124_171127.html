Liver Metastasis - Mini Breaking Bad News (SPIKES) & Palliative Planning

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / class 5 liver cases (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Version 1 - Stem & Investigations

Your next patient is a 65-year-old lady complaining of dyspepsia/indigestion and back pain. Investigations show microcytic hypochromic anaemia (low Hb, low MCV, low MCH) - the two main causes being iron-deficiency anaemia (IDA) and thalassaemia. A CT scan is provided.

Tasks: explain investigation results to the patient; request further investigations.

Teaching note: This is a 'mini breaking bad news' (BBN) station using the SPIKES framework - it is primarily a communication skill. Microcytic anaemia here points to bleeding (IDA), not chronic-disease anaemia (which is normocytic); combined with dyspepsia and a liver full of lesions, the picture is a GI cancer that has metastasised to the liver.

Interpreting the CT

The CT shows a liver with multiple lesions - a classic appearance for metastasis (resembling, but not identical to, the 'cannonball' appearance seen in the lungs). Dyspepsia + IDA (implying GI blood loss) → a GI cancer (gastric or bowel) spreading to the liver.

Most common sites of colon cancer metastasis: liver (top of the list), lungs, lymph nodes, peritoneum; bone rarely (relevant here given the back pain).

Explaining the Blood Results

"We did a Full Blood Count. We checked your red blood cells, which carry oxygen using a particle called haemoglobin. You have a low amount of haemoglobin, smaller red cells than expected, and less haemoglobin in each cell."

Then move to the CT: "We've done a scan of your abdomen and checked your liver and gallbladder."

SPIKES - Breaking the News

Pick up the CT image on the desk and mark on it. "I can see a number of circular lesions in your liver, which suggests there are some growths there."

  1. Warning shot: "Jenny, unfortunately I don't have any good news today."
  2. Offer support: "Would you like me to call anyone to be with you while we discuss the results?"
  3. Deliver clearly: "We usually see this picture when a cancer has spread to the liver. We call this metastasis."
  4. Empathy: "I'm really sorry I had to give you this news. I wish I had better news. The results are not what we were hoping for." Use tissues, water, and silence; allow the patient to absorb the news.
  5. Emotional check: "How are you feeling now?"

Explaining the Diagnosis & Further Investigation

"Based on your indigestion and the iron-deficiency anaemia - which means you may be bleeding in your bowel or stomach - I'm concerned about a bowel cancer or gastric cancer that has spread to your liver. I'm also concerned that your back pain may reflect spread to the bones."

Further investigations:

  1. Endoscopy and colonoscopy (camera via mouth and back passage; biopsy any ulcer/lesion)
  2. PET scan / bone scan to look for other sites of spread
  3. Liver lesion biopsy if the source is uncertain
  4. Triphasic MRI of the liver

Version 2 (2025) - 52-year-old man, 'What is metastasis?'

A 52-year-old man with previously treated colon cancer is told he has metastasis on abdominal CT. He looks sad but does not cry and asks: "What is metastasis?"

Tasks: take further history (colon cancer history); explain your management plan (still a mini-BBN of a sensitive topic).

Introduction/opening: "I went through your files, James, and I believe you had a tough day yesterday. Can you tell me more about yesterday / how you're feeling today?" → "They told me there's something in my liver - metastasis. What is metastasis?"

Explain: "Metastasis means a cancer has spread to another part of the body - in your case most likely a spread from the bowel cancer to the liver." Offer support, emotional check, empathy, and reassurance that the team will do their best.

Focused Colon Cancer History

Ask permission, then the four questions when a patient has a known condition:

  1. When were you diagnosed with colon cancer?
  2. What treatment did you receive?
  3. Did you have regular follow-up with your GP and specialists? (surveillance with tumour markers after resection)
  4. Complications - screening for metastasis

General cancer screen: weight loss, loss of appetite, lumps/bumps, tiredness. Other metastasis: cough (lung), bone pain. Liver metastasis symptoms: jaundice, itchiness, pale stool, dark urine. Social: "Do you have support from your family?" (stage IV, end-of-life planning).

Management Plan (Palliative)

  1. MDT approach: GP, oncologist, surgeon, psychologist, cancer nurse, social worker.
  2. Confirm the source: biopsy the lesion; special MRI of the liver as needed. (Colonoscopy/endoscopy may not be repeated if the primary is already resected.)
  3. Palliative care aimed at symptom control: analgesia, antiemetics; chemotherapy/radiotherapy or surgery to control symptoms.
  4. Advance Care Directive: recommend making one and appointing a substitute decision-maker while the patient retains capacity.
  5. Support: refer to Cancer Council and cancer nurses.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ Microcytic hypochromic anaemia is said to have 'just two conditions - IDA and thalassaemia'.
This is an over-simplification: the causes of microcytic anaemia in Australian practice also include anaemia of chronic disease/inflammation (can be microcytic), sideroblastic anaemia and lead poisoning. The teaching point that IDA from GI blood loss is the key concern here is valid, but the differential should not be limited to two conditions. In an older adult, new IDA mandates GI investigation to exclude malignancy.
✏️ The plan lists 'chemotherapy/radiotherapy or surgery to control symptoms' generically for a stage IV colorectal cancer.
Per eviQ/Australian oncology practice, systemic therapy for metastatic colorectal cancer is guided by molecular testing (RAS/BRAF, MSI status) and a solitary/oligometastatic liver deposit may be resectable with curative intent. The plan should be framed as MDT- and biomarker-directed rather than uniformly palliative, and radiotherapy has a limited role in colorectal (unlike rectal) primaries.
✏️ SPIKES is applied but the 'Perception' and 'Invitation' steps are compressed.
Full SPIKES includes S-Setting, P-Perception ('What is your understanding so far?'), I-Invitation ('How much detail would you like?'), K-Knowledge, E-Empathy, S-Strategy/Summary. Explicitly eliciting perception and invitation before delivering the news is the current Australian communication standard and should be added.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add explicit red flags that make this an urgent presentation: new IDA in a person over 50, weight loss, and back pain suggesting bony metastasis or (critically) spinal cord/cauda equina compression - screen for saddle anaesthesia, leg weakness and bladder/bowel dysfunction, which is a neurosurgical/oncological emergency.
⭐ Add a Strategy/Summary step and a clear safety-net: written information, a contact number, a follow-up appointment, and offer of a support person or interpreter - completing the SPIKES 'S'.
⭐ Add the correct Australian iron-deficiency work-up (iron studies: ferritin, transferrin saturation) before labelling IDA, and note haemoglobinopathy screening if thalassaemia is genuinely considered.
⭐ Add metadata flagging this as a communication/BBN station with a linked palliative-care and Advance Care Directive learning objective, and cross-reference the other liver-metastasis versions in this class.
