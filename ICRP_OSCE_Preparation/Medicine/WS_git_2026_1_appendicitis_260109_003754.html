Acute Appendicitis — Young Woman with Abdominal Pain

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / 2.Abdominal pain case (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Station Stem and Tasks

Your next patient is a young woman who presents to the emergency department complaining of abdominal pain.

  • Task 1: Take history for 4 minutes.
  • Task 2: Ask physical examination findings from the examiner — you will only be given findings if you ask for signs specifically.
  • Task 3: Explain the diagnosis and differentials to the patient.

Notes on the stem: no site or duration of the pain is given. A prompt (mini bell) sounds at 4 minutes inside the station. Physical examination findings must be requested from the examiner, and signs must be asked for very specifically.

Brainstorming During 2-Minute Reading Time

While reading the stem outside, use the 2 minutes to plan the 8 minutes inside the station:

  1. What is the list of differential diagnoses (DDx) for this patient?
  2. Map the history taking into four boxes: (1) Introduction, (2) Explore the pain, (3) Rule out the DDx groups, (4) Good closure.

For abdominal pain in a young woman, the DDx groups to rule out are:

  • GI group
  • KUB group (kidneys, ureters, bladder)
  • Pelvic group (if the pain is lower abdominal, pelvic causes must be considered; if upper abdominal, the relevant upper groups apply instead)

Opening the Station: Haemodynamic Stability and Introduction

On entering, the examiner checks ID; sanitise hands and approach the patient. Before starting the case, confirm the patient is haemodynamically stable:

"Before starting the case, I want to make sure my patient is vitally and haemodynamically stable. Can you please tell me the vitals?"

Examiner: BP 110/70 mmHg, pulse 88/min, respiratory rate 12/min, temperature 36.5°C.

Introduction (simple and quick): "Hi Jenna, my name is Amir. I'm your doctor for today." Then an open-ended question: "How can I help you today?"

Opening statement (role player): "Doctor, I've been having this pain in the right lower side of my stomach. It started yesterday, it's getting worse, and I'm starting to become a little bit worried about it." This gives the site, the onset, and an expressed concern.

Responding to the Opening Statement (Concern, Empathy, Analgesia)

  • Empathy and acknowledging the concern: "I'm really sorry to hear that, Jenna. The pain you describe is concerning."
  • Reassurance: "I'll need to ask you a few questions to get a better idea about your pain, and I promise to take good care of you. Is that okay?"
  • Painkiller offer: Ask two questions: (1) "Are you in pain now?" (2) If yes, "Would you like a painkiller?" Before 2025 the role player usually declined; from 2025 the role player may say "Yes, I want it". The recommended response is: "I'll arrange some painkillers for you, but let's proceed with our history taking."

Exam-time tactic: Dr Amir advises not to spend station time asking about analgesic allergies or which painkillers were already taken, for two reasons: (1) it consumes the 4-minute history time needed for the differentials; (2) a specific analgesic cannot sensibly be chosen before the history, examination and diagnosis. If the patient says yes, say "I'll arrange some painkillers" and proceed. (See expert review: in real Australian practice analgesia should not be withheld, and allergies must be checked before any drug is given.)

Step 2: Assess the Pain (SIQORAA)

  • S — Site: Reconfirm even though the patient has stated it, because role players always leave something out. "Can you tell me where you feel the pain?" — "Right lower side of my stomach." Key point — shifting of the pain: "Your pain is in the RLQ; where did the pain start?" — "Initially it started around my belly button yesterday, and it has now shifted down to the right lower quadrant."
  • I — Intensity: "On a scale of 1–10, 10 being the worst pain you've experienced, how bad is your pain?" — "It's 6."
  • Q — Quality: "Is it a dull ache or a sharp pain?" — "A dull, vague ache."
  • O — Onset/pattern: "Is it on and off, or continuous?" — "Continuous; it doesn't go away. It's worse today" (gradually worsening).
  • R — Radiation: "Does the pain travel anywhere?" — "No."
  • A, A — Alleviating and aggravating factors: Nothing makes it better; worsening with time. Not related to food or eating. No position makes a difference.

Summary after exploring the pain: acute pain that started at the umbilicus and shifted to the right lower quadrant — the differential list is now much narrower.

Ruling Out the Differential Groups

GI group:

  • Nausea and vomiting? — Yes, nausea; may have vomited once.
  • Bloating? — No. Passing gas/wind? — Yes. Diarrhoea? — No. Constipation? — No. Fever? — No.

Red flags:

  • Weight loss? — No.
  • Loss of appetite? — Yes (anorexia — a key positive for appendicitis; the structured red-flag screen surfaces it even if appendicitis had not yet been considered).
  • Lumps or bumps? — No. Light-headed or dizzy? — No.
  • Blood in stool? — No. Dark stool? — No. Recent change in bowel motions? — No.
  • Previous medications/surgery? Family history of bowel cancer? — No.

KUB group: dysuria — no; urinary frequency — no; blood in urine — no.

Pelvic group:

  • Hernia: lumps or swelling in the groin? — No.
  • Vaginal bleeding? — No. Vaginal discharge? — No.
  • Last menstrual period? — 3 weeks ago; periods regular.
  • Sexual history: sexually active — yes; practises safe sex with condoms — yes; can also ask about previous STI diagnoses.

Time usually runs out here; a full closure may not be possible, but this structure covers the essential expected components: pain description, three DDx groups ruled out, and the key diagnostic points.

History Findings Summary

  • RLQ abdominal pain, started around the umbilicus and moved to the RLQ
  • Anorexia present
  • Worse since last night; no radiation
  • Role player acting in pain

Physical Examination From the Examiner (PEFE)

  • General appearance: "As you can see."
  • Vital signs: already asked; postural drop — none.
  • Abdominal inspection: abdomen moves symmetrically with respiration — yes; Cullen sign / Grey Turner sign — no; distension — no.
  • Superficial and deep palpation: tenderness in the right lower quadrant — yes. Rigidity — no. Guarding — no. Rebound tenderness — YES (the component needed to make the diagnosis, indicating peritoneal irritation).
  • Percussion: tenderness — may be reported yes or no. Auscultation: bowel sounds present.
  • Inguinal area — normal. Digital rectal examination — normal.

Special tests of appendicitis (ask one or two by name; the examiner answers positive/negative and never asks you to describe how they are performed):

  • Rovsing's sign — positive.
  • Psoas sign (or obturator sign) — psoas positive.

Dr Amir does not favour asking for McBurney's point tenderness separately, since that spot has already been palpated; Rovsing's, psoas or obturator signs are preferred.

Pelvic Examination and Office Tests

Even when confident of appendicitis, complete the logical approach with a pelvic examination, with patient consent and a chaperone present:

  • Inspection: rashes/redness — no; lumps/swelling — no; discharge/bleeding — no.
  • Speculum: bleeding or discharge at the cervix — no.
  • Bimanual: cervical motion tenderness — negative; uterus normal size, non-tender; adnexal tenderness/mass — no.
  • Office tests: urine dipstick — normal. With an LMP 3 weeks ago a urine pregnancy test is not strictly required, but may still be done. Blood sugar level optional.

Working diagnosis: acute appendicitis.

Key Concept: Migration of Pain and the Alvarado Score

One of the most characteristic features of appendicitis is the shift of pain: it starts around the umbilicus and, a number of hours later (classically by 24 hours), shifts to the right lower quadrant.

Alvarado score components:

ComponentPoints
Right lower quadrant tenderness (sign)2
Rebound tenderness (sign)1
Anorexia (symptom)1
Nausea or vomiting (symptom)1
Migration of pain to the right lower quadrant (symptom)1
Elevated temperature1
Leukocytosis (>10,000 WBC)2
Left shift (>75% neutrophils)1
  • Score 5–6: compatible with acute appendicitis
  • Score 7–8: probable appendicitis
  • Score 9–10: very probable appendicitis

Teaching points: the Alvarado score is not formally used in Australia and does not need to be memorised, but it highlights the key history points (anorexia, nausea/vomiting, migration of pain), the key examination points (tenderness plus rebound tenderness), and why temperature is asked with the vital signs. A candidate who finds a positive Rovsing's sign but never asked about anorexia, nausea, vomiting and shifting pain in the history will not pass.

Explaining the Diagnosis to the Patient

Diagnosis: "Jenna, you're having a condition called acute appendicitis."

Explanation (keep it simple, under about 10 seconds): "The appendix is a small finger-shaped pouch attached to your large bowel in the right lower side of your stomach, and it is inflamed."

Reasons for the diagnosis:

  • Loss of appetite (anorexia)
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • The pain shifted — it started around the belly button and moved down to the right lower quadrant
  • On examination, the special tests of appendicitis were positive

Differentials Explained to the Patient

Rather than a long list, give a short list of good, specific differentials with the reason each is unlikely:

  1. Ectopic pregnancy (obstetric): a pregnancy that abnormally forms in the tubes — unlikely because the last period was not missed and the pregnancy test checked was negative.
  2. Pelvic inflammatory disease (gynaecological): an infection in the womb and tubes — unlikely because there is no vaginal discharge and no fever.
  3. Inguinal hernia (GI): a sac in the groin — unlikely because there is no swelling and the examination of the inguinal area was normal.
  4. Urinary tract infection / cystitis (KUB): an infection in the bladder — unlikely because there is no pain or burning on passing urine.

Extended (unlikely) list if time allows:

  • GI: bowel obstruction, bowel cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, infectious colitis/gastroenteritis, ischaemic colitis
  • KUB: kidney stone, urinary retention
  • Obstetrics & gynaecology: ovarian cyst, ovarian torsion
  • Other (young patient): shingles, traumatic injury to abdominal organs, diabetic ketoacidosis

If running out of time, switch to "list mode": "Jenna, I think I'm running out of time — I was also thinking about ectopic pregnancy, PID, hernias, UTI...", then expand on each only if time remains.

Investigations and Management (Reminder)

Investigations:

  • Full blood examination — looking for leukocytosis
  • ESR / CRP
  • Imaging — preferably ultrasound or CT scan if there is diagnostic doubt

Management:

  • Admit the patient
  • Urgent surgery (appendicectomy)
  • Supportive care: antibiotics, IV fluids, analgesia

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: history-taking-expert (Australian clinical supervisor) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ In the role-player dialogue the OCR reads 'Today I feel the pain around the belly button', which contradicts the rest of the case (pain started periumbilically yesterday and shifted to the RLQ today).
The intended finding, consistent with the case summary and the migration-of-pain teaching point, is: pain started around the umbilicus yesterday and TODAY is felt in the right lower quadrant. This classic visceral-to-somatic migration within ~24 hours is the key discriminating feature of acute appendicitis.
✏️ The note advises skipping questions about analgesic allergies and prior painkillers entirely, and effectively deferring analgesia ('I'll arrange some painkillers' without actioning it).
As an exam time-management tactic this is defensible, but candidates must understand real Australian practice: timely analgesia in the acute abdomen is standard of care and does NOT mask diagnosis or reduce diagnostic accuracy (eTG: Analgesia; NSW Health pain management standards). Before any analgesic is actually charted, allergies and prior doses MUST be checked. In an AMC management station, offering and arranging appropriate analgesia (e.g. IV paracetamol or titrated opioid) with allergy check is expected.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add a note that in Australian EDs the working diagnosis triggers a surgical registrar referral, keeping the patient nil by mouth (NBM), IV access with fluids, and a beta-hCG in ALL women of reproductive age with abdominal pain regardless of LMP — a negative urine pregnancy test should be documented before theatre and before imaging decisions, not treated as optional.
⭐ Add the Australian imaging pathway: ultrasound first in young women (avoids radiation, also assesses ovaries/tubes for torsion, ectopic, cyst); CT abdomen/pelvis if ultrasound is equivocal; note that a non-visualised appendix on ultrasound is common and does not exclude appendicitis.
⭐ Add red flags for complicated appendicitis to the teaching notes: fever >38, generalised peritonism (rigidity/guarding), tachycardia and hypotension suggest perforation — escalate immediately and involve the surgical team (duty-of-care escalation expected in AMC scoring).
⭐ Add explicit consent/chaperone language for the pelvic examination aligned with the Medical Board of Australia's Good Medical Practice code and AMC expectations: verbal consent, chaperone offered and documented.
⭐ Structure improvement: add a 30-second 'good closure' script (summarise, check understanding, safety-net with red flags: worsening pain, fever, vomiting — return to ED / call 000 if severe) so candidates who finish early score closure marks.
