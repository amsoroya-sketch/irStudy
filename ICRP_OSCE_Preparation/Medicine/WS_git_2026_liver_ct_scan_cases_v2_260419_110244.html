Single Liver Lesion on CT with Prior Bowel Cancer - Suspected Metastasis

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / class 5 liver cases (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Stem & Tasks

Your next patient is a 60-year-old man presenting with pale stools, a background of previous cholecystectomy and a prior bowel (colon) cancer. A CT scan is provided showing a single hypodense liver lesion (not multiple lesions).

Tasks:

  • Take a short, focused history (2-3 minutes)
  • Explain investigations to the patient
  • Explain diagnosis and differentials

Teaching note: A single lesion (rather than multiple) makes widespread metastasis less certain; the source may or may not be the previous colon cancer. The short history is deliberately designed to explore the colon cancer background.

Focused History - Colon Cancer Screen

After open-ended questions, move to the four colon-cancer questions:

  1. When were you diagnosed with your bowel cancer?
  2. What treatment did you receive? → "Only surgery; they told me I didn't need chemotherapy or radiotherapy and I'd be fine after surgery."
  3. Have you been having regular follow-up with your GP and specialists? → "No, I haven't seen them since the surgery."
  4. Complications - general cancer screen: weight loss, loss of appetite, lumps/bumps (lymph nodes), tiredness/light-headedness/dizziness, yellow discolouration of skin, itchiness.

Findings: surgical treatment only with no follow-up and no surveillance of tumour markers - raising the likelihood of metastatic recurrence.

Explaining the CT Scan

In the face-to-face exam, pick up the image. "We've done a CT scan of your abdomen and are looking at a cross-sectional image of your liver. I can see a circular lump in your liver, which is an abnormal finding."

Diagnosis & Differentials

Resist rushing to a metastasis label just because of the prior cancer and the pattern of earlier cases. With a single, hypodense (dark) liver lesion, consider the differentials:

  • Hepatocellular adenoma
  • Liver abscess
  • Liver cyst
  • Haemangioma
  • Primary hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)
  • Metastasis

Explanation to patient: "With your history of bowel cancer, I am concerned about the possibility of spread of the bowel cancer to your liver. The second condition I'm considering is a primary liver cancer (HCC) putting pressure on the tubes and causing your symptoms." Either way this can produce an obstructive jaundice picture (dark urine, pale stool, pain, yellowish discolouration).

Other differentials for the presentation (epigastric pain relevant to the liver): hepatitis, cholangitis, cholecystitis, pancreatitis and pancreatic cancer.

Investigations / Management

  1. Triphasic MRI of the liver
  2. Possible biopsy of the lesion
  3. Colon cancer follow-up markers - CEA - and PET scan
  4. Find the cause and treat the cause (colon cancer metastasis → oncology/palliative pathway; liver cancer → different pathway; benign cyst → different again)

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The history states the patient had colon cancer treated 'with no chemotherapy/radiotherapy' and 'no follow-up', presented as unremarkable pathway.
This is a genuine care gap to name explicitly: Australian colorectal cancer guidelines (Cancer Council/eviQ) mandate structured surveillance after curative resection - serial CEA, surveillance colonoscopy and CT - so the absence of follow-up is itself an abnormal finding that raises the pre-test probability of recurrence and should be documented as such.
✏️ A previous cholecystectomy is in the stem but the pale-stool/obstructive picture is attributed mainly to the liver lesion.
Post-cholecystectomy patients can still develop retained/CBD stones and biliary strictures; pale stools + dark urine indicate obstructive jaundice and should trigger LFTs (fractionated bilirubin, ALP, GGT) and biliary imaging (MRCP) as well as lesion work-up, not lesion characterisation alone.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add explicit red-flag/obstructive-jaundice screening (fever/rigors for cholangitis, degree of jaundice, steatorrhoea, pruritus) and a safety-net for cholangitis, which is a medical emergency requiring urgent referral.
⭐ Add the correct colorectal metastasis work-up per Australian practice: CEA, staging CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, contrast MRI liver for lesion characterisation and resectability assessment, and MDT (multidisciplinary team) discussion - the case lists PET but a resectable solitary liver metastasis may be curable, which changes counselling tone.
⭐ Because a single metastasis may be resectable, add counselling that the diagnosis is not yet confirmed and outcomes range from potentially curative surgery to palliative care - avoid prematurely framing it as terminal.
⭐ Add metadata cross-referencing the multiple-lesion 'mini breaking bad news' versions in this class so the single-vs-multiple lesion teaching contrast is preserved.
