Suspected Hepatobiliary Malignancy — 55-Year-Old Woman with 3 Months of RUQ Pain and Nodular Hepatomegaly

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / 2.Abdominal pain case (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Station Stem and Tasks

Your next patient is a 55-year-old lady who presents to you complaining of abdominal pain for 3 months.

  • Task 1: Take history for 4 minutes.
  • Task 2: Ask physical examination findings from the examiner (in the online exam the physical examination is given on a card, with no opportunity to ask for more).
  • Task 3: Explain your diagnosis and differentials to the patient.

This is a very rare case — it does not come up often (only once each in the online and face-to-face exams).

History Findings

  • Right upper quadrant (RUQ) abdominal pain for 3 months
  • Loss of weight (LOW)
  • Loss of appetite (LOA)
  • Recent travel to Queensland
  • Family history of bile duct cancer
  • Drinks 6 beers every day (significant alcohol intake — a risk factor)

Physical Examination Findings

  • Hepatic nodularity — on feeling the liver edge, it is nodular
  • Non-tender enlargement of the liver (non-tender hepatomegaly)

Interpretation: a nodular liver edge raises the thought of cirrhosis, but the findings must be put in the context of the whole case: a 55-year-old patient with 3 months of pain, red flags of weight loss and loss of appetite, a family history of cancer, and nodularity on examination. The top differential is therefore cancer.

Explaining the Diagnosis

Diagnosis to the patient: "In your case, I am quite concerned about the possibility of a cancer in your liver, or possibly in your gallbladder/bile ducts."

Reasons:

  • Pain persisting for a number of months
  • Red flags: loss of weight and loss of appetite
  • Family history of cancer in the bile ducts
  • A personal risk factor for cancer in that area: daily alcohol intake

So the top differential is cancer.

Other Differentials

RUQ abdominal pain differentials:

  • Hepatitis (various forms — viral, alcoholic)
  • Cholangitis
  • Cholecystitis
  • Pancreatitis and pancreatic cancer

Because it is still upper abdominal pain, also include the extra-abdominal mimics:

  • Acute coronary syndrome (ACS)
  • Lower lobe pneumonia

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: history-taking-expert (Australian clinical supervisor) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The notes state 'family history of Cancer in the Bile ducts, that means a GB cancer'. Bile duct cancer (cholangiocarcinoma) and gallbladder cancer are distinct malignancies, not synonyms.
Keep the terms separate when explaining to the patient: cholangiocarcinoma arises from the bile ducts; gallbladder carcinoma arises from the gallbladder. Both belong to the biliary tract cancer family, so the family history remains relevant, but the explanation should say 'cancer of the bile ducts or gallbladder (the biliary system)' rather than equating the two.
✏️ Alcohol is cited as a direct risk factor for 'cancer in that area' without mechanism, and cirrhosis is mentioned only as a passing thought.
Clarify the causal chain for candidates: chronic heavy alcohol use (here about 6 standard drinks-plus daily, far above the Australian guideline of no more than 10 standard drinks per week) causes alcoholic liver disease and cirrhosis, and cirrhosis of any cause is the dominant risk factor for hepatocellular carcinoma. A nodular non-tender liver in a heavy drinker with constitutional red flags suggests HCC arising in a cirrhotic liver — this reasoning should be explicit in the explanation.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add the expected investigation pathway for the closure/extension: LFTs, FBE, coagulation profile, hepatitis B and C serology, alpha-fetoprotein, and multiphase (triple-phase) contrast CT or MRI of the liver; urgent referral to a hepatobiliary/liver clinic per the Optimal Care Pathway for hepatocellular carcinoma. Do not biopsy before specialist imaging review.
⭐ Clarify the 'travel to QLD' clue: it invites a brief infective screen (e.g. hepatitis A risk, Q fever in rural QLD, amoebic liver abscess after tropical travel) — candidates should ask what the travel involved rather than ignore it; an abscess, however, is typically tender, unlike this liver.
⭐ Add an alcohol-history and brief-intervention component expected in Australian practice: quantify intake in standard drinks (one 375 mL full-strength beer is about 1.4 standard drinks, so '6 beers daily' is roughly 8+ standard drinks/day), screen with AUDIT-C, and flag withdrawal risk if admitted — RACGP SNAP framework.
⭐ Add a communication note: this station is effectively a 'raising the possibility of cancer' conversation — use a warning shot, plain language, and check understanding (SPIKES-style), and avoid giving a definitive cancer diagnosis before tissue/imaging confirmation; say 'I am concerned this could be a cancer and we need urgent tests' rather than 'you have cancer'.
