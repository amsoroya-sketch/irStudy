Weight Cases: Weight Gain (Fatty Liver), Abdominal Distension (Ovarian Cancer) and Weight Loss

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / 6. weight cases (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: gastroenterology

Case 1: Weight Gain (April 2025 recall) - History

Dr Amir describes this as the revised ‘fatty liver’ version of the abdominal pain case (April 2025).

  1. Open-ended question: the patient states he is putting on weight and it is getting worse. Address the concern and respond to the statement: “Let me ask you a few questions to figure out what’s going on. Is that okay with you?”
  2. Explore the complaint:
    • Describe your weight gain pattern.
    • How many kilos have you put on, and over what period? Have you measured your weight?
    • When did you first notice it?
    • Triggering factors: any recent changes in your lifestyle?
    • How is it affecting your life?

Case 1: Differential Diagnosis Questions

  1. Hypothyroidism: any weather preference (cold or hot)? Constipation?
  2. Fluid retention: heart failure — chest pain? Shortness of breath? Chronic kidney disease — swelling in your legs? Problems with urination?
  3. Cushing syndrome: any rashes? Striae?
  4. Medications: diabetes medications (e.g. insulin, sulfonylureas) and others (e.g. corticosteroids, antipsychotics).
  5. Eating disorders: anorexia nervosa screen — “Do you have an intense fear of gaining weight?” Binge eating / purging — “Have you tried to lose weight by vomiting or laxatives?”
  6. Lifestyle: describe your diet — is it healthy? Binge eating — “Do you have episodes where you eat a large amount of food over a short time?” Do you exercise regularly?
  7. Liver disease — emphasise this because raised LFTs are mentioned in the stem (do not put it first, but move it higher in the questioning order): yellow discolouration of the skin? Dark urine and pale stools? Any itching? Consider the different versions of liver disease and screen risk factors with ABPFIT NOSEX:
    • A — Alcohol: how long and how much?
    • B — Blood: blood transfusion? Tattooing? Piercing?
    • IV drug use history
    • Sexual history
    • Occupational history
    • Needlestick injury

If time allows, screen for complications of obesity: joint pains (any type) and mental health (“How has your mood been lately?”).

Case 1: Physical Examination and Office Tests

  • General appearance: BMI and waist circumference; moon face; dorsocervical fat pad (‘neck hump’); any rashes; striae; acanthosis nigricans.
  • Vital signs: all four (BP the most important).
  • Thyroid: “I want to do a thyroid examination.”
  • CVS: quick examination — S1, S2, murmurs, gallop rhythm.
  • Respiratory: check for bibasal crepitations.
  • Abdomen: inspection — distension; palpation — tenderness, masses, hepatosplenomegaly; shifting dullness.
  • Office tests: blood sugar level; urine dipstick (kidney disease, heart failure, IBD).

Known recall findings: BMI 38, waist circumference 91 cm.

Case 1: Diagnosis and Differentials

Diagnosis: fatty liver disease, with lifestyle (mainly diet and lack of physical activity) driving the weight gain.

Patient explanation: “Most likely you have a condition called fatty liver disease. When you have a higher amount of fat in your blood, together with lifestyle factors like diet and lack of exercise, the extra fat gets stored in your liver and can cause inflammation. The main cause of your weight gain is your diet and lack of exercise.” (See corrections regarding current MASLD/MASH terminology.)

Differentials to mention: thyroid disease, Cushing syndrome, fluid retention, medications, eating disorders and mental health conditions.

Case 2: Abdominal Girth / Distension - Differential Framework

You must know the differentials to pass this case.

  • Gas/GI causes: lactose intolerance, coeliac disease, bowel obstruction, bloating, bowel cancer, IBD, hernia, previous surgeries (adhesions), giardiasis, IBS.
  • Ovarian cancer.
  • Weight gain differentials: lifestyle, fluid retention, thyroid disease, Cushing syndrome (mental health and eating disorders may be skipped for time).

Case 2: History

Open-ended question, then respond to the concern.

Explore the complaint:

  1. Describe: “What do you mean by this? Are you feeling bloated or having excessive gas? Or have you noticed the increasing size of your tummy?”
  2. Timing: when did it start? On/off or constant? Is it getting worse?
  3. Alleviating and aggravating factors: any specific food?
  4. General GI questions: bloating, passing wind, diarrhoea or constipation? Abdominal pain?

Differential questions:

  • Red flags (GI): loss of weight (note: weight gain can also occur here from fluid), loss of appetite, lumps and bumps, tiredness, family history of cancers. Any blood in your stool or dark stool? Any change in your bowel habits?
  • Food-related: do symptoms worsen with dairy products (lactose intolerance)? Greasy stools that stick to the pan, float on water and are hard to flush (steatorrhoea — coeliac disease)?
  • Obstruction-related: any lumps or swellings in your groin (hernia)? Any previous surgeries (adhesions)?
  • IBD: any joint pain? Blurring of vision? Marks or rashes?
  • IBS: is the bloating better when you open your bowels? Alternating constipation and diarrhoea? Stressors at work (“where do you work?”) and at home (“who do you live with?”).

(A handwritten mind-map on the source page repeats this same flow: open-ended/respond → describe → timing → alleviating/aggravating factors → general GI questions → red flags [LOW, LOA, lumps and bumps, family history, blood in stool/dark stool, bowel habit change] → food-related [dairy, greasy stool] → obstruction → IBD → IBS/stress.)

  • Ovarian cancer questions:
    1. Family history of ovarian or breast cancer?
    2. Early satiety — “Do you feel full after a small amount of food?”
    3. Urinary symptoms — frequency (“Are you passing urine more frequently?”) and urgency (“Do you feel a sudden urge to pass urine?”).
    4. Tiredness.
  • Hypothyroidism: weather preference, weight change.
  • Fluid retention: heart failure — shortness of breath, chest pain; CKD; ascites; liver disease — yellow discolouration of the skin.
  • Medications, and other weight-gain differentials if time permits.

Case 2: Diagnosis Delivery

“In your case, I am concerned about the possibility of an ovarian cancer.”

Reasons: bloating and distension due to fluid collection (ascites); constipation; and the physical examination finding of a mass in the lower abdomen. “Putting all this together, there might be a chance you have ovarian cancer.”

Differentials:

  • Bloating/GI: bowel cancer (but no blood in stool and no weight loss), coeliac disease, lactose intolerance, IBD, bowel obstruction, hernia, previous surgeries, IBS.
  • Weight gain: lifestyle, fluid retention (heart failure, CKD, liver disease), hypothyroidism, Cushing syndrome, medications.

Case 3: Weight Loss - Differential Framework

  1. Malignancy: leukaemia/lymphoma, cervical cancer.
  2. Infections: HIV, TB, hepatitis B and C.
  3. Endocrine: diabetes mellitus, hyperthyroidism.
  4. GI: IBD, coeliac disease.
  5. Rheumatological/autoimmune: SLE, rheumatoid arthritis.
  6. Psychiatric: eating disorders, major depressive disorder.
  7. Substances and medications.
  8. Lifestyle.
  9. Respiratory: Pancoast tumour.

Case 3: History

  1. Open-ended question: the patient will be concerned about the weight loss — respond to this concern first.
  2. Explore the complaint: ask the patient to describe the weight loss. How many kilos have you lost, and over what period? When did you first notice it? How is it affecting your life?
  3. Differential questions:
    • Malignancy — general cancer questions: any change in appetite? Lumps and bumps? Tiredness? Night sweats? Specific: family history of cancer; cervical screening test up to date; period history.
    • Infections: general — fever/chills. Specific — travel history; sexual history (“Are you sexually active? Do you practise safe sex / use condoms?”); yellow discolouration of the skin (hepatitis).
    • Endocrine: hyperthyroidism — weather preference, heat intolerance, palpitations. Diabetes — polyuria, polydipsia (“Have you noticed an increase in your thirst, or in the amount of urine you pass?”).
    • GI: IBD — blood in stools, joint pain or rashes, blurring of vision. Coeliac — greasy stools (three characteristics: stick to the pan, float, hard to flush).
    • Psychiatric: mood? Sleep? Eating disorder screen — intense fear of gaining weight (see corrections); “Do you try to lose weight by vomiting, laxatives or exercise?”
    • Lifestyle: diet and exercise — “Can you describe your diet for me?” (one recall: the patient was taking smaller portions; another: she had a professional dietician). “Do you do any exercise?” (one recall: the patient was a ballet dancer; in another, she was preparing for the Olympics).
    • Respiratory: haemoptysis — “Have you ever coughed up blood?”
    • Autoimmune: rash, joint pain.
    • Medications, drugs, alcohol, substances.

Case 3: Recall Versions and Diagnosis

Version 1 findings: small diet; exercise 8 hours per day.

Diagnosis: “The most likely cause of your weight loss is the excessive amount of exercise, together with the changes in your diet.”

Differentials to mention: malignancy; infections; in a young woman — eating disorders and major depressive disorder; endocrine; GI; autoimmune; medications and substances.

Very old version of this recall: Pancoast tumour (with PEFE findings and other supporting features).

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: history-taking-expert (Australian clinical supervisor, AHPRA-aligned) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The notes use 'Cushing disease' as the generic differential for weight gain with rashes/striae.
Use 'Cushing syndrome' as the differential label. Cushing disease refers specifically to ACTH excess from a pituitary adenoma; Cushing syndrome is the umbrella term (including the most common Australian cause - exogenous corticosteroids), which is what the screening questions are targeting.
✏️ In the Weight Loss section the eating disorder screen is written as 'Do you have an intense fear of losing weight?'
The correct screening question is an intense fear of GAINING weight or becoming fat (DSM-5 criterion for anorexia nervosa). The Weight Gain section words it correctly; the Weight Loss section reverses it and would confuse candidates and patients.
✏️ The diagnosis is delivered as 'a condition called FLD or NASH', and NASH is presented as a bedside diagnosis.
Terminology is outdated: since the 2023 international Delphi consensus (adopted in Australian hepatology practice, GESA), NAFLD/NASH have been renamed MASLD (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease) and MASH. Additionally, MASH/NASH (steatohepatitis) is a histological diagnosis and cannot be declared from history and examination alone - at the bedside say 'fatty liver disease', and reserve MASH for biopsy-confirmed disease.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add the fatty liver work-up and management plan expected in Australian practice (RACGP/GESA): confirm with liver ultrasound, exclude other causes (hepatitis B and C serology, iron studies, consider autoimmune/metabolic screens), assess metabolic risk (HbA1c or fasting glucose, lipids, BP) and stratify fibrosis risk with FIB-4 (refer to hepatology if indeterminate/high). Management is lifestyle-first: 5-10% weight loss, exercise, and alcohol within NHMRC guidelines (no more than 10 standard drinks/week and no more than 4 on any day).
⭐ Define clinically significant unintentional weight loss for the Weight Loss case: loss of more than 5% of body weight over 6-12 months warrants investigation. Add a baseline investigation panel to the notes (FBE, ESR/CRP, UEC, LFTs, TSH, HbA1c, coeliac serology, HIV and hepatitis serology if risk factors, urinalysis, CXR, and age-appropriate cancer screening) so candidates can answer the investigations follow-up question.
⭐ For the ovarian cancer case, add the Australian next steps after delivering the provisional diagnosis: CA-125 plus pelvic/transvaginal ultrasound as initial investigations and urgent referral to gynaecology/gynae-oncology; note that population screening for ovarian cancer is NOT recommended (RACGP Red Book), and that the symptom cluster of persistent bloating, early satiety, pelvic pain and urinary frequency/urgency in a woman over 50 should prompt investigation.
⭐ Add interpretation anchors for the PEFE findings so they are usable: BMI 38 = class II obesity (obesity is BMI 30 or more), and waist circumference thresholds for increased metabolic risk are 94 cm or more in men and 80 cm or more in women (with 102/88 cm indicating substantially increased risk). The recall's WC of 91 cm should be interpreted against the patient's sex.
⭐ Structure improvement: the OCR'd handwritten mind-map page duplicated the typed history flow and has been merged into the Case 2 history section rather than reproduced verbatim; attach the source images (git_2026_weight_cases_p*.png) beside the matching sections, and add per-case timing guidance (about 6 minutes history, 2 minutes diagnosis and differentials) to mirror AMC station timing.
