Appears in 60%+ of AMC Clinical exams

Study Priority: CRITICAL - Practice 10-15 times before exam

Why High-Yield: Rash and skin lesion presentations appear in 50-60% of AMC Clinical exams, testing systematic skin examination, lesion description framework, and recognition of emergency dermatology (meningococcal rash, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, cellulitis). SECTION 1: DERMATOLOGY HISTORY (4 minutes)

Opening Statement (30 seconds)

Word-for-word script:

"Good morning/afternoon, I'm Dr [Your Name], one of the doctors here at Young District Hospital. Before we start, can I please confirm your name and date of birth? Thank you. I understand you've come in today with concerns about a skin problem. I'd like to ask you some questions about this to understand what's happening, and then with your permission, I'll examine the affected area. The whole consultation should take about 8 minutes. Is that okay with you?"

[Pause for patient consent - essential for dermatology examinations]

"Can I also confirm - do you have any concerns about me examining your skin today? Some areas may require removing clothing, and I can arrange a chaperone if you'd prefer."

Focused History - SKIN RASH Framework

Purpose: Systematic approach to differentiate the Top 15 dermatological presentations (eczema, psoriasis, cellulitis, melanoma, viral exanthem, drug eruption, tinea, scabies, shingles, acne, rosacea, contact dermatitis, urticaria, impetigo, meningococcal rash)

S - SITE (Location & Distribution)

"Where did the rash/lesion first appear?"

  • Why ask: Helps narrow differentials immediately
    • Face → Rosacea, acne, SLE, seborrhoeic dermatitis
    • Flexures (elbows, knees) → Eczema, psoriasis
    • Trunk → Drug eruption, viral exanthem, pityriasis rosea
    • Dermatomal → Shingles (herpes zoster)
    • Hands/feet → Contact dermatitis, fungal, hand-foot-mouth disease
    • Interdigital → Scabies (web spaces), tinea pedis

"Has it spread? Where to? How quickly?"

  • Rapid spread (hours) → Meningococcal (EMERGENCY), urticaria, cellulitis
  • Slow spread (days-weeks) → Psoriasis, eczema, tinea
  • Centrifugal (from trunk outward) → Viral exanthem

Differential Clue:

  • Single site, not spreading → Localized: melanoma, BCC, SCC, dermatofibroma
  • Multiple sites symmetrically → Systemic: drug eruption, viral, psoriasis
  • Dermatomal distribution → Shingles (⭐⭐⭐ - very common in AMC exams)

K - KIND/CHARACTER (Morphology)

"Can you describe what it looks like?"

Use patient-friendly terms, then translate to medical:

Patient Description Medical Term Common Causes
"Flat red patches" Macules Viral exanthem, drug eruption
"Raised bumps" Papules Psoriasis, lichen planus
"Fluid-filled blisters" Vesicles (<5mm) Shingles, eczema, contact dermatitis
"Larger blisters" Bullae (>5mm) Pemphigus, bullous pemphigoid, Stevens-Johnson
"Pus-filled spots" Pustules Acne, impetigo, folliculitis
"Scaly patches" Plaques Psoriasis, eczema, tinea
"Lumps under skin" Nodules Melanoma, lipoma, cyst

⚠️ CRITICAL QUESTION:

"Does the rash blanch when you press on it?" / "If I gave you a glass to press on it, does it go white?"

  • Blanches → Most rashes (vasodilation - benign)
  • Non-blanching → EMERGENCY - Meningococcal septicaemia, vasculitis, thrombocytopenia

I - ITCH (Pruritus)

"Does it itch? How severe - mild, moderate, or really bothering you?"

Differential by Itch Severity:

VERY ITCHY (patient can't sleep):

  1. Scabies (⭐⭐) - Worse at night, interdigital, family contacts
  2. Eczema (⭐⭐⭐) - Flexural, dry skin, childhood onset
  3. Urticaria (⭐⭐) - Migratory wheals, each lesion <24 hours
  4. Contact dermatitis (⭐⭐) - New exposure, localized to contact site

MODERATELY ITCHY:

  1. Psoriasis (⭐⭐⭐) - Can be itchy, but less than eczema
  2. Tinea (⭐⭐) - Ring-shaped, expanding border
  3. Pityriasis rosea - "Herald patch" then Christmas tree pattern

MINIMAL/NO ITCH:

  1. Acne (⭐⭐) - Tender, not itchy
  2. Rosacea (⭐) - Facial flushing, burning sensation
  3. Melanoma (⭐⭐⭐) - Usually asymptomatic (may bleed or itch if advanced)
  4. Drug eruption (⭐⭐⭐) - Variable, can be very itchy or not

N - NEW Exposures/Triggers

⚠️ MOST IMPORTANT QUESTION for Narrowing Differentials

"Have you started any new medications in the last 2-4 weeks?"

  • Drug eruption (⭐⭐⭐ - very common)
    • Common culprits (Australia): Antibiotics (penicillin, sulfonamides), anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, phenytoin), allopurinol, NSAIDs
    • Timeline: Usually 7-14 days after starting drug
    • EMERGENCY signs: Mucosal involvement (mouth, eyes) → Stevens-Johnson syndrome

"Any new soaps, detergents, creams, perfumes, jewellery?"

  • Contact dermatitis (⭐⭐)
    • Nickel (belt buckles, jewellery), fragrance, preservatives
    • Occupational: hairdressers (dyes), builders (cement), healthcare workers (latex, chlorhexidine)

"Anyone else at home or close contacts with similar rash?"

  • Scabies (⭐⭐) - Highly contagious
  • Viral exanthem (⭐⭐) - Measles, rubella, chickenpox
  • Impetigo (⭐⭐) - School children, contact sports

SECTION 2: SKIN EXAMINATION (4 minutes)

The 5 Ps Framework Applied to Dermatology

1. POSITION (30 seconds)

"Please could you stand up / sit on the examination couch with good lighting."

  • Full body examination (melanoma screening, widespread rash): Patient in underwear
  • Localized rash: Expose affected area PLUS adjacent areas to assess distribution
  • Good lighting: Natural light best (windows), or bright overhead light

2. GENERAL INSPECTION (30 seconds)

Distribution pattern (CRITICAL for differentials):

Pattern Common Conditions
Symmetrical (bilateral) Eczema (flexures), Psoriasis (extensors), Drug eruption, Viral exanthem
Asymmetrical (one-sided) Shingles (dermatomal), Cellulitis, Tinea corporis
Localized (single lesion) Melanoma, BCC, SCC, Dermatofibroma
Flexural (skin folds) Eczema, Candida, Inverse psoriasis
Extensor surfaces Psoriasis (elbows, knees)
Dermatomal Shingles (unilateral, follows dermatome)
Photo-distributed (sun-exposed) Rosacea (face), SLE (malar rash), Polymorphic light eruption
Interdigital (between fingers/toes) Scabies (web spaces), Tinea pedis

3. PALPATION (1 minute)

"I'm going to gently feel the rash/lesion now."

  • Temperature:
    • Warm/hot → Infection (cellulitis, abscess)
    • Normal → Most rashes (eczema, psoriasis, drug eruption)
  • Tenderness:
    • Tender → Cellulitis (⭐⭐⭐), abscess, shingles
    • Non-tender → Eczema, psoriasis, most rashes
  • Texture:
    • Smooth → Macules (flat), urticaria (wheals)
    • Rough/scaly → Psoriasis (silvery scale), eczema (dry scale), tinea
    • Firm/hard → Melanoma (invasive), SCC, BCC
    • Soft → Lipoma, seborrhoeic keratosis

⚠️ BLANCHING TEST (CRITICAL)

"I'm going to press on the rash with this glass slide."

Technique: Use glass slide or clear plastic ruler, press firmly

  • Blanches (goes white) → Vasodilation (benign - most rashes)
  • Non-blanching (stays red/purple) → EMERGENCY
    • Meningococcal septicaemia (⭐⭐⭐ - DON'T MISS)
    • Vasculitis (Henoch-Schönlein purpura)
    • Thrombocytopenia (petechiae)

LESION DESCRIPTION Framework: "ABCDEFG"

For ANY discrete lesion (especially suspicious pigmented lesions), describe systematically:

A - ASYMMETRY

"Is the lesion symmetrical or asymmetrical?"

  • Symmetrical → Benign (seborrhoeic keratosis, naevus)
  • Asymmetrical → Melanoma (⭐⭐⭐ - one half doesn't match other half)

B - BORDER

"Is the border regular (smooth, well-defined) or irregular (jagged, notched, blurred)?"

  • Regular, well-defined → Benign (naevus, BCC initially)
  • Irregular, jagged, notched → Melanoma (⭐⭐⭐)

C - COLOR

"Is the color uniform (one color) or varied (multiple colors)?"

  • Uniform (one color) → Benign naevi usually uniform brown
  • Varied (multiple colors - red, white, blue, black) → Melanoma (⭐⭐⭐ - HIGHLY suspicious)

D - DIAMETER

"Measure the lesion - is it >6mm?"

  • <6mm → Usually benign (but small melanomas exist)
  • >6mm → Increased suspicion for melanoma (⭐⭐⭐)

Rule: "Larger than a pencil eraser" (6mm)

E - EVOLUTION (MOST IMPORTANT FEATURE)

"Has the lesion changed over time - size, shape, color, symptoms?"

  • CHANGING lesion → MELANOMA (⭐⭐⭐ - most important single feature)
    • New lesion in adult >40 years
    • Existing mole changing over weeks-months
  • Stable for years → Benign naevus, seborrhoeic keratosis

F - FEEL (Texture)

"What does it feel like - smooth, rough, scaly, hard?"

  • Smooth: Naevus (mole), dermatofibroma, lipoma
  • Rough/scaly: Solar keratosis (actinic keratosis - pre-malignant), SCC (⭐⭐⭐), Psoriasis
  • Hard/firm: Melanoma (invasive), SCC, BCC
  • Soft: Lipoma, skin tag

G - GENERAL DISTRIBUTION

"Is it a single lesion or multiple lesions?"

  • Solitary: Melanoma (⭐⭐⭐ - "ugly duckling sign"), BCC, SCC
  • Multiple similar lesions: Naevi (moles - most people have 10-40), Seborrhoeic keratoses (elderly)
  • Dermatomal: Shingles (clustered vesicles in dermatome)

SECTION 3: TOP 15 DERMATOLOGY PRESENTATIONS

1. ECZEMA (Atopic Dermatitis) ⭐⭐⭐

Frequency: Appears in 15-20% of AMC Clinical exams

Clinical Features:

  • Distribution: Flexures (antecubital, popliteal fossae), neck, wrists, ankles
  • Morphology: Erythematous papules/plaques, dry scaly skin, excoriations (scratch marks)
  • Symptoms: VERY itchy (worse at night), chronic relapsing
  • Atopic triad: Eczema + asthma + allergic rhinitis (hay fever)

Management (eTG 2024):

1. Emollients (MOST IMPORTANT):

  • "Moisturize, moisturize, moisturize" - apply liberally 3-4 times daily
  • Australian PBS-subsidized: QV Cream, Cetaphil, Sorbolene with 10% glycerine
  • Use 250-500g per week for adult

2. Topical corticosteroids:

  • Mild (face, children): Hydrocortisone 1% (OTC)
  • Moderate (body, adults): Betamethasone valerate 0.05% (Betnovate) - PBS
  • Potent (severe flares): Mometasone furoate 0.1% (Elocon) - PBS
  • Apply ONCE daily to active lesions for 5-7 days

3. Antihistamines (for itch):

  • Sedating (night): Promethazine (Phenergan) 25mg nocte - PBS
  • Non-sedating (day): Cetirizine 10mg daily - OTC

2. PSORIASIS ⭐⭐⭐

Frequency: Appears in 15-20% of AMC Clinical exams

Clinical Features:

  • Distribution: Extensor surfaces (elbows, knees), scalp, sacrum, nails
  • Morphology: Well-demarcated erythematous plaques with silvery-white scale
  • Symptoms: Mildly itchy (less than eczema), chronic relapsing
  • Nail changes (50%): Pitting, onycholysis, oil drop sign

Management (eTG 2024):

1. Topical therapies (first-line):

  • Potent corticosteroids: Betamethasone dipropionate 0.05% (Diprosone) - PBS
  • Vitamin D analogues: Calcipotriol 0.005% (Daivonex) - PBS, apply BD
  • Combination: Calcipotriol/betamethasone (Daivobet, Enstilar foam) - PBS

2. Systemic therapies (severe, >10% BSA):

  • Methotrexate 10-25mg weekly + folic acid 5mg next day - PBS
  • Acitretin (retinoid) 25-50mg daily - PBS

3. Biologics (severe, failed conventional):

  • TNF-alpha inhibitors: Adalimumab (Humira), Etanercept (Enbrel) - PBS (Authority)
  • IL-17 inhibitors: Secukinumab (Cosentyx), Ixekizumab (Taltz) - PBS (Authority)

3. CELLULITIS ⭐⭐⭐

Frequency: Appears in 20-25% of AMC Clinical exams

Clinical Features:

  • Definition: Acute bacterial infection of dermis and subcutaneous tissue
  • Common organisms: Streptococcus pyogenes, Staphylococcus aureus
  • Portal of entry: Tinea pedis (toe web - most common in Australia), trauma, ulcer

⚠️ CRITICAL DIFFERENTIAL - DON'T MISS:

1. Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT)

  • Similar presentation: Red, swollen, painful leg
  • Differences: DVT usually calf tenderness, recent immobility/surgery/long flight
  • Work-up: Wells score, D-dimer, Doppler ultrasound
  • TREAT cellulitis first if uncertain (antibiotics safe), then investigate DVT

2. Necrotizing Fasciitis (EMERGENCY)

  • Red flags: Pain out of proportion, rapidly progressive, skin necrosis (purple/black), crepitus (gas in tissues), systemic toxicity (shock)
  • Management: URGENT surgical debridement + IV antibiotics + ICU

Management (eTG 2024):

ORAL (mild cellulitis, outpatient):

  • Cephalexin 500mg QID for 5-10 days - PBS
  • OR Dicloxacillin 500mg QID - PBS

IV (severe cellulitis, admission):

  • Flucloxacillin 2g IV Q6H PLUS Benzylpenicillin 1.2g IV Q6H

Adjunctive:

  • Elevate affected limb
  • Mark border with pen (date and time) - track progression
  • Treat portal of entry: Tinea pedis - Clotrimazole 1% cream BD for 4 weeks

4. MELANOMA (Suspicious Pigmented Lesion) ⭐⭐⭐

Frequency: Appears in 20-25% of AMC Clinical exams

Why High-Yield: Australia has the HIGHEST melanoma incidence globally (1 in 17 Australians by age 85)

Risk Factors (Australia-specific):

  • Sun exposure: Outdoor work (farmers, builders), childhood sunburns
  • Fair skin: Fitzpatrick types I-II (burn easily, tan poorly)
  • High UV index: Australia has highest UV globally
  • Family history: FAMMM syndrome (familial atypical mole melanoma)
  • Multiple naevi: >50 moles

Management (Australian context - eTG 2024):

1. Suspicious lesion (in primary care):

  • URGENT REFERRAL to dermatology/skin cancer clinic
  • 2-week rule: Suspected melanoma should be seen within 2 weeks

2. Confirmed melanoma:

  • Wide local excision (WLE): Margin depends on Breslow thickness
    • <1mm: 10mm margin
    • 1-2mm: 10-20mm margin
    • >2mm: 20mm margin
  • Sentinel lymph node biopsy: If Breslow >1mm or ulcerated

3. Surveillance (high-risk patients):

  • 3-monthly skin checks for first 2 years
  • 6-monthly for next 3 years
  • Annual lifelong

☀️ PREVENTION (CRITICAL in Australia):

SunSmart Campaign (5 S's):

  1. Slip on sun-protective clothing
  2. Slop on SPF 50+ sunscreen (reapply every 2 hours)
  3. Slap on wide-brimmed hat
  4. Seek shade (especially 10am-3pm)
  5. Slide on sunglasses (UV protection)

UV Index: Summer in Australia = 10-14 (extreme) - even on cloudy days

5. SHINGLES (Herpes Zoster) ⭐⭐⭐

Frequency: Appears in 15-20% of AMC Clinical exams

Clinical Features:

  • Distribution: DERMATOMAL (follows sensory dermatome), UNILATERAL (doesn't cross midline) - pathognomonic
  • Morphology: Grouped vesicles on erythematous base ("dew drops on rose petal")
  • Timeline:
    • Day 0-3: Dermatomal pain (before rash)
    • Day 3-5: Vesicles appear
    • Day 5-10: Pustules → crusting
    • Day 10-21: Heals

⚠️ OPHTHALMIC ZOSTER (V1 dermatome) - EMERGENCY

Features: Forehead, upper eyelid, tip of nose (Hutchinson's sign)

Complications: Keratitis, uveitis, glaucoma, BLINDNESS

Management:

  • Start antivirals IMMEDIATELY (don't wait for ophthalmology)
  • URGENT same-day ophthalmology referral

Management (eTG 2024):

1. Antivirals (MOST IMPORTANT - start <72 hours from rash):

  • Valaciclovir 1000mg TDS for 7 days - PBS
  • OR Famciclovir 500mg TDS for 7 days - PBS
  • Reduces PHN risk by 50%

2. Analgesia (PHN prevention):

  • Paracetamol 1g QID, Ibuprofen 400mg TDS
  • Neuropathic pain: Gabapentin 300mg nocte, titrate up - PBS (Authority)

3. Post-herpetic Neuralgia (PHN) - if pain >3 months:

  • Gabapentin or Pregabalin (first-line)
  • Amitriptyline 25-75mg nocte
  • Lignocaine 5% patches (Versatis) - PBS

💉 PREVENTION - Shingles Vaccine:

Shingrix (recombinant):

  • Recommended for all adults ≥50 years
  • 2 doses (0, 2-6 months)
  • 90% effective at preventing shingles
  • NOT free (~$560 for 2 doses) - patient pays
  • IS free for immunocompromised ≥18 years (NIP from Feb 2023)

❌ COMMON IMG MISTAKES

  1. ❌ Using "diaper rash" instead of "nappy rash"
    • Australian term: Nappy rash (infants). American term "diaper" immediately flags you as foreign graduate
  2. ❌ Missing meningococcal septicaemia in non-blanching rash
    • CRITICAL: Always perform glass test/blanching test. Non-blanching purpura + fever = meningococcal until proven otherwise. DON'T MISS - fatal within hours if untreated
  3. ❌ Not asking about new medications (drug eruption)
    • Drug eruptions very common (especially antibiotics, allopurinol, anticonvulsants). Always ask: "Any new medications in last 2-4 weeks?"
  4. ❌ Poor lesion description - not using systematic framework
    • MUST use ABCDEFG for pigmented lesions (melanoma screening). Examiners expect systematic approach (not "looks suspicious")
  5. ❌ Not examining whole body for melanoma
    • Melanoma can occur anywhere (including scalp, soles, genitals). Full skin examination = patient in underwear, check all areas including back, scalp
  6. ❌ Using non-Australian drug names
    • WRONG: Acetaminophen, lidocaine, epinephrine, albuterol. CORRECT: Paracetamol, lignocaine, adrenaline, salbutamol
  7. ❌ Not safety-netting for melanoma
    • MUST tell patient: "Return if this lesion changes in size, color, shape, or starts bleeding." Melanoma is #1 medicolegal risk in Australian dermatology
  8. ❌ Forgetting Australian sun exposure context
    • Australia has highest UV index globally (8-14 in summer). MUST ask: "Do you work outdoors? Do you use sunscreen?" Sun protection advice is EXPECTED in every Australian dermatology consult
  9. ❌ Not recognizing tinea pedis as cellulitis portal of entry
    • Tinea pedis (athlete's foot) = most common cause recurrent cellulitis in Australia. If cellulitis → MUST examine between toes for tinea. MUST treat tinea to prevent recurrent cellulitis
  10. ❌ Confusing eczema distribution (flexural) with psoriasis (extensor)
    • Eczema: FLEXURES (antecubital, popliteal). Psoriasis: EXTENSORS (elbows, knees). Getting this wrong = instant loss of examiner confidence
  11. ❌ Not offering chaperone for intimate skin examinations
    • MUST offer chaperone for: Breast examination, genital examination, buttock examination. Medicolegal requirement in Australia
  12. ❌ Not documenting penicillin allergy reaction type
    • MUST ask: "What happened when you took penicillin?" (rash vs anaphylaxis). True allergy vs intolerance affects antibiotic choice for cellulitis

SAFETY-NETTING (Dermatology-Specific Red Flags)

General Safety-Netting Statement:

"Please call 000 or return to ED immediately if you develop:
• Non-blanching rash (purple spots that don't go white when pressed with a glass) with fever
• Rapid spread of redness (over hours, not days)
• Blistering in mouth, eyes, or genitals
• Facial swelling or difficulty breathing (allergic reaction)
• Severe pain out of proportion to rash appearance
• Black or dead-looking skin
• High fever (>38.5°C) with rash
• Confusion or severe headache with rash"

Lesion-Specific Safety-Netting:

Melanoma/Suspicious Lesion:

"Please return urgently if this spot:

  • Changes in size, shape, or color
  • Starts bleeding or becomes painful
  • Develops a new dark area
  • Any new spots appear that look different from your other moles"

Cellulitis:

"Please return to ED if:

  • The redness is spreading (I've marked the border - take a photo to compare)
  • You develop fever or feel very unwell
  • The area becomes more painful or you develop blisters
  • No improvement after 48 hours of antibiotics"

Shingles:

"Please return urgently if:

  • The rash involves your eye or the tip of your nose (risk of vision loss)
  • You develop severe headache or confusion
  • The rash spreads widely beyond the original area"

SUMMARY - HIGH-YIELD EXAM TIPS

Top 5 Dermatology Presentations (AMC Clinical):

  1. Eczema (⭐⭐⭐): Flexural, itchy, emollients + topical steroids
  2. Psoriasis (⭐⭐⭐): Extensor, plaques, silvery scale
  3. Cellulitis (⭐⭐⭐): Red, hot, tender leg - oral cephalexin or IV flucloxacillin
  4. Melanoma (⭐⭐⭐): ABCDEFG, Australia highest rates globally, 2-week rule
  5. Shingles (⭐⭐⭐): Dermatomal, unilateral vesicles, antivirals <72 hours

Must-Know Frameworks:

  • SKIN rash: Site, Kind, Itch, New exposures
  • ABCDEFG lesion description: Asymmetry, Border, Color, Diameter, Evolution, Feel, General distribution

⚠️ Emergency Dermatology (DON'T MISS):

  1. Meningococcal rash: Non-blanching purpura + fever
  2. Stevens-Johnson syndrome: Mucosal involvement, blistering
  3. Necrotizing fasciitis: Pain out of proportion, crepitus
  4. Ophthalmic zoster: V1 dermatome, Hutchinson sign (tip of nose)
  5. Anaphylaxis: Urticaria + angioedema + respiratory distress

Australian Context MUST-MENTION:

  • Sun protection advice (every dermatology consult)
  • Melanoma screening (Australia highest rates)
  • Tinea pedis as cellulitis portal of entry
  • Australian drug names (paracetamol, lignocaine)
  • PBS medications (affordable treatment options)

Practice Priority:

  • 10-15 practice sessions before AMC Clinical
  • Focus on: Melanoma (ABCDEFG), cellulitis vs DVT, eczema vs psoriasis distribution, shingles dermatomal pattern
  • Word-for-word scripts: Opening statement, sun protection advice, safety-netting
