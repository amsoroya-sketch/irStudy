Approach to Tiredness — History Taking Framework

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med workshop 2 - 2026 / CLASS 4 TIREDNESS (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: general_practice

Overview and Exam Format

Tiredness/fatigue is a common, deliberately vague AMC cluster presentation. In the current face-to-face format the station usually gives a Physical Examination From Examiner (PEFE) component, so history taking may be compressed to about 4 minutes, with roughly 1 minute for diagnosis and differentials. In the longer 7-minute history version there is time for a good closure. The core task is to systematically rule out the large list of differential diagnoses for tiredness.

Differential Diagnosis Groups (HEMIADCOP2)

Common causes include lifestyle/psychosocial factors (depression, anxiety), viral/post-viral infection (long COVID, EBV) and sleep-related disorders (obstructive sleep apnoea). Serious not-to-miss causes are malignancy and infection. Pitfalls include pregnancy (morning sickness, breast tenderness, missed period), early menopause, endocrine causes (Cushing syndrome, hyperaldosteronism) and medications (e.g. sedating antihistamines for hay fever).

The updated mnemonic HEMIADCOP2 is used in place of the older HEMIFADOICP:

  • H — Hepatitis, Haemochromatosis
  • E — Endocrine (diabetes mellitus, thyroid)
  • M — Malignancy (leukaemia, lymphoma)
  • I — Infections (viral, and STI e.g. HIV)
  • A — Anaemia; Autoimmune (SLE, RA, PMR)
  • D — Drugs/medications; Depression and psychiatric conditions
  • C — Cardiopulmonary (heart failure, COPD); Coeliac/bowel disease (rule out IBD and bowel cancer)
  • O — OSA; Occupation (night-shift work)
  • P — Period (pregnancy in young women; menopause in older women)

Sequence of History Taking

  1. Introduction and open-ended question
  2. Explore the complaint
  3. Rule out differentials
  4. Good closure if a 7-minute history; if only 4 minutes, closure time is limited

Opening for a 50-year-old with tiredness: ID check by examiner, hand hygiene, sit down, introduce self, then open question (“how can I help you, Marion?”) and “tell me more about your tiredness.” Acknowledge and empathise with concerns (e.g. a patient worried about lymphoma because of an affected cousin), build rapport and reassure while explaining the need to ask further questions.

Exploring the Complaint

Tiredness is vague — clarify what the patient means before proceeding. Four domains to characterise it:

  • Describe: Low in energy? Sleepy (sleep disorder)? Difficulty concentrating (anxiety)? Tired on walking/exertion (heart failure)? Feverish (infection)?
  • Timing: When did it start? On and off (e.g. morning tiredness in OSA) or constant? Getting worse?
  • Alleviating/aggravating factors: Anything better or worse?
  • Effect on life: Impact on work, family and function (very important — these are chronic presentations).

Ruling Out Differentials — Screening and Key-Point Questions

Use screening questions to find positive groups, then key-point questions for red flags. Examples:

  • Hepatitis: jaundice, dark urine, pale stool.
  • Haemochromatosis: skin tanning, family history of liver disease.
  • Endocrine — thyroid: weather/temperature preference, cold or heat intolerance, constipation, diarrhoea, dry skin, hair loss. Diabetes: polyuria, polydipsia.
  • Malignancy: loss of weight (LOW), loss of appetite (LOA), night sweats, lumps and bumps, family history of cancer; screening (mammogram, bowel cancer screening).
  • Infection: fever/chills, rashes, flu-like symptoms, sore throat; sexual history for STI; recent travel.
  • Anaemia: exertional shortness of breath, palpitations. Autoimmune: joint pain, rash (SLE/RA), shoulder/hip girdle pain (PMR), headache (temporal arteritis).
  • Drugs/Depression: medications, smoking, alcohol; mood, stressors (e.g. recent bereavement).
  • Coeliac/bowel: diarrhoea, greasy stool, blood in stool. Cardiopulmonary: chest pain, leg swelling, cough.
  • OSA: snoring, witnessed apnoeas. Occupation: shift work, driving (for OSA and stress assessment).
  • Period: LMP, menorrhagia (young women); last period, hot flushes, night sweats (older women).

Closure

If time permits (6–7 minutes), complete SADMA (smoking, alcohol, drugs/medications, allergies), past medical history and family history (cancer, haemochromatosis, thyroid disorder, autoimmune disease, diabetes).

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The screening line implies bowel cancer screening becomes relevant at age 50 (“have you done your bowel cancer screening as a 50-year-old”).
Since July 2024 the National Bowel Cancer Screening Program (NBCSP) offers free iFOBT from age 45, and people aged 45–49 can opt in; eligibility now runs 45–74. Update the teaching so candidates offer/enquire about screening from age 45.
✏️ Alcohol is listed only as a generic closure question with no quantification.
Use Australian NHMRC guidance as the benchmark: no more than 10 standard drinks per week and no more than 4 on any one day. Quantify intake in standard drinks so alcohol as a fatigue cause (and dependence) can be assessed.
