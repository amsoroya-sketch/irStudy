Red Eye (v2) - Viral Conjunctivitis Not Responding to Antibiotic Drops

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med workshop 2 - 2026 / CLASS 3 OPHTHALMOLOGY (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: ophthalmology

Scenario

A man presents with a red eye that has not improved despite five days of antibiotic drops. His three-year-old son had similar symptoms nine days ago that resolved spontaneously; the child attends childcare. A photograph is provided on the stem (nominally viral conjunctivitis, though the appearance can mimic other causes). Tasks: take a history for five minutes; give the diagnosis and differentials; and outline a management plan for viral conjunctivitis.

History Structure

Open with an open-ended question and respond to the concern, then explore the complaint across redness, watery discharge and grittiness.

Redness: one or both eyes; eyeball itself or eyelids; timing; aggravating/relieving factors.

Eyelid questions: swelling and redness; discharge (present - clear and watery); itch.

Eye questions: vision problems, photophobia, floaters, pain on eye movement.

Systemic: feeling weak and tired; sick contacts (his son, now recovered); ask all red flags.

Findings and Diagnosis

Discharge is watery and clear; there is a burning/aching pain looking straight ahead (conjunctivitis can cause burning pain); paracetamol helps the pain; he feels weak; no pain on eye movement; no foreign body; no outdoor activity. With a clear sick contact, typical symptoms, no red flags, clear discharge and failure of antibiotic drops, the diagnosis is viral conjunctivitis.

Note on treatment already tried: in Australia the common over-the-counter antibiotic eye drop is Chlorsig (chloramphenicol). If asked which drop was used, it is almost always chloramphenicol.

Explanation

Explain that viral conjunctivitis is inflammation of the surface covering of the eye caused by a virus. Supporting features: the son is unwell and attends childcare, and there is clear watery discharge.

Management of Viral Conjunctivitis

Viral conjunctivitis is self-limiting and usually settles within one to two weeks; it does not need antibiotics (which will not help, and have already been tried).

Symptomatic treatment:

  • Cool compresses to ease burning and inflammation.
  • Paracetamol for pain/burning.
  • Artificial tears / lubricating drops.

Reducing spread (key part of management): frequent hand washing; do not transfer the hand from the affected eye to the other eye; avoid rubbing the eyes; do not share towels; consider staying off work/school until the discharge settles.

Safety-netting / red flags: return to the GP or Emergency Department if severe pain, visual disturbance or photophobia develops (which may indicate a secondary bacterial infection or a more serious cause).

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The notes state chloramphenicol (Chlorsig) is 'our first line treatment of conjunctivitis' generally.
In current Australian practice most acute infective conjunctivitis is self-limiting and topical antibiotics are NOT routinely first-line. Chloramphenicol may modestly shorten bacterial conjunctivitis but is unnecessary for viral or mild cases (Therapeutic Guidelines). Frame chloramphenicol as an option for bacterial conjunctivitis, not as first-line treatment for conjunctivitis in general - and explicitly not indicated for the viral case in this station.
✏️ The stem reasoning 'antibiotic drops not working rules out bacterial conjunctivitis'.
Failure of chloramphenicol does not exclude bacterial conjunctivitis (bacterial conjunctivitis is often self-limiting and may resolve without antibiotics, or the organism may be resistant/atypical). Non-response should broaden the differential to viral, allergic, chlamydial/gonococcal, or a non-conjunctival cause (keratitis, iritis) rather than simply 'ruling out bacterial'.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add the mandatory red-eye step of testing and recording visual acuity, and 'never pad a discharging eye' (Murtagh golden rules) - neither is mentioned.
⭐ Add contact-lens screening and the rule that a contact-lens wearer with a red eye must be assumed to have microbial keratitis until proven otherwise and referred - do not label as simple conjunctivitis.
⭐ Reference Therapeutic Guidelines (eTG) explicitly stating infective conjunctivitis is usually self-limiting, to justify the symptomatic (not antibiotic) approach and to counsel the patient that stopping the antibiotic drops is appropriate.
⭐ Strengthen public-health advice consistent with childcare exclusion guidance (highly contagious; exclude until discharge resolves), and add adenoviral epidemic keratoconjunctivitis (pre-auricular node) as a differential given the childcare contact.
