Diplopia - Binocular Diplopia from Diabetic Cranial Neuropathy

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med workshop 2 - 2026 / CLASS 3 OPHTHALMOLOGY (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: ophthalmology

Scenario and Overview

Originally a physical-examination station, now also delivered as history taking. The pivotal concept is separating the two types of diplopia:

  • Uniocular (monocular) diplopia: the problem is within one eye; double vision persists when the normal (fellow) eye is covered and resolves only when the affected eye is covered. Arises from the eye itself.
  • Binocular diplopia: each eye alone is normal, but the eyes fail to move together, producing two images. Double vision disappears when either eye is covered.

The Cover Test

On examination, with both eyes open and diplopia present, cover one eye and then the other. If diplopia persists with one eye covered, it is uniocular (e.g. cataract in that eye). If diplopia disappears whenever either eye is covered, it is binocular (an ocular-movement problem). In a history-only station, ask the patient directly: does the double vision improve when you cover or close either eye? A scripted answer (e.g. "it gets better when I cover my right eye but not my left") is used to convey the finding.

Differential Diagnosis

Monocular diplopia (one eye): astigmatism, refractive error, cataract, keratoconus, lens problems.

Binocular diplopia (movement problem) - localise along the pathway:

  • Brain: tumour, multiple sclerosis, stroke, head injury.
  • Cranial nerves III, IV, VI: e.g. diabetic (microvascular) cranial neuropathy.
  • Neuromuscular junction: myasthenia gravis (often first presents with end-of-day diplopia).
  • Muscle: traumatic (orbital blow-out fracture), orbital tumour, thyroid eye disease (thyroid ophthalmopathy).
  • Other: ophthalmoplegic migraine, orbital myositis.

Focused Diplopia History

  • Which direction of gaze makes it worse (the affected muscle's field of action)?
  • In which gaze are the two images closest together?
  • Is the image separation vertical, horizontal or oblique (helps localise the muscle/nerve)?
  • Is there a corrective/compensatory head position (common in strabismus and long-standing diplopia)?
  • Is it worse at distance or near?
  • Is there associated pain? Painful diplopia shifts the case into the 'sore eye' category and broadens the differential to orbital pathology.

Systems review: diabetes (retinopathy, cranial neuropathy, maculopathy), vascular disease and hypertension, neurological symptoms, muscle weakness and fatigue (myasthenia gravis), and facial/head trauma.

Localising rule of thumb: vertical image separation points to CN III (and CN IV); horizontal separation points to CN VI.

History Taking Structure

Introduce yourself and gain consent. Explore onset/timing (myasthenia gravis is typically intermittent and worse at the end of the day), progression, and the cover question (better when either eye is covered = binocular). Clarify diplopia vs blurring; image separation (vertical/horizontal); gaze that worsens it; distance vs near; and alleviating factors (rest, end-of-day worsening, specific head positions).

Then screen eyelid and eye questions (swelling, discharge, itch, photophobia, floaters, pain on movement, redness). Most exam cases are binocular (movement) problems.

Structured Differential Questioning

  1. Brain (tumour, MS): headache, early-morning vomiting, weight loss, focal neurological deficit (weakness, numbness, gait or speech problems).
  2. Cranial nerves (diabetes, hypertension): when diagnosed, treatment (e.g. metformin, perindopril), adherence, follow-up, complications (peripheral numbness, chest pain).
  3. Myasthenia gravis: ptosis (eyelid drooping), speech problems, difficulty with facial movements, worse at end of day.
  4. Muscle/thyroid: weather (temperature) preference, family history of thyroid disease; and trauma to head/eyes.

If extra time - monocular screening: cataract (haloes around lights, faded colours), glaucoma (peripheral visual-field loss - e.g. bumping into people, turning the head to read road signs), macular degeneration (central field loss - difficulty seeing a face, wavy lines on an Amsler grid). Finish with past medical and family history (SADMA).

The patient reports diplopia that improves on covering either eye (binocular). She is on metformin, non-compliant, and not attending follow-up; urine shows 3+ glucose. The physical-examination card (when provided) documents diplopia worse in one gaze that improves on covering an eye, i.e. binocular, with signs of a sixth-nerve palsy. Diagnosis: binocular diplopia due to diabetic cranial (microvascular) neuropathy.

Explanation to patient: binocular diplopia is a movement problem of both eyes; in her case high blood sugar has damaged the nerves supplying the eye muscles. Reasons: the cover test (double vision improves when either eye is covered - a movement problem), known poorly controlled diabetes with 3+ glucosuria, and non-adherence to treatment. Differentials offered: brain tumour, multiple sclerosis, head trauma, stroke, myasthenia gravis, muscle/orbital problems (blow-out fracture), thyroid eye disease, and monocular causes (cataract, astigmatism, refractive error).

A newer version reportedly combines tremor, diplopia and a gait problem; diplopia recurs frequently in the exam.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The notes state 'six nerve palsy was looking for near Medial rectus problem', conflating CN VI with the medial rectus.
CN VI (abducens) supplies the LATERAL rectus (abduction). A sixth-nerve palsy causes a convergent squint with HORIZONTAL diplopia that is worse on distance viewing and on gaze towards the affected side. The MEDIAL rectus is supplied by CN III. Correct the muscle-nerve mapping: CN III = medial/superior/inferior rectus and inferior oblique (+ levator, pupil); CN IV = superior oblique; CN VI = lateral rectus.
✏️ CN IV (trochlear) palsy is omitted from the vertical-diplopia teaching, which attributes vertical diplopia only to CN III.
Add CN IV palsy: superior oblique weakness causes vertical (and torsional) diplopia that is worse on looking down and in (e.g. reading, going down stairs), with a characteristic compensatory head tilt away from the affected side. Vertical diplopia is therefore CN III OR CN IV.
✏️ Diabetic third-nerve palsy is grouped generically without noting the pupil.
Emphasise the pupil: microvascular (diabetic/hypertensive) CN III palsy is typically PUPIL-SPARING, whereas a PUPIL-INVOLVING (fixed dilated) painful third-nerve palsy suggests a compressive lesion - classically a posterior communicating artery aneurysm - which is a neurosurgical emergency requiring urgent CT/CT-angiography. This distinction is a critical must-not-miss safety point missing from the case.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add the red flag: sudden binocular diplopia, or diplopia with headache, altered consciousness, or a pupil-involving CN III palsy, warrants urgent neuroimaging to exclude aneurysm, stroke or space-occupying lesion.
⭐ Specify the ocular-motility examination (test the 'H' pattern), cover/uncover and alternate-cover tests, plus assessment of visual acuity, pupils (RAPD), lids (ptosis, fatigability with sustained upgaze or ice-pack test for myasthenia) and thyroid eye signs (proptosis, lid lag).
⭐ Add internuclear ophthalmoplegia (failure of adduction with contralateral abducting nystagmus) as a brainstem/MS cause of horizontal diplopia in younger patients - relevant to the MS differential.
⭐ Reference the Murtagh diplopia diagnostic strategy (probability, serious-not-to-miss and pitfalls) and add the diabetes 'four questions' (control, adherence, complications, follow-up) with HbA1c and eye-clinic review as the management plan for a microvascular cranial neuropathy.
