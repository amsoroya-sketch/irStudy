Ureteric Colic (Version 2) - CT Explanation and Management

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / Class 8 ureteric case (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: urology

Clinical Anatomy - Where the Stone Is Determines the Pain

While the stone is in your kidney, you have loin pain. When it comes down, the pain starts dragging down towards your groin.

  • Stones in the upper two-thirds of the ureter give flank pain and renal angle tenderness.
  • As they progress to the distal third of the ureter, the patient feels pain in the groin, labia or scrotum - which is why the external genitalia should still be examined.
  • Stones near the bladder can produce LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms) very similar to prostate disease.

Signs

  • Renal angle tenderness may be positive.
  • Urine may be smoky; if frankly bloody it may be pinkish or contain blood streaks. Sometimes urine becomes dark enough that hepatitis (bilirubinuria) must be excluded.

Investigations

Although a plain X-ray and ultrasound can be done, non-contrast CT KUB is the gold standard. Urine microscopy and culture, looking for infection, is essential.

Murtagh: if the stone is small (up to about 5 mm) it will usually pass; if larger than 5 mm there is a chance it will not pass and intervention may be needed. If the patient passes the stone, they should retrieve it and present it for analysis.

Strain the urine: "I want you to strain your urine. If you're passing a stone, I want you to catch it and bring it in for analysis." Finding a calcium oxalate versus a uric acid stone makes a big difference to long-term management.

CT appearance: anything calcified is very bright ('shiny') on CT. A stone just leaving the kidney, one in the mid-ureter, or one sitting at the vesico-ureteric junction (VUJ) just before entering the bladder can all be identified. Remember a 3-4 mm stone appears very small - not a huge bright circle.

Urine dipstick: in renal colic you typically have blood - usually microscopic haematuria (often just 1-2+ blood), sometimes macroscopic - because the stone abrades the ureteric wall as it moves. Dipstick may also reveal nitrites, and urine microscopy/culture may confirm a urinary tract infection. This is a vital differentiator that significantly alters management.

Step 1 - Rule Out Infection FIRST (Critical Step)

If asked to manage renal colic in the emergency department or general practice, do not lead with painkillers - always first ask: does this patient have a UTI?

  • Look for: fever, chills/rigors, dysuria, pyuria on urine testing.

Why it matters: an obstructing stone + UTI leads to urosepsis: rapid pus accumulation (pyonephrosis), clinical deterioration, and poor response to antibiotics unless the obstruction is relieved.

Fever in renal colic = RED FLAG - think infection + obstruction - this is an emergency.

Guideline note (from the source's quoted reference, OCR restored): an obstructed infected system can result in catastrophic gram-negative sepsis, often requiring intensive care admission and occasionally causing death. Early recognition is key, hence the necessity of urine dipstick analysis in ALL patients with suspected renal colic. Occasionally the dipstick is negative because of complete obstruction of infected urine - so in any patient with fever, tachycardia or hypotension, infection must still be considered. Patients with minimal renal reserve (pre-existing kidney disease) should also be sent to hospital for treatment.

Step 2 - Imaging

Gold standard: non-contrast CT KUB - most sensitive and specific; detects stone size, location and obstruction.

Other options:

  • X-ray KUB: useful for radio-opaque stones; can be done the same day as the CT to provide a baseline for follow-up comparison (avoids repeat CT radiation).
  • Ultrasound KUB: preferred when avoiding radiation, especially in pregnancy. Limitation: less sensitive - may miss small ureteric stones.

Special case - pregnancy: avoid radiation (no CT or X-ray); use ultrasound. Diagnosis may be difficult due to anatomical displacement in late pregnancy. Always do a pregnancy test in females of childbearing age.

Step 3 - Blood Tests / Renal Function

Order urea, electrolytes and creatinine (UEC) to detect renal impairment - severe renal impairment may require urgent intervention instead of conservative management. Also consider FBE, eGFR, serum calcium and serum uric acid.

Red Flags (DO NOT MISS)

These patients must NOT be managed conservatively:

  • Signs of infection - fever, chills - think urosepsis
  • Single kidney / kidney transplant - high risk; protect remaining function
  • Anuria / urinary retention - "not passing urine" = obstruction until proven otherwise
  • Renal failure - abnormal UEC (raised creatinine)
  • Bilateral ureteric stones / bilateral or severe obstruction

Clinical rule: red flag present = urgent urology referral + decompression - NOT "go home and wait".

Conservative Management (Stable, No Red Flags)

Initial priorities: exclude infection (urine dipstick +/- culture) and check renal function (UEC).

Indication for conservative management: stone approximately 5-7 mm or smaller (likely to pass).

  1. Analgesia: first line NSAIDs (e.g. ibuprofen); escalate to opioids if severe.
  2. Antiemetics: e.g. ondansetron (wafers).
  3. Medical expulsive therapy: tamsulosin 400 micrograms daily - relaxes the ureter and helps stone passage. Side effect: postural hypotension (warn the patient to stand up slowly). Note: off-label and non-PBS for this indication (cost approximately $35 at time of publication); most useful for distal stones larger than 5 mm; if unsure, seek urology advice, and confirm doses in the Australian Medicines Handbook before prescribing.
  4. Hydration: do NOT advise drinking excessive water during acute pain - recommend adequate hydration only. Excessive fluid intake does not increase the likelihood of stone passage.

Strain the Urine

Ask the patient to pass urine into a container and strain it through a sieve to catch the stone. Purpose: confirms passage (avoids repeat imaging) and allows stone analysis, which guides prevention strategies.

Safety Netting and Follow-Up

Safety netting - give clear return instructions. Return immediately (arrange emergency assessment) if:

  • Fever (possible infection)
  • Persistent or uncontrolled pain despite analgesia in the outpatient setting
  • Persistent nausea and vomiting
  • Not passing urine
  • Any signs of sepsis

Follow-up schedule:

  • Review within 48-72 hours for test results and symptom control (and at any stage if symptoms worsen).
  • Repeat imaging at 2-4 weeks to assess whether the stone has passed: X-ray KUB if the stone was radio-opaque on initial imaging (avoids repeat CT radiation); CT KUB if radiolucent.
  • If the stone has not passed on follow-up imaging, arrange non-acute urology assessment (within 4 weeks).

Large Stone (>5-7 mm) or Failed Conservative Treatment - Urology Options

Refer to urology. Options:

  • Laser lithotripsy: laser breaks the stone into smaller fragments so it can pass.
  • Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL): sound waves from outside the body break the stone.
  • Ureteroscopy (retrograde stone removal): a fine scope is passed via the urethra to grab, fragment and remove the stone - suited to distal stones near the bladder.
  • Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL): surgical removal for large/complex stones or stones higher in the collecting system.

Long-Term Prevention (After the Acute Episode)

  • Stone analysis (retrieved stone).
  • Blood tests: calcium, uric acid, PTH (parathyroid hormone) - a full metabolic work-up is indicated for recurrent stones.
  • 24-hour urine: calcium, oxalate, citrate (and urine pH).
  • Lifestyle: good hydration (keep urine light in colour), low-salt diet.
  • Tailor prevention to the stone type.

Common Misconceptions (Exam Traps)

  • WRONG: "Drink lots of water to pass the stone" - it worsens the pain and does not increase the likelihood of passage.
  • WRONG: "Dietary calcium is always bad" - not true; do not routinely restrict dietary calcium.
  • CORRECT: focus on oxalate, uric acid and the underlying metabolic cause.
  • Exam tip: the stem often does NOT give the stone size - you must say "management depends on the size", then cover both arms: small = conservative; large = urology referral.

OSCE Approach - Opening the Station

  • Haemodynamic stability: temperature is the most important vital sign (a patient with a stone AND a fever is critical).
  • Open-ended questions: introduce yourself; "How can I help you?"; "Can you tell me more about the pain?" Patient: "Yeah doctor, I'm having pain. It's not getting better. They've done a scan - just tell me what's happening in the scan."
  • Initial statement: acknowledge, empathise, reassure - "I'm so sorry that you're going through such a bad experience. I'm here to help you. I'll explain what's happening."

Explaining the CT Scan to the Patient

  • "This scan is a CT scan, which is like a slice view of your abdomen."
  • "Here I can see your hip bones and surrounding muscles."
  • "This round structure is your bladder."
  • "There are two tubes that connect your kidneys to your bladder - these are called ureters."
  • "You can see this shiny white spot - this is a stone."
  • "The stone is located in the ureter at the point where it joins the bladder" - the vesico-ureteric junction (VUJ); the anatomical name is not important for the patient.

Diagnosis Explanation to the Patient

"You have a condition called ureteric colic. That means you have a kidney stone that you are passing, and because of the pressure on the wall of the tubes, it causes the pain you are having. We've done the CT scan and I can see the stone, but your symptoms were also suggestive:

  1. The site of your pain started high up when the stone was in your kidney, and moved down as the stone entered the tubes.
  2. The pain is colicky - coming in waves.
  3. I found blood in your urine."

Management Counselling Script

"The management depends on the size of the stone. But before we decide on treatment, the most important thing is to make sure you don't have an infection along with the stone. So I'll do a urine test - a dipstick, and send it for culture - because an infection with a stone can become serious quickly. I'll also do a blood test to check your kidney function - urea, electrolytes and creatinine - which helps guide how we manage you safely. I'll also quickly ask if you've ever had kidney problems or surgeries, or if you have only one kidney."

If the stone is small (less than about 5-7 mm) - good chance it will pass on its own, so we treat conservatively:

  1. "Pain relief first - usually anti-inflammatory medications like ibuprofen. If that's not enough we can step up to stronger pain relief (a medication containing codeine)."
  2. "Medication for nausea if needed - ondansetron wafers."
  3. "A medication called tamsulosin, which relaxes the tube and helps the stone pass more easily. One small thing - it can cause a drop in blood pressure when you stand up, so if you're standing from a seated position or getting out of bed, do it slowly."

When to come back immediately: "You develop a fever; the pain is not improving; or you're unable to pass urine - these could be signs of complications." Also: "I'd like you to strain your urine to catch the stone when it passes so we can analyse what type it is. I'll review you again in about 2-3 days."

If the stone is larger than about 5-7 mm - less likely to pass on its own; refer to a urologist. Options to explain: laser lithotripsy (break the stone into smaller pieces so you can pass it); percutaneous nephrolithotomy (surgery by the urologist if the stone is higher in the ureter); shock wave lithotripsy - ESWL (sound waves from outside the body); or retrograde ureteroscopic stone removal ("we insert a small telescope through the water passage, grab the stone, crush it and remove it - since your stone is very close to the bladder").

"Overall this is a common condition, and most stones - especially smaller ones - pass on their own with the help of medications. We'll keep a close eye on you and make sure you're safe throughout. Do you have any questions or concerns?"

Long-term: "Once this stone is treated we'll focus on preventing future stones: good hydration (urine light in colour), a low-salt diet, stone analysis if you catch it, blood tests for calcium, uric acid and a hormone called PTH, and urine checks for things like pH and oxalate - this helps us understand why the stone formed and reduce the chance of it happening again."

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The transcript says 'Dipstick may reveal nitrates' and 'ondacetron wafers'.
The dipstick analyte is 'nitrites' (urinary nitrate is reduced to nitrite by gram-negative bacteria); the antiemetic is 'ondansetron'. Corrected in the structured sections.
✏️ The case states 'We recommend it as a routine use of 400 micrograms in a conservatively managed renal colic' for tamsulosin, implying routine medical expulsive therapy for all stones.
Current Australian guidance (eTG; and the RACGP-style reference quoted in the case's own OCR excerpt) treats tamsulosin as OFF-LABEL and non-PBS for ureteric stones, to be CONSIDERED - particularly for distal ureteric stones larger than 5 mm - rather than used routinely for all conservatively managed colic. Contemporary trial evidence (e.g. SUSPEND) shows no benefit for small (<5 mm) stones. The dose (tamsulosin 400 micrograms orally daily, up to 4 weeks) is correct; doses should be confirmed in the Australian Medicines Handbook before prescribing.
✏️ The case says 'if the stone is small, the size is five millimeters, it'll pass', presenting spontaneous passage as certain.
Stones up to about 5 mm pass spontaneously in most but not all cases (roughly 70-90% for <5 mm, falling with increasing size and more proximal location) - hence the mandatory follow-up imaging at 2-4 weeks and safety netting described elsewhere in the same case. Counsel probability, not certainty.
✏️ Description of ureteroscopy as inserting a tube 'through your penis'.
Use anatomically accurate, patient-friendly and sex-neutral language: the scope is passed via the urethra ('the water passage') into the bladder and ureter. Corrected in the counselling script.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add explicit management of the septic obstructed kidney for completeness: resuscitation, blood and urine cultures, IV broad-spectrum antibiotics per eTG sepsis guidance, and URGENT decompression by retrograde ureteric stent (JJ stent) or percutaneous nephrostomy - antibiotics alone fail while the system remains obstructed. This is the natural examiner follow-up question to the 'fever = red flag' teaching point.
⭐ Add NSAID cautions to the analgesia teaching: avoid or use NSAIDs carefully in renal impairment, the elderly, and volume-depleted patients (risk of acute kidney injury), and note the effective Australian first-line option of indomethacin 100 mg per rectum for acute colic per eTG - students should be able to name a specific agent, dose and route.
⭐ Add the AAA trap to this version as well: in patients over 50 with a first presentation of loin-to-groin pain, a leaking abdominal aortic aneurysm must be excluded before diagnosing ureteric colic - one advantage of CT KUB is that it also demonstrates the aorta.
⭐ Add '000' to safety-netting language for the Australian context: patients sent home on conservative management should be told to call 000 or attend the nearest emergency department immediately if they develop fever/rigors, uncontrollable pain or vomiting, or stop passing urine.
⭐ Add a stone-type prevention table to the long-term section (calcium oxalate: adequate dietary calcium, reduce oxalate-rich foods and salt; uric acid: urinary alkalinisation and consider allopurinol; struvite: treat infection; cystine: high fluid intake and specialist referral) - the case sets up stone analysis as pivotal but never states what changes as a result.
