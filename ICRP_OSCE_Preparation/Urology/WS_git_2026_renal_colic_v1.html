Renal Colic (Version 1) - History, Examination and Diagnosis

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — GIT 2026 / Class 8 ureteric case (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: urology

Examiner's Cue and Task Approach

Why does the examiner tell you the pain has already improved? Because you are not expected to offer a painkiller in this station. Your task is to take a history, perform (request) the examination, and make the diagnosis.

Step 1 - Initial Approach

  • Haemodynamic stability: check vital signs first. Temperature is the most important - a patient with a stone AND a fever is critical (obstructed infected system until proven otherwise).
  • Open-ended questions: introduce yourself; "How can I help you?"; "Can you tell me more about the pain?"
  • Initial statement and observed behaviours: address the patient's concern - acknowledge, empathise, reassure: "I'm so sorry to hear that. I'll just ask you a few questions."

Step 2 - Pain Assessment (SIQORAA)

  • S - Site: "Tell me where you feel the pain?" Patient: "It started on the right side of my abdomen, but then it moved towards my groin. So now I'm feeling it in the lower part of my tummy." (In some versions of the case the pain starts in the flank.)
  • I - Intensity: "On a scale of 1-10, 1 being the least and 10 the highest, how bad is your pain?" Patient: "A good eight or nine out of 10."
  • Q - Quality: sharp versus dull ache - you expect a sharp, severe, stabbing pain.
  • O - Onset/Timing: "When did it start?" Patient: "A couple of hours ago." On/off or constant? Patient: "It comes in waves - I suddenly have the pain, then I'm okay, then it comes again." Is it getting worse?
  • R - Radiation: "Is it travelling anywhere?" Patient: "The pain is kind of radiating down to my groin now."
  • A - Alleviating/Aggravating factors: "Does anything make the pain better or worse?" Patient: "I haven't noticed anything making it better or worse." Also ask: "Is your pain worse after eating?" (rules out peptic ulcer disease, pancreatitis, cholecystitis); "Is there any specific position in which the pain is better or worse?"; "Have you taken any painkillers, and did they help?"

Step 3 - Ruling Out Differentials

1. General GI screen:

  • Nausea and vomiting? (upper GI)
  • Diarrhoea or constipation? (lower GI)
  • Bloating / passing gas or wind? (mid GI)
  • Fever? (screening for UTI symptoms in a patient who may have a stone)

2. KUB (kidneys, ureters, bladder) screen:

  • Dysuria - "Do you have any pain or burning on passing urine?"
  • Frequency - "Are you passing urine more frequently?" Patient: "Yeah, I'm going to the toilet more often."
  • Blood in the urine? Patient: "Yeah, I'm seeing blood in my urine."
  • Any dribbling?
  • Sudden urge to pass urine (urgency)?
  • Any personal or family history of kidney stones?

Teaching note: you can go into further stone risk questions (hydration, diet), but that is likely too much at this point because the differentials have not yet been ruled out.

3. Liver and gallbladder:

  • Yellow discolouration of the skin (jaundice)?
  • Dark urine / pale stools?
  • "Do you have any history of cramps or pain on the right side after eating?" Patient: "Yeah."
  • Travel history?

4. Hernia: any swelling or lump in the groin (relevant because the pain radiates to the groin)?

5. Pelvic/genital: "Have you noticed any swelling or redness in your private parts?" If time permits, work down the structured approach to right-sided abdominal pain, including pelvic causes (e.g. mittelschmerz in females).

If you have time: ask the red-flag questions, previous surgeries, and complete the remaining history.

PEFE (Physical Examination Findings from Examiner)

  1. General appearance: Is my patient drowsy? Is my patient dehydrated? (Ask the "pilot" opening question.)
  2. Vitals: BP, pulse rate (rate and rhythm, postural drop), respiratory rate, temperature - do not forget the temperature.
  3. Abdominal examination:
    • Inspection: Is the abdomen moving symmetrically with respiration? Any Cullen sign or Grey Turner sign? Any distension?
    • Palpation: tenderness or mass on superficial/deep palpation? Guarding? Rigidity? Rebound tenderness?
    • Percussion: any percussion tenderness?
    • Auscultation: bowel sounds present or absent?
    • Inguinal area: any lumps or swellings?
    • DRE (digital rectal examination).
  4. Special test: renal angle (costovertebral angle) tenderness.
  5. Office test: urine dipstick - blood is typically present in ureteric colic. (A pelvic examination can also be requested.)

Examination Findings and Interpretation

On physical examination the important points are: temperature is normal, there is tenderness in the lumbar (renal angle) region, and the urine dipstick shows blood.

Is this pyelonephritis? No - there are no nitrites and no white cells on the dipstick, and the patient is afebrile.

Teaching point (Murtagh): costovertebral angle tenderness is not specific for pyelonephritis. It also occurs in ureteric colic and in renal trauma - for example, a patient who fell off a bike with a kidney injury will also be tender there.

Diagnosis Explanation to the Patient

"Most likely you have a condition called renal colic, or kidney stones. This is most likely happening because of a stone in your kidney. The reason you are having the pain is that the stone has started moving, and because of the pressure it causes on the tubes draining your kidney, you are having this pain."

Reasons for the diagnosis:

  1. The site of your pain - it started in your flank and is now dragging down to your groin.
  2. The pain comes in waves (colicky), which is typical for this condition.
  3. You have blood in your urine.

Differential Diagnoses

  1. UTI - specifically pyelonephritis: "I was thinking about urinary tract infections, but more specifically the type of infection in your kidney that we call pyelonephritis."
  2. Kidney cancers.
  3. Liver and gallbladder problems: cholelithiasis, cholecystitis, hepatitis, liver cancer.
  4. Incarcerated/strangulated inguinal hernia.
  5. Appendicitis - because the pain drags towards the groin/right iliac fossa.
  6. Scrotal pathology - e.g. epididymo-orchitis (and testicular torsion in this age group).
  7. Other GI differentials: bowel cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, gastroenteritis.
  8. Masquerades: diabetic ketoacidosis in an older patient, herpes zoster (shingles).

Structure note: this is the same framework as the other right-sided abdominal pain cases - KUB differentials are simply moved to the top of the list, and because the pain starts in the right flank, liver, gallbladder and other right-sided structures are actively screened.

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: surgical-skills-expert (Australian surgical educator, FRACS) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The case states of the urine dipstick: 'You always have blood.' This overstates the finding - microscopic haematuria is absent in roughly 10-15% of confirmed ureteric stones.
Teach that haematuria (usually microscopic) is present in the large majority of ureteric colic cases and strongly supports the diagnosis, but a negative dipstick does NOT exclude a stone. Non-contrast CT KUB remains the definitive investigation if clinical suspicion is high.
✏️ The transcript says 'You don't have any nitrates' when interpreting the dipstick, and refers to 'epididymal ochitis' and 'mitchelmarsh pain' (OCR/transcription artifacts).
The correct dipstick analyte is 'nitrites' (bacterial reduction of urinary nitrate to nitrite suggests infection); the conditions are 'epididymo-orchitis' and 'mittelschmerz'. These have been corrected in the structured sections. Also note that an obstructed infected kidney can have a falsely negative dipstick because infected urine may not drain past a completely obstructing stone - fever with colic mandates urgent assessment regardless of dipstick result.
✏️ The differential list omits abdominal aortic aneurysm. Presenting a first episode of apparent 'renal colic' in a patient over 50 without mentioning ruptured/leaking AAA is a recognised and potentially fatal diagnostic trap.
In any patient over 50 years presenting with a first episode of loin-to-groin pain (especially with haemodynamic instability or absent haematuria), a leaking abdominal aortic aneurysm must be actively excluded before accepting a diagnosis of renal colic (Murtagh lists AAA as a classic renal colic mimic; this is also standard RACS/EMST teaching). Add abdominal palpation for a pulsatile expansile mass to the PEFE checklist.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add testicular torsion explicitly to the differential list and to the PEFE (scrotal examination) for males with loin-to-groin pain - torsion can present with abdominal/groin pain and is a surgical emergency with a 6-hour window; asking to examine the external genitalia is a scoring point in AMC clinical stations.
⭐ Add an investigations plan to close the station: urine dipstick + midstream urine microscopy/culture, FBC, UEC (creatinine/eGFR), serum calcium and uric acid, beta-hCG in women of childbearing age, and non-contrast CT KUB as the gold-standard imaging (ultrasound KUB first-line in pregnancy). The current version stops at diagnosis and DDx, but AMC examiners commonly ask 'what will you do next?'
⭐ Add an explicit red-flag box aligned with eTG/Murtagh: fever or rigors with colic (obstructed infected system - urosepsis risk, needs emergency decompression), solitary or transplanted kidney, anuria/urinary retention, bilateral stones, and rising creatinine - all require urgent hospital referral, not GP-level conservative care. Instruct patients that if severe deterioration occurs at home they should call 000.
⭐ Add Murtagh's diagnostic triad as a memory aid: intense loin pain radiating to the groin + microscopic haematuria = ureteric colic; pain comes in waves lasting about 30 seconds with 1-2 minute respite and usually settles within 8 hours - this directly matches the patient script and gives students a citable anchor.
⭐ Add brief analgesia teaching for the debrief (even though this station forbids offering analgesia): first-line for renal colic per eTG is an NSAID (e.g. indomethacin 100 mg rectally or IV/oral NSAID), with opioids reserved for refractory pain - students frequently default to opioids first, which loses marks.
