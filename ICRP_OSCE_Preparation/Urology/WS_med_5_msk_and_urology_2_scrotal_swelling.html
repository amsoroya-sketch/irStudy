Scrotal Swelling (Hydrocele)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med 5 MSK and urology / 5. urology (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: urology

Scrotal Swelling (Hydrocele)

Exam Stem

  • Your next patient is a 25 year old man who presents to you complaining of swelling in his scrotum.
  • Task:
    • Take a 6-minute history
    • Explain diagnosis and differentials

Differentials of Scrotal Swelling (Young Man)

Core Differentials

  1. Hydrocele (top differential – exam case)
  2. Testicular cancer
    • Must always be included
    • Occurs in 20–30 years and older age
    • Examiner will fail you if not ruled out

Other “-celes”

  • Varicocele
  • Spermatocele
  • Hematocele
  • Chylocele

Other Important Differentials

  • Epididymo-orchitis
  • Inguinal hernia (extending into scrotum)
  • Epididymal cyst

Key Concepts of Hydrocele

Causes

  • Primary (Idiopathic) – most common
  • Secondary causes (must be ruled out):
    • Trauma
    • Sexually transmitted infections
    • Cancers

History-Taking Structure

1. Opening Question

Example dialogue

“Can you tell me more about the swelling you’ve felt in your scrotum or private parts?”

Patient response example:

  • Duration: weeks to months
  • Noticed incidentally (e.g. shower)
  • Expresses anxiety

Doctor response

“I understand how concerning this can be. Let me ask you a few questions and examine you, and we’ll find the cause.”

2. Exploring the Presenting Complaint (Swelling)

Timing

  • When did it start?
  • How long has it been there?

Course

  • Constant or on and off?
  • Is it getting worse; Increasing in size?

Laterality

  • One side or both sides?

Pain

  • Painful to touch?

Do NOT ask about:

  • Consistency
  • Mobility
  • Upper border
  • Hard vs rubbery

    (these are examination findings, not history)

3. Aggravating / Alleviating Factors

  • Any change in swelling with position?
    • Worse on standing?
    • Better lying down?
  • Important for hernia

Ruling Out Differentials Systematically

A. Testicular Cancer (First Priority)

Ask about:

  • Weight loss
  • Appetite changes
  • Lumps or bumps elsewhere especially groin
  • Loss of sensation over scrotum (nerve invasion)

B. Epididymo-Orchitis

Local Symptoms

  • Redness on scrotum
  • Pain on scrotum
  • Heaviness / dragging sensation (non-specific)
  • Rashes
  • Ulcers

Penile Symptoms

  • Penile discharge

Urinary Symptoms

  • Dysuria (pain, burning, stinging)
  • Frequency (not polyuria)> do u pass urine more frequently

C. Sexual History (Very Important)

Structure

  1. Are you sexually active?
    • If no → ever been sexually active?
  2. Partners:
    • Stable relationship?
    • Ask even if in stable relationship: have you ever had Multiple partners?
  3. Are your partners, male or female?
  4. Protection:
    • Condom use
    • Safe sex
  5. Type of sex:
    • Vaginal
    • Oral
    • Anal
  6. Risk questions:
    • Sex under influence of alcohol/drugs
    • Previous STI diagnosis

Purpose

  • Epididymo-orchitis
  • Secondary hydrocele due to STIs

D. Trauma

  • Any trauma to scrotum or private parts?

2023 variant example

  • Kicked during football
  • Swelling developed weeks later

E. Hernia

  • Already asked: positional changes> does swelling worse on standing
  • Risk factors:
    • Heavy lifting
    • Long-term cough
    • Long-term constipation

Incarcerated Hernia Features (rule out)

  • Abdominal pain
  • Nausea
  • Vomiting
  • Bloating

F. Viral Orchitis

  • Fever
  • Chills
  • Flu-like symptoms
  • Any swelling on face?
  • Mumps (rare but classic)


  • End with closure

Old Case Variant (2019 Recall)

Key Findings

  • Multiple partners
  • No condom use
  • Previous STI
  • Penile discharge weeks ago
  • No current pain
  • No redness
  • No fever or chills

Diagnostic Reasoning

  • Differentials:
    • Epididymo-orchitis
    • Hydrocele
  • No pain / redness / fever → Hydrocele more likely
  • Epididymo-orchitis still remains a differential
  • No definitive diagnosis without examination

Explaining Diagnoses to Patient

Explaining Hydrocele

“You most likely have a condition called hydrocele. Hydrocele is a collection of fluid around the testis. It can happen due to sexually transmitted infections, trauma to the private parts, cancers, or sometimes we don’t find a cause.”

Explaining Epididymo-Orchitis

“Epididymo-orchitis is an infection of the testis and its tubes, usually caused by a sexually transmitted infection.”

2023 Variant Case

History

  • Swelling for 2 weeks
  • One side only
  • Sexual history normal
  • Condom use
  • No STIs
  • Trauma positive (football injury)
  • Initially painful → now painless
  • No redness
  • No systemic symptoms

Differentials

  • Hematocele (hematoma)
  • Hydrocele

Timing Logic

  • Immediate swelling after trauma → Hematoma
  • Delayed swelling after trauma → Hydrocele

Very Old Face-to-Face Exam Version

Your next patient is a 25 year old man who presented with scrotal swelling. On physical examination you can feel a 3cm mass, you can get above the mass, transillumination is positive, the left testis iss not palpable. Right testis normal

  • Explain the findings
  • Explain your Dx and DDx
  • Further investigation and management

Explaining Findings to Patient (Example Dialogue)

“I examined your scrotum to understand the swelling. I was able to feel a lump confined to the scrotum, meaning it’s not coming from the abdomen. When I shined a light through it, it lit up, which tells us it contains fluid. Because of the fluid, I couldn’t feel your left testis. These findings are consistent with a hydrocele.”

Investigations

1. Scrotal Ultrasound (First)

  • Investigation of choice

2. STI Screening

  • Chlamydia & Gonorrhea PCR (first-pass urine)
  • Offer full STI screen:
    • HIV
    • Hepatitis B
    • Syphilis

3. Tumor Markers

  • Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP)
  • LDH
  • Beta-HCG

Optional (Acceptable)

  • Urine MCS
  • ESR / CRP

Management of Hydrocele

Principle

  • Management depends on the cause

If Cancer

  • Surgery (orchidectomy)
  • ± Chemotherapy

If STI

  • Treat infection
    • Example: Chlamydia → Doxycycline 100 mg BD for 7 days

Idiopathic / Traumatic

  • Conservative management: if pain is not severe
    • Observe
    • Follow-up

If Symptomatic or Persistent

  • Aspiration: put a small needle in it and drain it out
    • Risk: recurrence
  • Surgical removal
    • For large or recurrent hydrocele

Additional

  • Patient education
  • Reading materials

Final Teaching Point

  • Very rare case now (<1%)
  • Included to complete urology package
  • Focus on justifying diagnosis, not memorising recalls

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: surgical-skills-expert (Australian surgical educator, FRACS) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The differential list for scrotal swelling omits testicular torsion, the single most important time-critical diagnosis for any scrotal presentation.
Always include testicular torsion as the must-not-miss differential for a swollen/painful scrotum - it is a surgical emergency requiring exploration within ~6 hours of onset to salvage the testis. This case is deliberately a painless chronic swelling so torsion is unlikely, but the candidate must be seen to consider and exclude it (sudden severe pain, high-riding testis, absent cremasteric reflex).
✏️ Testicular cancer age range is stated as 'Occurs in 20-30 years and older age', which is slightly narrow.
Peak incidence of testicular germ cell tumours is 20-40 years (with a second smaller peak in older men for lymphoma/seminoma). Phrase as 'most common solid malignancy in young men aged 20-40'.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add empirical management pathway for the epididymo-orchitis differential per Therapeutic Guidelines: if sexually acquired (younger men) treat for Chlamydia/gonorrhoea (ceftriaxone 500 mg IM stat plus doxycycline 100 mg BD for 10-14 days) and arrange contact tracing; if enteric organism likely (older men, urinary source) cover Gram-negatives. The case treats chlamydia with doxycycline 100 mg BD 7 days, which is correct for uncomplicated chlamydia but epididymo-orchitis needs the longer 10-14 day course.
⭐ Add the classic examination triad that clinches hydrocele and should structure the 'explain findings' task: swelling confined to scrotum, able to 'get above' it (excludes inguinoscrotal hernia), and transilluminates - all present in the RAG scrotal-lump comparison tables. Emphasise that a secondary hydrocele in a young man mandates scrotal ultrasound to exclude an underlying testicular tumour before reassurance.
⭐ Metadata to add: tag first-line investigation (scrotal ultrasound) and tumour markers (AFP, beta-hCG, LDH) and cross-link to testicular self-examination patient education.
