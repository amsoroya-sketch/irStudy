Haematuria with LUTS (Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med 5 MSK and urology / 5. urology (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: urology

BPH Hematuria

  • In GI workshop / medicine chapter three, dark urine was discussed.
    • Dark urine could be:
      • Hematuria
      • Or hepatitis-related causes
  • That dark urine cluster had a different structure.
  • This case is different: focus is red/pink urine → hematuria structure.

Case Stem

Your next patient is a 55 year old man who has presented to your General Practice complaining of red/Pink urine.

Task:

Take history for 6 minutes

(F2F exams: Ask physical examination form examiner )

Explain your diagnosis and differentials

Differentials for Hematuria

4.1 Morta’s list (high-yield)

1) Infection (top of list)

  • UTI can definitely cause hematuria:
    • Cystitis
    • Urethritis
    • Prostatitis
    • Pyelonephritis

2) Stones (KUB system)

  • “Stone anywhere in KUB system”:
    • kidney / ureter / bladder stone
  • Can cause hematuria

3) Cancers

  • “Cancers all over the tract”
    • Kidney cancer
    • Ureter cancer
    • Bladder cancer (especially bladder)

4) Rare differentials (mentioned)

  • Kidney infarction
  • Kidney vein thrombosis
    • rare, hard to ask history for

5) Glomerulonephritis

  • Any type of GN can cause hematuria
    • Includes PSGN
  • IgA nephropathy
    • discussed previously in dark urine case

4.2 Pitfalls / Often missed

Pseudo-hematuria

  • Example:
    • Beetroots
    • Too much beetroots → urine becomes reddish/pinkish

4.3 Prostate-related hematuria

  • BPH can cause hematuria
  • Prostate cancer can cause hematuria
  • Important point:
    • In a man >45 or >50, if dipstick shows blood:
      • Always ensure no prostate problem

4.4 Additional differentials listed

  • Trauma to:
    • abdomen / kidney / bladder / ureter / urethra
  • Bleeding disorders
  • Hemorrhagic cystitis (another infection type)
  • Exercise-induced hematuria
    • Not really RBCs → myoglobin
    • Can show as RBCs on dipstick
  • Radiation cystitis (mentioned “in ladies”)
  • Menstruation / periods (be careful in ladies)
  • HSP (part of criteria)
  • Polycystic kidney disease

5) Preferred Differential List (Simplified List to Write Down)

Write a good list quickly:

Top differentials

  1. Infections
    • cystitis / urethritis / pyelonephritis / prostatitis
  2. Cancers
    • “KUB cancer” (kidney / bladder / etc.)
  3. Stones
    • “KUB calculus”

Other differentials

  1. Glomerulonephritis
  2. Prostate problems
    • BPH + prostate cancer
  3. Exercise-induced
  4. Food / pseudo-hematuria
  5. Polycystic kidney disease
  6. Bleeding disorders / blood thinners
  7. Others (“and so on”)

History Taking

6.1 Open-ended Introduction

Start with:

  • “How can I help you today?”
  • “Tell me more about your red urine.”

Patient will say:

  • “I noticed my urine is red/pink.”
  • “I’m worried it’s blood.”

Address concern

  • Use your prepared reassurance statement (as you do in all cases).

6.2 Explore the Complaint – Timing

Ask:

  • When did it start?
  • How long has it been happening?
  • Is it on and off or every time u pass urine?
  • Is it getting worse?
  • Is this the first time or happened before?

6.3 Pattern of Red Urine (Similar idea to CCVO)

C1: Color

Ask:

  • “Is it red?”
  • “Is it pink?”
  • “Or is it dark cola color?”

(Here, patient confirms red.)

C2: Contents

Ask:

  • “Any clots in your urine?”
  • “Any pus or discharge?”

O: Odour

  • Can ask about bad smell (thinking infection)
  • Volume not important
  • Odour not that important

Most important pattern question

Ask:

  • Is the blood:
    • only at the beginning?
    • throughout the stream?
    • only at the end?

Important note

  • Nice question but not specific
  • Helps localize thinking:
    • Beginning → urethra / prostate (before bladder)
    • Throughout → bleeding lesion throughout (e.g. bladder cancer, kidney cancer)

Differential-Focused History

7.1 Prostate Differentials → LUTS (Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms)

Important prostate knowledge:

  • LUTS divided into 2 groups:
    1. Voiding / Obstructive symptoms
    2. Storage / Irritative symptoms

Key teaching point

  • Obstructive symptoms → usually prostate disease
  • Only irritative without obstructive → think bladder problem

7.2 Obstructive (Voiding) Symptoms

Must remember & ask a good number:

  1. Hesitancy
    • takes time to start passing urine
  2. Weak stream
  3. Dribbling
  4. Incomplete emptying
    • sensation bladder not fully emptied
  5. Straining
    • needs to push hard

Example question phrasing

  • “Do you have a weak stream?”
  • “Any dribbling?”
  • “Do you feel you have emptied your bladder incompletely?”
  • “Do you need to push or strain to pass urine?”
  • “Does it take long to start passing urine?”

(Ask most of them, not just one or two.)

7.3 Irritative (Storage) Symptoms

Irritative reminds of UTI-type symptoms:

  • urgency, frequency, suprapubic pain

Important prostate case key question:

Nocturia

Ask:

  • “Do you wake up in the middle of the night to pass urine?”

If yes → follow-up:

  • “How many times per night?”

Note:

  • Waking once may be normal in older men
  • Higher number = more severe prostate problem

Other irritative questions:

  • Frequency
    • “Do you need to pass urine more frequently?”
  • Urgency
    • “Do you feel a sudden urge to pass urine?”
  • Urge incontinence
    • “Do you leak any urine after that sudden urge?”

7.4 Missing urinary symptom (not in either list)

Not obstructive, not irritative:

  • Dysuria

(Keep it in mind.)

7.5 Cancer Questions (Prostate cancer / Kidney / Bladder cancers)

Same cancer screening questions:

  • “Have you lost weight lately?”
  • “Any changes in appetite?”
  • “Any lumps and bumps?”
  • “Are you feeling tired?”
  • Night sweats (optional)

Metastasis question (important):

  • “Have you noticed any bone pain?”
  • “Any back pain?”

    (bone metastasis discussed previously)

7.6 Infection Questions

Ask:

  • Fever / chills
  • Abdominal pain / flank pain
  • Dysuria:
    • “Any pain/burning/stinging when passing urine?”
  • Nausea/vomiting (for pyelonephritis if you want)

7.7 Renal Colic / Stones

Ask:

  • Past history / family history of kidney stones

7.8 Glomerulonephritis Group

Ask at least:

  • “Have you had any recent flu-like symptoms?”
  • “Any sore throats?”

This ticks off:

  • PSGN
  • IgA nephropathy
  • HSP

Optional extra:

  • Joint pain (SLE/others)
  • Rashes (if wanted, but don’t go dramatic)

7.9 Exercise-induced hematuria

Ask:

  • “Have you done any recent exercise, especially excessive exercise?”

7.10 Trauma

Ask:

  • “Any trauma to your abdomen/stomach/private parts?”

7.11 Pseudo-hematuria (Beetroots)

Ask:

  • “Have you eaten a lot of beetroots recently?”
  • “A large amount?”

(Note: one beetroot not enough, too much causes it.)

7.12 Polycystic Kidney Disease / CKD

Ask:

  • History of hypertension
  • Family history of kidney disease

(ticks off CKD + PCKD together)

7.13 Bleeding / Blood thinners

Ask:

  • “Are you on any blood thinners?”

    Patient may say: “I’m on aspirin.”

Also ask:

  • Family history of bleeding disorders
  • Easy bruising
  • Nosebleeds

7.14 Closure

Finish with:

  • Smoking
  • Alcohol
  • Drugs
  • Medications
  • Allergies

Also:

  • “Any other past medical history I need to know?”
  • “Any family history?”

History Findings

Patient tells you:

  • + Nocturia
  • + Urgency
  • + weak stream
  • Incomplete emptying of bladder
  • Dribling
  • On Aspirin and Perindopril
  • Some cases PMHx of Kidney stones

Important reassurance point

  • Multiple findings = don’t panic
  • It means you are ruling out differentials properly
  • You still know the likely diagnosis

9) Provisional Diagnosis (Based on History Only)

Version 1: No physical examination

Provisional diagnosis:

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)

(before prostate cancer because no red flags)

Reason:

  • No weight loss
  • No appetite loss
  • No lumps/bumps
  • No bone pain

Physical Examination Findings (Online Exam Screen Version)

  • On Abdo examination: Dullness in suprapubic area
  • On DRE: Enlarged prostate felt
  • Smooth surface and no nodularity
  • Normal consistency
  • Median sulcus felt
  • Urine dipstick: + RBC

Quick Comparison: BPH vs Prostate Cancer (DRE)

BPH

  • Enlarged prostate
  • Smooth
  • No nodules
  • Normal consistency
  • Median sulcus felt

Prostate cancer

  • Enlarged prostate
  • Nodular / irregular surface
  • Hard / stony consistency
  • Median sulcus not felt / obliterated

Explain Diagnosis to Patient (BPH Explanation)

Don’t say “BPH”

Say:

  • “Most likely you have a condition called benign prostatic hyperplasia.”

Explain what prostate is

  • “There is a gland below your bladder / at the bladder neck.”
  • “It produces part of your semen.”
  • “In your case, it has increased in size.”
  • “This is a normal change in older men / higher age groups.”
  • “It narrows your urinary tubes causing your symptoms.”

Reasons

  • Blood in urine
  • Wakes up at night
  • Sudden urge
  • Weak stream → obstruction
  • No cancer features
  • Large prostate felt on exam

Explain Differentials to Patient

First differential

  • Prostate cancer
    • prostate can enlarge in cancer
    • but exam does not suggest it
    • no weight loss

Other cancers

  • Kidney or bladder cancer
    • but no weight loss features

Infections

  • cystitis / pyelonephritis
    • but no fever/chills
    • no abdominal pain

Trauma

  • injury to kidney causing bleeding

Glomerulonephritis

  • inflammation of kidney:
    • IgA nephropathy
    • Henoch–Schönlein purpura
  • but no sore throat / flu-like symptoms

Exercise-induced hematuria

  • no heavy exercise

Pseudo-hematuria

  • not too many beetroots

Other differentials reminder list

  • blood thinners
  • kidney stones
  • full list covered earlier

14) Aspirin Question (Blood Thinners Discussion)

Q: Can aspirin give hematuria? Is it common?

  • Aspirin is an anti-platelet
  • It is not like:
    • warfarin
    • apixaban
  • Usually you don’t expect aspirin to cause that level of bleeding tendency
  • Still, one differential includes:
    • bleeding disorders / blood thinners

Second Version

Stem

Your next patient is a 49 year old man who has presented to your General Practice complaining of trouble passing urine.

Task:

  • Take history for 6 minutes, you will have a prompt time at minutes
  • Explain your diagnosis and differentials to the patient

Differentials that can cause trouble passing urine

  • Prostate (in old man)
  • Cancers (KUB tract, prostate etc.)
  • Infections
    • most common infection causing trouble passing urine: prostatitis
    • cystitis / urethritis also possible
  • Renal/ureteric colic:
    • patient feels urge, passes few drops, can be bloody

Extra addition due to vague complaint

  • Trouble passing urine can be interpreted as painful urination:
    • skin problems like herpes, balanitis, etc.

History Taking

1) Opening & Introduction

1.1 Start with an Open-Ended Question

  • Begin with a good open-ended question (even under exam stress).
  • Let the patient explain their main concern first.

Example dialogue

  • Doctor: “Hi, I’m one of the doctors here today. How can I help you?”
  • Patient: “I’m having difficulty passing urine.”

2) Explore the Complaint (Clarify What the Patient Means)

2.1 Key First Clarifying Question (Before Anything Else)

  • The first question is to clarify:

    “What do you mean by trouble passing urine?”
  • Do NOT jump straight into prostate questions (e.g., nocturia) without clarifying.

Example dialogue

  • Doctor: “When you say trouble passing urine, what exactly do you mean by that?”

2.2 Clarify the Type of Difficulty

Ask specifically:

A) Painful urination

  • “Is it painful to pass urine?”
  • “Is there burning or stinging when you pass urine?”

B) Obstruction / difficulty starting or passing urine

  • “Is it difficult to pass urine and you have to strain or push hard?”
  • “What do you mean by this problem?”

Example dialogue

  • Doctor: “Is it burning or stinging when you pass urine?”
  • Doctor: “Or is it that you have to strain and push hard to pass urine?”

3) Timing & Progression

3.1 Duration

  • “How long have you had this problem for?”

3.2 Pattern

  • “Is it on and off or does it happen every time you pass urine?”

3.3 Trend

  • “Is it getting worse?”

3.4 Aggravating/Relieving Factors

  • “Have you noticed anything that makes it better or worse?”

Given example response from patient

  • Difficult to pass urine
  • Has to push hard
  • Present for a few months
  • Happens every time
  • Getting worse
  • Nothing makes it better or worse

4) Prostate-Focused Questions (After Clarifying Complaint)

4.1 Main Point

  • After the initial exploration, start thinking prostate.
  • Ask a good number of:
    • Obstructive symptoms
    • Irritative symptoms

5) Obstructive Symptoms (Prostate)

Ask a good number of these:

5.1 Weak Stream

  • “Have you had a weak stream?”

5.2 Dribbling

  • “Any dribbling?”

5.3 Incomplete Emptying

  • “Do you feel that you’ve incompletely emptied your bladder after passing urine?”
    • Incomplete sensation

5.4 Hesitancy

  • “Do you feel it takes long to start passing urine?”

5.5 Straining

  • “Do you have to push hard or strain to pass your urine?”

6) Irritative Symptoms (Prostate)

6.1 Key Irritative Question: Nocturia (Must Ask)

  • “Do you wake up in the middle of the night to pass urine?”
  • If yes → quantify:
    • “How many times do you wake up?”
  • Patient: “Two to three times.”

Teaching point mentioned:

  • Nocturia is remembered as a risk factor for falls (from the falls cluster).

6.2 Other Irritative Symptoms

You can also ask:

  • Frequency: “Are you passing urine more frequently?” / “Are you going to the toilet more often than usual?”
  • Urgency: “Do you get that sudden urge to pass urine?”
  • Incontinence: “Have you ever leaked urine or leaked a few drops?”

7) Cancer Questions (Prostate Cancer + KUB Cancer)

Bladder cancer can cause obstruction and difficulty passing urine

7.2 Cancer Screening Questions

Ask:

  • Weight loss: “Have you lost weight lately?”
  • Appetite: “Have you noticed any changes in your appetite?”
  • Lumps/bumps: “Any bumps in your body?”
  • Tiredness
  • Bone pain/back pain: “Have you noticed any bone pain lately? Back pain?”

16.1 Main Diagnosis (Agreeing Component)

  • There is a component of BPH

16.2 Additional Possibility

16.3 Teaching Notes About Dysuria

  • Dysuria is not so famous and common in BPH
  • Some references mention dysuria can happen in prostate problems
  • But it is not a common feature

16.4 Problem in This Case Design

  • There is no urine dipstick
  • No physical examination findings (e.g., dipstick positive/negative)
  • Keep the level as:
    • “plus minus a possible urinary tract infection”

16.5 Prostatitis as a Differential

  • Prostatitis can also give the same picture
  • But usually associated with:
    • Fever
    • Chills
    • Abnormal risky sexual behavior suggesting STI
  • Patient has had symptoms for a while, so not an acute condition
  • Chronic prostatitis acknowledged, but:
    • Without physical examination, hard to confirm exact diagnosis

Explanation to Patient

17.1 Start with “Different Causes”

  • Tell patient symptoms can have different causes
  • You don’t have exam findings to confirm
  • But you believe it is:
    • Benign prostatic hyperplasia with a possible urinary tract infection

Example dialogue

  • Doctor: “Your symptoms can have different causes. I don’t have examination findings today to confirm it completely, but I believe you may have a condition called benign prostatic hyperplasia, with a possible urinary tract infection.”

18) Explaining BPH (How Lecturer Explains It)

18.1 What is the Prostate?

  • “Prostate is a gland located below your bladder.”
  • The urinary tube(s) cross through the prostate gland.

18.2 What’s Happening Here?

  • The prostate has enlarged in size
  • It obstructs/narrows the urinary tube
  • This causes trouble passing urine

Example dialogue

  • Doctor: “The prostate is a gland located below your bladder, and the urinary tube passes through it. In your case, the prostate has enlarged and it’s narrowing that tube, which makes it difficult for urine to pass.”

19) Linking BPH → UTI Risk (As Explained)

19.1 Key Mechanism

  • Enlarged prostate increases urine left behind in the bladder after urination
  • More urine staying in the bladder increases the risk of UTI

Example dialogue

  • Doctor: “This narrowing can increase the amount of urine that stays in the bladder after you pass urine, and that increases the risk of a urinary tract infection.”

20) Mentioning Other Differentials

20.1 Prostatitis (Why Less Likely Here)

  • No fever/chills
  • No deep pain around back passage
  • No risky sexual behaviour suggesting STI

20.2 Prostate Cancer / Other Cancers

  • Consider prostate cancer
  • Other urinary tract cancers:
    • Bladder (especially)
    • Kidney

20.3 Other Infections

  • Cystitis
  • Pyelonephritis

20.4 Kidney Stones

  • Kidney stones can cause difficulty passing urine “when you start passing them”

20.5 STIs (Example Mentioned)

  • Herpes can be very painful → difficulty passing urine

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: surgical-skills-expert (Australian surgical educator, FRACS) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The case attributes the haematuria to BPH once a smooth enlarged prostate is felt on DRE, treating BPH as a sufficient explanation.
Visible (macroscopic) haematuria in a man over 40 - and this patient is 55 with additional bladder-cancer risk - is bladder/urothelial cancer until proven otherwise and mandates urgent urology referral for cystoscopy PLUS upper-tract imaging (CT urography), regardless of a benign-feeling prostate. BPH is a diagnosis of exclusion for haematuria; a normal DRE does not exclude bladder cancer. This is the central safety point the station must not soften.
✏️ Exercise-induced haematuria is described as 'Not really RBCs -> myoglobin, can show as RBCs on dipstick', conflating two separate entities.
Exercise CAN cause genuine haematuria (true RBCs, usually transient). Separately, rhabdomyolysis causes myoglobinuria, which produces a dipstick POSITIVE for blood but with NO red cells on microscopy - it is not 'haematuria'. Keep these distinct: a positive dipstick with no RBCs on microscopy suggests myoglobin/haemoglobin, not exercise haematuria.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add the formal Australian haematuria work-up the history should lead into: urine microscopy/culture and urine cytology, renal-tract imaging (CT urography or renal-tract ultrasound), flexible cystoscopy, plus UEC, PSA and coagulation studies if on antiplatelets/anticoagulants - then explicit urology referral. This converts the history-only case into a defensible management pathway.
⭐ Broaden the risk-factor history: the case asks diet/beetroot and blood thinners but omits key bladder-cancer risks - smoking (the single biggest modifiable risk), occupational exposure (aniline dyes, rubber, hydrocarbons), prior pelvic radiotherapy/cyclophosphamide, and schistosomiasis travel history. Add these to strengthen the malignancy screen.
⭐ Add a validated LUTS assessment tool (IPSS - International Prostate Symptom Score) to quantify the obstructive/irritative symptoms the case elicits, and note dysuria is uncommon in pure BPH so its presence should prompt looking for concurrent infection or bladder pathology.
