Acute Gout (Monoarthritis) with Septic Arthritis Exclusion

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med 5 MSK and urology / 5. urology (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: urology

Gout

Your next patient is a 40 year old man who has presented to your general practice complaining of left knee pain for 2 weeks. He has had similar episodes before which subsided with no treatment. He has not fever and had no injuries recently. He is a known case of hypertension who is on hydrochlorothiazide. He does not smoke, but drinks 4SD daily.

  • On your examination his knee is swollen and red on inspection
  • On palpation the knee is warm and painful to touch
  • Temp 36.5

Task:

  • Explain your most likely diagnosis and differentials
  • Order investigations
  • Immediate management

2) Provisional Diagnosis + First Priority Differential

Most likely diagnosis: Gout

Most important differential to rule out

  • Septic arthritis
  • Because it can also present with:
    • swollen joint
    • red
    • warm to touch
    • painful joint

How you rule out septic arthritis in this case

  • Checking temperature / no fever
  • Making sure no infection signs

3) Key Teaching Point: Gout is not only in the big toe

  • Even if it’s not first metatarsophalangeal joint (big toe)
  • It can still be gout
  • Can happen in:
    • knee
    • ankle
    • other joints

4) Differentials for Knee Pain

  • Different versions of:
    • arthritis
    • malignancies
    • infections
    • “other things like bacosis and so on”
    • lumbar radiculopathy
    • PVD
    • DVT
    • “and so on”

5) Investigations + Diagnosis (Key Controversial Points)

Controversy Question 1

Does dramatic improvement with NSAIDs confirm gout 100%?

  • Example scenario:
    • swollen red painful toe
    • treated with indometacin (or another NSAID)
    • dramatic improvement
    • trigger like barbecue + steaks + beers

Answer: No, it does NOT confirm gout 100%

Controversy Question 2

Does high uric acid confirm gout?

Answer: No

  • Serum uric acid is important, but not diagnostic

What confirms gout

Joint aspiration → send for polarized microscopy

  • You must say:
    • “polarized microscopy”
  • Joint aspiration often done with ultrasound guidance

Is joint aspiration necessary for everyone?

Yes

  • At least once to confirm diagnosis
  • Once confirmed:
    • you don’t need to do it again

6) Risk Factors for Gout

Diet-related

  • Purine-rich foods
    • red meat
    • seafood (important: some AMC recalls give seafood, not red meat)

Alcohol

  • Beer is the worst

Sugary drinks

  • sugary drinks containing fructose

Medical causes

  • Hematological malignancies (e.g., leukemia)
    • especially if:
      • weight loss
      • lymphadenopathy
      • recurrent “gout-like” episodes

Renal handling

  • Uric acid removed mainly by:
    • kidneys
    • small part by gut

Drugs

  • Drugs that decrease renal uric acid excretion worsen gout
  • Most famous:

    Thiazides
    • Diuretics are a problem → you may need to change them

7) Acute Attack Treatment Options (Immediate Management)

Acute gout management options

  • NSAIDs
    • “No difference between any type of NSAID”
  • Colchicine
  • Prednisolone
  • Prednisolone injection
  • Local corticosteroid injection
    • Practical option
    • Can be done at the same time as aspiration
    • Example:
      • aspirate joint fluid
      • radiologist “chucks a little bit of steroid inside”
    • Only if you can confidently rule out septic arthritis

8) Long-Term Management (Urate Lowering Therapy)

Do all gout patients need allopurinol?

Yes — all patients

Why (what you are preventing)

  • Tophaceous gout
  • Cardiovascular disease
  • Kidney stones
  • Kidney disease

9) Allopurinol Dosing Approach (Treat-to-Target)

Treat-to-target approach

  • Increase dose until target uric acid range reached

Targets

  • Regular gout: 0.36
  • Tophaceous gout: 0.36 (as stated)

Dose escalation plan

  • Start allopurinol 50 mg daily for 4 weeks
  • Then increase by 50 mg every 4 weeks
  • Continue until target achieved

Clinical comment

  • Many patients are “on allopurinol but not hitting target range”
  • If uric acid not pushed down → “it’s not helping them”

10) Allopurinol and Flare-Ups

Does allopurinol increase flare-up risk when started?

Yes — significantly

Therefore: flare prophylaxis is required

  • When starting allopurinol:

    Give colchicine as flare prophylaxis
  • Duration:
    • “Not clear”
    • Therapeutic guideline says:

      continue for 6 months
  • Reason colchicine is preferred:
    • “less side effects compared to NSAIDs and steroids”

11) Starting Allopurinol During Acute Attack (Old vs New Thinking)

Question

Do you start allopurinol in an acute attack?

  • Old teaching: never
  • New guidelines: not so strict anymore

Answer: Yes

  • Can start with treatment for acute attack
  • “No data on the optimal start”
  • Traditionally delayed until attack resolved
  • But starting it during acute treatment “may still be fine”

OSCE Station

A) Introduction

Start with:

  • Introduce yourself
  • Then:

    “Look, James, I have your history findings. I have your physical examination finding. I just want to discuss the condition with you and how we want to manage it. But is there anything that you’d like to tell me before I start?”

B) State the diagnosis

Tell the patient:

  • “Most likely you have a condition called gout.”
  • Optional wording:
    • “acute gout”
    • “gouty arthritis / gout arthritis”

C) Explanation (Simple)

Explain:

  • “In this condition, you have a high amount of uric acid in your blood.”
  • “The uric acid crystals create inflammation in your joints.”

D) Reasons for diagnosis (Explain your thinking)

  1. This is arthritis / inflammation
  • Painful
  • swollen
  • red
  • warm to touch

    → “This means there is an inflammation happening in your joint.”
  1. No fever
  • “We are very worried about septic arthritis, but you don’t have any fever.”

13) Investigations (What you order)

Key Investigation #1 (before anything else)

I will send u for Joint ultrasound + joint aspiration

Say clearly:

  • “I’ll send you for a joint ultrasound, which is a scan of the joint.”
  • “During that ultrasound we will do a joint aspiration.”
  • “We will check it under a special microscope called polarized microscopy to see the uric acid crystals.”

Key Investigation #2

Serum uric acid

Other blood tests

  • Kidney function
  • Full blood examination
  • ESR
  • CRP
    • “just to make sure I’m not having an infection”

Optional metabolic/cardiovascular association tests

  • blood sugar level
  • lipid profile
  • “and so on”
    • “closely associated with each other”

X-ray

  • “can be appropriate”
  • may show damage / tophaceous gout
  • Put it at the very end of the list

Immediate Management (Acute Attack Management)

Framework

Split into:

Pharmacological + Non-pharmacological

A) Pharmacological management

Tell the patient:

  • “We have a few options.”

Options:

  1. NSAIDs
    • indometacin / ibuprofen / diclofenac
  2. Steroids course
  3. Colchicine
  4. Steroid injection during aspiration

B) Non-pharmacological management

  • rest
  • ice

Key non-pharma focus: avoid triggers

  • avoid red meat
  • avoid alcohol
  • avoid sugary drinks
  • avoid seafood

Other advice:

  • good hydration
    • “drink plenty of fluids”
    • “avoid dehydration”
  • wear comfortable footwear
  • avoid trauma and injuries to the foot

Long-Term Management (After Confirmation)

Once gout is confirmed

Start allopurinol

  • “decreases uric acid”

Add flare prophylaxis

Colchicine for 6 months

  • because allopurinol increases flare-up risk initially

Address Hydrochlorothiazide (Important Management Point)

Patient is on hydrochlorothiazide

  • “I would change your medication.”

Change to:

  • ACE inhibitor
  • calcium channel blocker

Key phrase:

  • “No reason to be on thiazide when you have gout.”

Practical OSCE reassurance

  • GP can do this
  • No need cardiologist input (simple HTN)
  • Example line:
    • “I’m changing it to perindopril.”

NSAID Precautions (OSCE Trap Warning)

Be careful:

  • NSAIDs have side effects / precautions
  • Avoid / be cautious in:
    • hypertension patients
    • chronic kidney disease
    • people using ACE inhibitors + diuretics

Key exam warning:

If patient is on ACE inhibitor + diuretic

  • NSAIDs may be incorrect because of:
    • “triple whammy”

Also:

  • in hypertensive patients, be careful with kidney function

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: surgical-skills-expert (Australian surgical educator, FRACS) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The case rules out septic arthritis mainly by 'checking temperature / no fever'. This is unsafe.
Absence of fever does NOT exclude septic arthritis - a substantial proportion of septic joints are afebrile, and the patient's normal temperature (36.5) is falsely reassuring. Any acutely hot, swollen, red joint must be aspirated and the fluid sent urgently for Gram stain, culture and cell count (as well as polarised microscopy for crystals) before it is called gout. Septic arthritis is excluded on synovial fluid analysis, not on temperature.
✏️ Serum urate targets are stated as 'Regular gout: 0.36, Tophaceous gout: 0.36'.
The Australian target is serum urate <0.36 mmol/L for uncomplicated gout but <0.30 mmol/L for tophaceous or severe/erosive gout (to promote tophus dissolution). The case incorrectly gives the same 0.36 target for both; tophaceous gout requires the lower <0.30 mmol/L target. Express targets in mmol/L (Australian units).
✏️ The case states 'Do all gout patients need allopurinol? Yes - all patients'.
Urate-lowering therapy is not mandatory after a single first attack. It is indicated for recurrent attacks (>=2 per year), tophi, radiographic joint damage/erosions, urate nephrolithiasis, or gout with CKD/diuretic use. This patient (recurrent attacks, on a thiazide) does qualify, but the blanket 'all patients' teaching is an over-statement.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Specify the current low-dose colchicine regimen per Therapeutic Guidelines: colchicine 1 mg orally initially then 0.5 mg one hour later (maximum 1.5 mg over the first day), NOT the outdated high-dose hourly regimen - and reduce dose in renal impairment and with interacting drugs (statins, clarithromycin). Also name prednis(ol)one 15-30 mg daily short course as an option, especially where NSAIDs are contraindicated.
⭐ Add allopurinol-safety detail: start low (50 mg/day) and go slow with dose reason being that it lowers this patient's risk of the 'triple whammy'; consider HLA-B*5801 testing in higher-risk ancestries (e.g. Han Chinese, Thai, Korean) given the risk of allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome, and co-prescribe flare prophylaxis (low-dose colchicine) for up to 6 months when initiating urate-lowering therapy. Febuxostat is the PBS alternative if allopurinol is not tolerated.
⭐ Sharpen the antihypertensive switch: the case suggests changing hydrochlorothiazide to an ACE inhibitor or CCB - add losartan as a particularly good choice because it has a mild uricosuric effect, and reiterate the 'triple whammy' (ACEi/ARB + diuretic + NSAID) acute-kidney-injury warning the case already flags.
