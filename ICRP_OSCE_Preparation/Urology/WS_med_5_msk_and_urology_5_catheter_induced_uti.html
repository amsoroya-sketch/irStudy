Catheter-Associated UTI with Urosepsis / Septic Shock

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med 5 MSK and urology / 5. urology (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: urology

Catheter-Induced Urinary Tract Infection


Your next patient is a 60 year old man who is a known case of BPH and presented to the Emergency Department with urinary retention and as part of his treatment a urinary catheter was placed for him.

The catheter was removed yesterday. He has presented to the emergency department today complaining of fatigue and feeling feverish.

On your examination he has a temp of 39.5 BP 80/50 PR 120 Sat 90%

The nurse has called you to discuss about this patient.

Task:

  • Explain the condition to the nurse
  • Discuss the required investigation and immediate management
  • Discuss your long-term management?

Diagnosis

Primary Diagnosis

  • Catheter-induced urinary tract infection

Associated / Severity Diagnosis

  • Urosepsis
  • Possible sepsis or septic shock

The case deliberately highlights sepsis features:

  • Drowsiness
  • Hypotension
  • Tachycardia
  • Fever

Background Explanation (Why This Happens)

  • Urinary catheter is a foreign body
  • Increases risk of UTI, especially in:
    • Patients with prostate problems
    • Nursing home patients
  • Severe UTIs can lead to:
    • Acute mental state changes

Symptoms of Catheter-Induced UTI (As Mentioned)

  • Fever
  • Pelvic discomfort
  • Hematuria
  • Flank pain
  • Acute mental state change

Key Principles Before Antibiotics

  • Always obtain samples for:
    • Urine culture and sensitivity (MCS)
    • Blood culture
  • Samples should be taken before antibiotics

Catheter Management

  • Preferred approach:
    • Remove the catheter
  • Options:
    • Remove catheter → take midstream urine
    • If catheter must remain → replace and take sample from catheter port
  • In this case:
    • Catheter already removed → proceed with urine MCS

How to Explain Diagnosis to the Nurse (Example Dialogue)

“I think this patient has a catheter-induced urinary tract infection, and given his low blood pressure, high pulse rate, fever, and drowsiness, I am also concerned about possible sepsis or even septic shock.”

REASONS: Triggering event> patient recently catherized, patient has BPH that itself icreases risk of UTI, pt has septic shock features as he is drowsy, his BP is low, PR is high, high temperature.

Immediate Management (Sepsis-Focused)

Initial Stabilisation

  • I want to Insert two large-bore IV cannulas
  • Start my pt normal saline(500ml- 1 liter)
  • Oxygen:
    • If saturation <94% → give oxygen
    • If normal → monitor closely

Immediate Investigations (Before Antibiotics)

  1. Urine MCS
  2. Blood culture

These two are prioritised before antibiotics

Other blood tests are not affected by antibiotics

Antibiotic Management

  • I want u to quickly start antibiotics to patient
  • Start empirical IV antibiotics
  • Given within one hour after samples taken
  • Medications used:
    • Gentamicin IV
    • Amoxicillin IV
  • Doses:
    • Let me checkthe dosages in therapeutic guidelines

Example Dialogue

“I will start the patient on IV gentamicin and IV amoxicillin. I’ll check the exact doses in the therapeutic guidelines and confirm them with you shortly.”

Monitoring

  • I want u to Closely monitor :
    • Vital signs
  • Continue IV fluids as needed

Additional Investigations

  • Urea, electrolytes, creatinine
  • Full blood examination
  • ESR, CRP

Intermediate Management

  • We will follow up urine and blood culture results
  • Adjust antibiotics based on:
    • Organism
    • Sensitivity
  • Duration of antibiotics:
    • 7 days if good response
    • 10–14 days if inadequate response
  • Switch from IV to oral antibiotics:
    • Once patient is stable
    • Once oral intake is tolerated

Long-Term Management (New Task)

Aim

  • Best Prevention of catheter-induced UTI is to:
    • Treat BPH
    • Limit catheter use

Pharmacological Management of BPH

  1. I want to start Alpha-receptor antagonists
    • Example: Tamsulosin
    • Side effect to warn about:
      • Postural hypotension
  2. 5-alpha reductase inhibitors
    • Example: Finasteride
  3. Combination therapy
    • Tamsulosin + Dutasteride
    • Single combination tablet preferred

If Medical Treatment Fails

  • Recurrent urinary retention
  • Poor symptom control

→ Refer to urologist

Surgical Management

  • Gold standard:
    • Transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP)
  • Explanation:
    • Endoscopic procedure
    • Instrument passed through urethra
    • Part of prostate shaved to relieve obstruction

Important Exam Clarification

  • PSA testing not included coz BPH already confirmed in the stem
  • Focus is on management, not diagnosis of BPH

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: surgical-skills-expert (Australian surgical educator, FRACS) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ Fluid resuscitation is stated as 'normal saline 500 mL - 1 litre'. For a patient in septic shock (BP 80/50, HR 120, SpO2 90%) this is inadequate.
Give a balanced crystalloid bolus targeting 30 mL/kg (approximately 2 L for a 70 kg adult) within the first 3 hours per Surviving Sepsis / Australian sepsis pathways, delivered as repeated 250-500 mL boluses with reassessment after each. Escalate to noradrenaline (vasopressor) if the patient remains hypotensive (MAP <65) after adequate fluid - i.e. this is septic shock, not just sepsis.
✏️ The sepsis work-up omits serum lactate and structured perfusion monitoring.
Measure serum lactate immediately (a lactate >2 mmol/L, and especially >4 mmol/L, defines high-risk sepsis and guides resuscitation) and re-measure to assess response. Insert a urinary catheter and monitor hourly urine output as a marker of end-organ perfusion. These are core components of the Sepsis Six / Australian sepsis bundle that the case currently misses.
✏️ IV amoxicillin is started without documenting a check for penicillin allergy.
Confirm allergy status before giving amoxicillin/ampicillin. The Australian empirical regimen for urosepsis (amoxicillin or ampicillin 2 g IV 6-hourly PLUS gentamicin IV, dosed to actual body weight and renal function) is appropriate, but penicillin allergy must be excluded first and gentamicin dosing checked against renal function.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ State the timing bundle explicitly: take blood cultures and urine culture (from a freshly replaced catheter port, or MSU as here) BEFORE antibiotics, then give empirical IV antibiotics within 1 hour of recognising sepsis - the case has the sequence right but should name the '1-hour' and 'Sepsis Six' standard and add early senior/ICU escalation and source control (remove/replace the offending catheter).
⭐ Add long-term prevention detail: the case correctly targets BPH treatment (tamsulosin, finasteride/dutasteride, TURP) but should add catheter-stewardship measures - avoid unnecessary catheterisation, use the smallest appropriate catheter, aseptic insertion and prompt removal, and consider trial-of-void or intermittent self-catheterisation rather than a long-term indwelling catheter, per the ACSQHC CAUTI-prevention program.
⭐ Metadata to add: flag this as an emergency communication/escalation station (ISBAR handover to the nurse) and cross-link to the catheterisation procedure (case 1) and BPH/retention (case 7).
