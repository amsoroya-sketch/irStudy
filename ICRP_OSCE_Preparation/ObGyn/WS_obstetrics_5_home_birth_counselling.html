Home Birth Request Counselling (High-Risk, Rural)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — obstetrics / class 8 (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: obstetrics_gynaecology

HOME BIRTH COUNSELLING

1. CASE STEM & CONTEXT

Your next patient is a 25 year old lady who is 20 weeks pregnant and has come for her antenatal check. She wants to discuss her birth plans with you and wants everything to be natural.

You are a GP in a rural hospital with level 2 services and 400km away from the tertiary centre.

Task:

• Take history for 4 minutes

• Discuss the birth plan with her

  • Previous lecture sequence:
    • Case 1: wanted cesarean because of pain
    • Case 2: wanted VBAC
    • Now: wants natural vaginal delivery, but not in hospital – wants to deliver at home

THEORY

2. KEY DESIGN POINTS OF THE CASE

Two important things before getting lost in candles and music:

  1. Find the agenda
    • “What is the reason that you’ve decided to do it at home?”
    • Any previous bad experiences or other issues with doctors / hospital / placenta etc.?
  2. Check for indication / contraindication for home birth
    • Main specific key point of history
    • Ask: “Do we have any indication or contraindication for home birth?”
    • This is what most people forget to ask → main reason they fail

Key point of history in this counseling case:

    1. Find the agenda
    2. Find reasons that may make you concerned about home birth (indications/contraindications)

3. FINDINGS

  • Wants the whole family to be there for delivery
  • Wants music and candles
  • Doesn't want any Drs or midwives
  • Wants placenta to be delivered by itself
  • Doesn't want to be handled by medical staff
  • Old→ Complicated delivery, One C/S, one miscarriage
  • New → PPH, difficult placenta delivery twice

This should alert you to agenda:

    • “What is the reason of this request?”
    • “Any problem, previous experience, or issues with this case?”
  • Question: Is she safe for home birth?
    • You want to tell her it is not safe
    • But remember: it is a patient-centered approach
      • You do not say “No, you cannot deliver at home”
      • You give good things about home birth
      • You give cons/concerns
      • Educate your patient, give her time to think
      • Still mid-pregnancy, a bit too early to finalize delivery plans

4. COMMUNICATION STYLE & OPENING

  • Expect an annoying patient who:
    • Refuses whatever you suggest
    • Says “No doctor, I don’t want that; not in hospital”
  • Your approach:
    • Good communication skills “a little bit more than usual”
    • Put your big smile on and deal with her calmly

HISTORY TAKING

Opening steps:

  • Introduce yourself
  • Good open-ended question:
    • “Okay Karen, tell me, how can I help you today?”
  • She answers:
    • Here for regular pregnancy visit
    • Wants to start planning for delivery
    • Wants home birth, candles, family, etc.

Response to her opening statement:

  • Keep environment happy
  • Example response:
    • “Karen, that’s a very exciting decision that you’ve made.”
    • “I’m here to support you to make the best decision about delivery.”

5. BREAKING DOWN THE AGENDA

Historically, hard to break this lady’s agenda. So go step by step:

  1. First agenda question
    • “Can I ask why you have decided to deliver at home?”
    • Patient may just say: “I just want to, doctor.”
  2. Second agenda question (go deeper):
    • “Do you have any concern or issue about delivery?”
    • (Recall there is a version where she worries about pain)
  3. Third agenda question (even deeper):
    • “Any previous bad experiences you have had with your deliveries, with the hospital, with medical staff?”
  • Here is where the story starts:
    • “Yes, I’ve had problems in my previous pregnancies... That’s why I don’t want any doctors or midwives.”

Acknowledge and empathize:

  • “I’m so sorry to hear about your experience.”
  • “I can imagine how difficult that experience could have been.”

Then:

  • “Is it okay if I ask you a few questions now?”

6. HISTORY TAKING STRUCTURE (2 MIN CHAT + 2 MIN HISTORY)

Divide history into two parts:

  • 2 minutes chit-chat / agenda / acknowledgement
  • 2 minutes proper history

A. Current Pregnancy

  • “How is this current pregnancy going?”
  • Have you done:
    • Regular bloods at the beginning of pregnancy?
    • Dating scan?
    • Down syndrome screening?
    • 18–20 week ultrasound?
  • Any problems or concerns in the results?
    • low-lying placenta / placenta previa in these cases
  • Maybe: “Have you started feeling the baby kicking?”

B. Previous Pregnancies (very important)

  • “Tell me about your previous pregnancies.”
    • How many pregnancies?
    • Method of delivery: vaginal or cesarean?
  • Any complications during pregnancy:
    • High blood pressure
    • High blood sugar
    • If present, you’d be concerned for current pregnancy too
  • Any complications of delivery:
    • Long, prolonged labor
    • Incontinence
    • Instrumental delivery
    • Bleeding after delivery
  • Findings in this case:
    • Difficulty delivering the placenta (both times)
    • Massive bleed after last delivery → postpartum hemorrhage
    • This is one of the reasons she now wants home birth

C. Past Medical History / Medications (if time)

  • Ask briefly if she has any medical conditions
  • If she is on any medications

D. Home Situation / Psychosocial (imp)

  • Last important thing to assess: home situation
  • Open-ended:
    • “Can you describe your home situation for me?”
  • Then:
    • “Who do you live with?”
    • “Are they supportive?”
    • Any stresses or possible problems at home?
  • Purpose:
    • When planning home birth, ensure home environment is a good environment for delivery
  • You can include psychosocial as part of home assessment:
    • “How are you coping with your pregnancy?”

End of history:

  • Thank her:
    • “Thank you for answering my questions and being cooperative.”
  • Move into counseling

THEORY

7. PURPOSE & BOXES OF COUNSELING

Expect:

  • She will refuse many things
  • Wants to stick to her own plan

Purpose of counseling – boxes to tick off:

  1. Acknowledgement (strong)
    • You see she has had a bad experience
    • Acknowledge that clearly
  2. Risks specific to this lady
    • “In your specific case, I’m a little bit concerned.”
  3. Pros and cons of home birth
    • Pros – evidence-based good points
    • Cons – especially considering her risks and location
  4. Patient-centered approach
    • Provide information and options
    • Give reading material
    • Offer follow-up session
    • She makes the final decision, not you

8. EVIDENCE SUMMARY (PROS OF HOME BIRTH FOR SUITABLE WOMEN)

For women identified as suitable for home birth, evidence shows:

  • Higher rate of unassisted vaginal birth at home
  • Lower rate of:
    • Intrapartum cesarean section
    • Instrumental delivery
  • Lower rate of postpartum hemorrhage and tears
  • Nulliparous women (first baby):
    • Do not experience higher rates of adverse perinatal and maternal outcomes
  • Rates of stillbirth and neonatal death are similar
    • We are not too concerned about higher adverse outcomes for the baby when suitable

9. WHO CAN DELIVER AT HOME? (LOW-RISK PREGNANCY)

Only one category: low-risk pregnancy

Low-risk pregnancy =

  • Low-risk current pregnancy
  • Low-risk previous pregnancies

So, for home birth:

  • No pre-existing medical conditions
  • No medical conditions
  • Single pregnancy (no twins)
  • Baby is cephalic (head down)
  • At term
  • No previous cesarean sections
  • No previous uterine surgeries
  • No previous complications (e.g. long complicated labors, instrumental, etc.)

Example:

  • If both previous pregnancies had very long labors, and they had to use instruments to pull the baby out → not suitable for home birth

10. INDICATIONS TO TRANSFER TO HOSPITAL & HOME SAFETY

A. Indications to Transfer from Home Birth to Hospital

  • Need for further pain relief
  • Progress not as expected (slow or abnormal labor)
  • Any concern about maternal or fetal well-being
    • Abnormal monitoring → “Let’s stop home birth and go to hospital.”

B. Ensuring Safety of Mother at Home

  • Ask about and consider:
    • Any dangerous animals
    • Illicit drug use
    • Domestic violence at home
    • Vehicle access
    • Reliable telecommunication
    • Suitable heating and lighting
  • These are part of your history and planning.

C. Transfer Possibility & Rural Context

  • Always consider the possibility of transfer to hospital:
    • During pregnancy
    • During labor
    • After delivery
  • Home birth provider should have a management plan for transfer:
    • How far is the nearest hospital?
    • How long to get there?
    • Peak hour travel time
    • Private vehicle vs emergency services
      • In a very rural area, ambulances may take 2 hours to arrive → not acceptable in urgent situations

11. PLANNING & TIMING

  • Offer:
    • Make her familiar with the Hospital visit in the third trimester
    • We will do Home visit at 36 weeks to finalize plans
  • General rule across delivery discussions:
    • “We like to start planning delivery at same time as GBS swab (around 36 weeks)”
  • Plan should be supported by:
    • Midwife, specialist, or GP obstetrician (in rural area)
  • For home birth:
    • Usually 2 APRA-registered health professionals (most often two midwives)
    • Not usually obstetricians or doctors attending home births
  • Pain relief at home:
    • Some options not available
    • You can get morphine and pethidine at home

12. WHY WOMEN MAY CHOOSE HOME BIRTH

Common reasons (for your counseling discussion):

  • Feel comfortable at home
  • Previous traumatic experiences (our exam case)
  • No desire for unnecessary interventions or drugs
  • Wanting to do cultural or spiritual practices around delivery

Big theme across references:

  • Transfer plan, transfer plan, transfer plan
  • Even if she chooses home birth, must have a clear plan to get to hospital if:
    • Labor not progressing
    • Pain worse than expected
    • She changes her mind

13. SUMMARY OF KEY RISKS IN THIS CASE

When the lecturer “summarizes boxes”:

  • Risk 1 – Previous pregnancies
    • Recurrent difficulty delivering placenta
    • Massive postpartum hemorrhage
  • Risk 2 – Location
    • Rural area
    • 400 km from tertiary centre
    • Local services may be insufficient to manage severe complications

COUNSELLING

14. COUNSELING BOX 1 – ACKNOWLEDGEMENT

Script-style structure:

  • “First of all, I want to tell you, Karen, that I fully support your decision.”
  • “I understand how your previous experiences have been difficult.”
  • Acknowledge:
    • Her excitement about delivering at home
    • With family, candles, music
  • Also acknowledge:
    • Her bad experiences in previous deliveries
    • How they have given her a bad association with hospital

Introduce purpose:

  • “I would like to discuss further questions on home birth so you can make an informed decision based on the pros and cons of delivering at home.”
  • Emphasis:
    • You support her
    • Your job is to give information

15. COUNSELING BOX 2 – SPECIFIC CONCERNS / RISKS

Do this early – this is the most important box.

Tell her:

  • “Overall, I have two main concerns in your case.”

Concern 1 – Difficulty Delivering Placenta (Twice)

  • “As you have had difficulty delivering the placenta twice in your previous deliveries, there is a higher chance we will face difficulty delivering the placenta this time.”
  • Build understanding about her “placenta to deliver by itself” wish:
    • “We usually expect the placenta to deliver by itself.”
    • “If it doesn’t, we may need to gently pull the cord to remove it.”
    • “Sometimes this can cause bleeding and complications.”
    • “We try to keep it gentle and careful to avoid complications, but occasionally the cord may snap and cause severe bleeding or other complications.”
    • “This is likely what happened in your previous pregnancies.”

Concern 2 – Rural Location & Limited Services

  • “You live in a rural area, and the services in the hospital might be insufficient to deal with complications such as severe bleeding, difficulty delivering the placenta, or needing urgent surgery (e.g. emergency cesarean).”
  • Because you are 400 km from a tertiary centre, performing urgent cesarean sections and urgent surgeries may be problematic.

16. COUNSELING BOX 3 – HOME BIRTH ITSELF (PROS & CONS)

Introduce:

  • “Regarding home birth, we’ll have to do a few things. Let me tell you a few pros first.”

Pros (home birth)

  • Evidence shows:
    • Lower risk of tears and traumas
    • Lower risk of instrument use
    • Lower risk of needing urgent cesarean section
  • For the baby:
    • Risk to the baby is the same as in hospital (stillbirth/neonatal death rates are similar)

Cons (home birth)

  • In the event of complications, transferring to hospital may be challenging:
    • Need a vehicle
    • Someone must drive you
    • Driving while in pain/bleeding is difficult
    • During peak hours you may be stuck in traffic
  • Limited pain relief options:
    • Cannot do epidural at home
    • Cannot use some gas (nitrous) or other hospital-based options
    • Only some options like morphine/pethidine are available

17. COUNSELING BOX 4 – TIMING & FLEXIBILITY

  • “You are at 20 weeks of pregnancy. It is a little early to finalize delivery plans.”
  • “We usually make our delivery plan around 36 weeks of pregnancy.”

Build expectation that plans may change:

  • “If any complications arise, we may need to do a cesarean section or plan a hospital delivery.”
    • Example:
      • If later scans show placenta previa (placenta on the outlet) → no home birth
      • If decreased fetal activity or abnormal fetal heart rate on tests → we may need urgent surgery
  • Message:
    • “I support your idea for home birth, but there are occasions where there is no option other than hospital delivery to provide urgent treatment.”

18. COUNSELING BOX 5 – PATIENT-CENTERED WRAP-UP

End with strong patient-centered approach:

  • “I want you to think about it.”
  • “I will give you some reading material.”
  • “We can have another meeting with your family and partner to discuss home birth.”
  • “If you’re happy, I can involve an obstetrician or a midwife so you can discuss your concerns with them.”

If she still refuses midwife/doctors:

  • “Thank you for coming to me.”
  • “I respect your decision.”
  • “It might be a little safer if we involve them and have a chat, just to put a plan in place.”

19. IF SHE DECIDES TO PROCEED WITH HOME BIRTH – FINAL CONDITIONS

If her final decision is: “I want to do home birth”, emphasize:

  1. Eligibility criteria (repeated)
    • Single pregnancy
    • No previous cesarean or uterine surgery
    • Head down (cephalic) position close to delivery
      • If baby is breech near delivery, no home birth
    • Needs to be low-risk, as per earlier list
  2. Transfer Plan (main plan)
    • “If you decide to deliver at home, we need a transfer plan.”
    • You recommend planning delivery closer to a tertiary hospital:
      • Birth suite / birth centre option:
        • Very similar to home
        • Family can be there
        • Music, candles etc.
        • Feels like a five-star hotel
    • Need details:
      • How she will be transferred if needed
      • Who will drive
      • How long it will take
  3. Presence of Midwife(s)
  • “I would still recommend that it is a safer option to have a midwife with you during delivery.”
  • Usual practice:
    • Two midwives for home or birth-centre deliveries
  • Offer:
    • “If you like, I can introduce you to a midwife, you can discuss your concerns with them, and if you’re happy, having someone professional there is much safer in case we need extra help.”

20. EXAM EXPECTATIONS / PASS-FAIL KEYS (AS LECTURER STATES)

To pass this OSCE case, you must complete all key boxes:

  • History:
    • Agenda explored properly
    • Indications/contraindications for home birth (especially placenta history)
    • Current pregnancy, previous pregnancies, home/psychosocial
  • Counseling:
    • Acknowledgement of her previous bad experience and her wishes
    • Clear explanation of two main concerns:
      • Previous placenta difficulties & PPH
      • Rural location and limited services
    • Balanced pros and cons of home birth
    • Explain timing (20 weeks vs 36 weeks) and possible future complications that would mandate hospital delivery/CS
    • Patient-centered approach with:
      • Shared decision-making
      • Reading material
      • Follow-up session
      • Option to involve midwife/obstetrician
      • Respecting her decision while still recommending safer options
  • If you:
    • Do not do the risks → “I wouldn’t pass you.”
    • Do not acknowledge and do not show patient-centered approach (e.g. you simply say “Yes go do home birth” or “No don’t do home birth”) → “I wouldn’t pass you.”

You must do all components together – all the boxes.

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: clinical-documentation-expert (Australian clinical supervisor, O&G documentation standards) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The notes state nitrous oxide (gas) cannot be used at home and only morphine/pethidine are available.
Portable nitrous oxide (Entonox) is in fact commonly available and used at Australian publicly-funded home births / community-midwifery programs; the true limitation is that epidural and continuous electronic monitoring are not available at home. Correct the pain-relief comparison accordingly.
✏️ The document refers to '2 APRA-registered health professionals'.
This is a typo/terminology error - practitioners are registered with AHPRA (Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency), not APRA (the prudential/superannuation regulator). Home births are typically attended by two AHPRA-registered midwives.
✏️ The eligibility discussion does not state plainly that THIS woman is ineligible for a planned publicly-funded home birth.
Her history of a previous caesarean, previous PPH and twice-retained placenta are explicit exclusion criteria for publicly-funded home birth under Australian state home-birth program guidelines. Document clearly that she does not meet low-risk eligibility, while still using a patient-centred, non-coercive approach.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Given recurrent retained placenta and PPH, add a specific management recommendation: active management of the third stage (IM oxytocin, controlled cord traction) and planned birth in a facility with immediate access to blood products and theatre - directly addresses her highest-risk feature.
⭐ Add anti-D consideration if the woman is Rh D negative, and iron studies/optimisation of haemoglobin antenatally given prior PPH.
⭐ Reference the RANZCOG statement on home births and the relevant state publicly-funded home-birth program eligibility criteria in the teaching notes to anchor Australian practice.
⭐ Document a concrete transfer plan (distance, driver, travel time in peak hour, 000/ambulance availability which may exceed 2 hours rurally) and offer the safer 'birth centre / family birthing suite near a tertiary hospital' alternative as a recorded option.
⭐ Record involvement of a midwife/obstetrician and provision of written information with a follow-up appointment - satisfies shared decision-making and medicolegal documentation expectations.
