Antepartum Haemorrhage (with Domestic Violence Screening)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — obstetrics / class 6 (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: obstetrics_gynaecology

ANTEPARTUM HEMORRHAGE

Your next patient is a 29 year old lady who is 31 weeks pregnant. She has presented to your General Practice complaining of vaginal bleeding. On her 20 week USD, the placenta was seen 5cm from the OS.

On examination: Vitals are stable

Bruise seen on abdomen

FH 29 cm

Task:

• Take history from the patient

• Explain diagnosis with reasons ( Differentials )

• Patient is admitted in the ward - Do not explain management

Placenta Distance Discussion

  • Placenta 5 cm from the os
  • Not placenta previa
  • Not a low-lying placenta
  • Low-lying placenta = 2 cm from the os
  • No concerns and nothing to do with placenta previa

Definition

  • Vaginal bleeding beyond 20 weeks = antepartum hemorrhage
  • Early pregnancy bleeding had differentials like ectopic pregnancy, molar pregnancy, miscarriages
  • After the halfway point of pregnancy, it’s no longer miscarriage, most likely antepartum hemorrhage

Key Differentials for Antepartum Hemorrhage

  • Uterine rupture
  • Placental abruption
  • Preterm labor
  • Placenta previa
  • Trauma (accidental or non-accidental)
  • Can still be vaginal causes or cervix causes

Trauma

  • Accidental: e.g., motor vehicle accident
  • Non-accidental: domestic violence

Domestic and Family Violence – Medical Legal Point

  • Child abuse is mandatory notification
  • Unborn child: not mandatory to report, even at 36–38 weeks
  • Look for other children who may be subjected to physical abuse
  • Manage domestic violence by referring to support services

Priority in This Case

  • Before domestic violence, sort out the bleeding problem

Differential 1: Uterine Rupture

  • Severe pain
  • Contents go into abdominal cavity → severe peritonitis
  • Uterine tenderness, guarding, rigidity
  • Vaginal bleeding
  • Fetal parts felt on abdomen, no longer in uterus

Differential 2: Placental Abruption

  • Vaginal bleeding in 60–80% of cases
  • Tender uterus late in pregnancy
  • Tense, woody feeling consistency
  • Severe abruption → think about delivery fast via cesarean section
  • Worry about isoimmunization → give anti-D
  • 24–34 weeks → give corticosteroids
  • Look for DIC and clotting problems

Differential 3: Preterm Labor

  • Can still be labor starting
  • Before 37 weeks = preterm labor
  • Beyond 37 weeks: normal labor
  • Key point: contractions
  • Contractions, pelvic pressure, bleeding, later discharge and breaking of water
  • Management depends on gestational age and other factors

Domestic Violence Support Note

  • Specific support groups, financial support, helplines, websites
  • Websites have quick exit button
  • Communication skills: reassurance and empathic statements like.
  • “Everybody deserves to feel safe. No one deserves to be hit.”
  • I am concerned about your safety and wellbeing. Let's work togethar on this
  • Abuse is common and happens in all kinds of relationships. It tends to continue. You are not to blame Abuse can affect your health and that of your children

On examination: Vitals are stable

Bruise seen on abdomen

FH 29 cm

Task:

• Take history from the patient

• Explain diagnosis with reasons ( Differentials )

• Patient is admitted in the ward - Do not explain management

History taking

Approach & hemodynamic stability

  • For antepartum hemorrhage, hemodynamic stability is always relevant
  • Enter the station:
    • Examiner does ID check, you use the sanitizer.
    • Examiner takes you to the patient: “This is your patient Mary, copy of your case.”
  • Preferred sequence:
    • Before starting full intro, briefly turn to examiner:
      • “Examiner, tell me blood pressure, pulse rate.”
      • Clarify if the patient is stable or not.
    • “My name is Amir. Karen, how can I help you?”

Presenting complaint – Vaginal bleeding in late pregnancy

  • Patient’s complaint (given in stem):
    • “Yeah doctor, I’m pregnant, 31 weeks pregnant, I’m having vaginal bleeding. I’m really worried about it.”
  • Response style:
    • Acknowledge and reassure:
      • “Yep, I’m here to address your concern.”
      • “I know how stressful it is, but I’m here to help you.”
      • “Let’s just go through a few questions, we’ll figure out this together, I’ll make the best management plan for you.”

Exploration of the complaint – Bleeding (similar to other bleeding cases)

  1. Timing
    • “Tell me, when did it start?”
    • “When did you first notice it?”
    • “Since when have you had the bleeding?”
  2. On–off / ongoing question
    • Prefer wording:
      • “Is the bleeding ongoing? Are you still bleeding or has it stopped?”
    • This replaces the usual “on and off or continuous” phrasing.
  3. Progression
    • “Is it getting worse?”
  4. CCVO (pushing it down like early pregnancy bleeding)
    • Colour
      • “Describe the color to me.”
      • “Is it bright blood or is it dark blood?”

    • Content (most important)
      • “Are you passing any clots?”
      • “Have you passed any tissue? Anything?”
      • Optional acceptable:
        • “Have you passed any discharge or any fluid?”
    • Volume
      • “Tell me, how many pads have you used?”
        • Example: “I’ve used two, doctor, since I started two hours ago.”
      • “Were they fully soaked?” (Yes / No)
    • Odour
      • “Is there any bad smell, odour to the bleeding?”
    • Order is not critically important, but you still make a structure.

Next step after bleeding – Thinking about differentials

  • List of main differentials in this setting:
    • Rupture
    • Placental abruption
    • Placenta previa
    • Preterm labor
  • Problem:
    • When you ask: “What questions do I want to ask for each?” – they don’t give you good separate questions.
  • Therefore, Amir uses a “red flag symptoms” cluster:
    • “What are the red flags in a pregnant lady who is bleeding?”
    • “What is the symptom that all of these guys share together?”

Red flag symptoms (Group 1)

Abdominal pain (key, often forgotten)

    • Comment: Some candidates forget to ask abdominal pain, which is shocking in a bleeding pregnant lady.
    • Shared feature:
      • Bleeding + abdominal pain present in all except placenta previa.
    • Question:
      • “Do you have abdominal pain?”
    • If yes, explore in detail:
      • Site – “Where do you feel the pain?”
      • Intensity – “Tell me the intensity of the pain, scale of 1 to 10.”
      • Quality – “Is it a dull ache, stabbing pain?”
      • Onset – “When did the pain start?”
        • “Did it start with the bleeding?”
      • Radiation – “Is there any radiation?”
      • Relieving/aggravating factors – “Is there anything that makes it better or worse?”
    • Key question in pain history (must not forget):
      • “Tell me, is your pain on and off, or is it constant?”
    • Importance:
      • On-and-off pain → contractions.
      • If contractions:
        • “Is the duration increasing, intervals decreasing?”
        • This defines contractions and points towards preterm labor.
    • Main message:
      • On history, the only thing that helps differentiate rupture vs abruption vs preterm labor is:
        • “Do you have contractions?”
        • And the quality/characteristics of the pain: on–off, duration ↑, intervals ↓.
        • Severe + constant + tender, tense uterus→ Think Abruption
        • Sudden tearing pain → then pain may reduce + collapse signs → Think Rupture
        • Intermittent, crampy, rhythmic contractions→ Think Preterm Labour
  1. fluid leakage
    • “Did you notice any fluid leakage?”
    • Aim: figure out if there is rupture of membranes/fluid loss component.
  2. dizziness
    • “Any dizziness?”

  3. fetal movement
    • “Is the baby kicking as usual?”

Traumatic and local causes of bleeding (Group 2 – “traumatic bleeding”)

  1. Trauma to the abdomen
    • “Any traumas or injuries to your stomach?”
    • If yes:
      • “Can you tell me, how did you hit your stomach?”
    • This opens the storyline for domestic violence.
    • Note:
      • Old AMC case: MVA (motor vehicle accident).
      • New case: domestic violence.
      • Structure must work for both, not just DV.
  2. Trauma related to sex / intercourse
    • “Was it related to sex or intercourse?”
    • Thinking about:
      • Vaginal causes
      • Cervical causes
  3. Cervical screening and postcoital bleeding
    • “Were you up to date with your cervical screening before pregnancy?”
    • “Have you ever had bleeding after sex?”
    • Purpose:
      • Looking for cervix, vaginal, and other causes.
      • Trauma down below (not only to abdomen) can cause bleeding.
  • Note:
    • Most AMC cases fall into this group (trauma / domestic violence), but you still ask all of the above.

Home history & domestic violence screening (Group 3 – assessing home)

  • Principle:
    • Even if the current recall is DV or MVA, take it as a structure:
      • You always explore home and DV, regardless of the specific trauma type.
  1. Mini open-ended home history
    • “So tell me, how are things at home?”
    • “Are you in a stable relationship?”
    • “Is your partner supportive?”
    • Answers may reveal:
      • “Yes, I am.”
      • Or: “No, I just live with my boyfriend and he’s not happy with the pregnancy.”
  2. If domestic violence is disclosed
    • Example: “Yes, there is domestic violence.”
    • Then you open a new group of questioning specifically about DV.

Domestic violence – detailed assessment (new group)

  1. Other injuries
    • “Any other injuries? Any head injury?
  2. Children at home
    • “Do you have any other children at home?”
    • If yes:
      • “Any violence towards the children?”

  3. Alcohol and drugs
    • “Do you or your partner drink alcohol or use drugs?”

  4. Financial independence
    • “Are you able to financially support yourself?”
    • Reason:
      • To assess if she can leave home if needed.
  5. Psychological assessment
    • “Tell me, how’s your mood been?”
    • “How are you coping with everything?”
    • “How’s your sleep?”

Obstetric – routine obstetric background

  1. Blood tests (“Bloods”)
    • Ask about routine antenatal blood tests.
  2. Dating scan & Down syndrome screening
    • “Dating scan?”
    • “Down syndrome?”
  3. Regular ultrasound
    • “Have you done your regular ultrasound? Was it normal?”
    • Example finding they may give:
      • “They told me the placenta is 5 cm away – that’s totally fine, we’re not going to worry about that.”
  4. Screening for gestational diabetes
    • “Have you done your sweet ring test?” (if at ~26 weeks).
  5. Fetal movements (again)
    • “Is the baby kicking?”
    • Note:
      • If you forgot earlier in red flags, this is where you remember to add it.
  6. Preeclampsia symptoms / complications list
    • Linked to complications like preeclampsia, preterm labor, placental abruption, etc.
    • Ask:
      • “Do you have any headache with this bleeding?”
      • “Any blurring of vision?”
      • “Any swelling in your legs?”
      • “Any chest pain?”
  7. Previous pregnancies
    • “Is this your first pregnancy? Yes or no?”
    • As always: look for complications in previous pregnancies.

SUMMARY

Three groups of questions (after exploring the bleeding)

Amir’s final structure of history questions after CCVO bleeding:

  1. Group 1 – Red flag symptoms
    • Associated symptoms that are part of:
      • Preterm labor
      • Rupture
      • Placental abruption, and so on.
    • Includes: abdominal pain, contractions, fluid leakage, dizziness, baby kicking.
  2. Group 2 – Traumatic causes of bleeding
    • “Any traumatic bleeding?”
    • Includes:
      • Injuries / trauma
      • Domestic violence
      • Sex / intercourse-related bleeding
      • Cervical / vaginal causes
  3. Group 3 – Assess home & OB-GYN
    • Home assessment, including DV.
    • Obstetric – bloods, scans, preeclampsia, previous pregnancies, etc.

FINDINGS

Mild spotting when woke up

• Domestic violence, partner hits her under the influence of Alcohol and drugs

• Partner not happy with having a child

• Patient is smoking

• Works double jobs

• Missed folic acid as she cant afford it

• All scans normal

Diagnosis and differentials in this case

  • Main question: “What is your diagnosis?”
  • You must pick one or more diagnoses based on:
    • Bleeding (spotting), has stopped.
    • Fundal height normal.
    • No strong red flag findings in history given (e.g. no severe pain).

How to think through the differentials:

  1. Uterine rupture
    • Question: “How did you rule out rupture?”
    • Depends heavily on history:
      • No severe abdominal pain, no signs consistent with rupture.
  2. Placental abruption
    • Usual expectations (especially in concealed abruption):
      • Increased fundal height.
      • Uterine tenderness.
      • Abdominal pain that is getting worse.
  3. Preterm labor
    • Need:
      • Pain.
      • Characteristics of contractions (on-off, duration ↑, interval ↓).
  • In this stem:
    • Bleeding is spotting that has stopped.
    • Fundal height is normal.
    • Even if there is pain, not enough clear features to favor one over the others.
  • Conclusion:
    • You cannot confidently conclude a single diagnosis.
    • You have more reasons NOT to put one diagnosis at the top than to pick one.
    • Therefore, explain the possible differentials of antepartum hemorrhage to the patient and what you are concerned about.
    • You don’t have complete examination findings, so you cannot finalise the diagnosis.

Physical examination (PFE) – what this case really needs

Amir writes down a PFE that would help:

  1. General appearance
    • Level of consciousness.
    • Pallor.
  2. Vital signs
    • Blood pressure.
    • Pulse (asked earlier).
    • Postural drops – do not forget this.
  3. Abdominal examination
    • Usual structure, but with key points:
      • Leopold maneuvers (“low pulse maneuvers”).
      • Fundal height – “Tell me the fundal height.”
      • Fetal heart rate – “I want to check the fetal heart rate.”
      • Feel the uterus:
        • “Do I feel contractions?”
        • “Do I feel tenderness?”
  4. Pelvic examination
    • Inspection:
      • Look for bleeding and discharge.
    • Speculum examination:
      • Look at the cervix.
      • Key point:
        • “Tell me the os – open or closed?”
        • This gives a good answer on what’s happening.
      • Check:
        • Any tears?
        • Any bleeding from the vaginal walls?
    • No need for bimanual examination:
      • Uterus is large in the abdomen this late in pregnancy.
  • Purpose:
    • To decide between rupture, abruption, preterm labor, etc., and reach an exact diagnosis.

Explaining concerns and differentials to the patient

  • To wrap the case if exam stays as pure history + diagnosis:
  1. Main concern
    • “In your case, I’m concerned about an injury to your womb and the placenta which is bridge / tissue that connects mother to the baby.
  2. When we have trauma to the stomach in pregnant lady , Three main things we are concerned about
    • Rupture of the womb

    • Placental abruption
      • Placenta disconnects / detaches from the uterine wall.
    • Preterm (early) labor
      • Trauma and bleeding can sometimes trigger early labor.
  3. Reasons from history
    • Bleeding is spotting, mild.
    • Bleeding has stopped.
    • U don’t have abdominal pain, or if she does, it’s mild.
    • Therefore:
      • “I am not really concerned, I don’t have a great concern.”
      • But:
        • “We’ll need to investigate you further to figure out what is happening.”
  4. Other possible causes of bleeding
    • Vagina:
      • Bleeding from tears and lacerations in the vagina.
    • Cervix:
      • Problems like cervical ectropion.
      • Abnormalities in the cervix.
    • These are still possible causes of her bleeding.
  • Overall:
    • It’s paradoxical – she has bleeding, but it’s not bad, and you don’t have clear findings.
    • Your job is to show your differentials to the examiner and explain what you’re thinking.

Key PFE findings for each main differential

  1. Placental abruption
    • Tenderness of the uterus is the key point.
    • You would explain:
      • Abdominal pain that is constant, not on and off.
    • You may have:
      • Increased fundal height, especially if concealed abruption.
      • Not always, but it can happen.
  2. Preterm labor
    • Decision based on two things:
      • Contractions on history:
        • On-and-off pain.
        • Duration increasing.
        • Intervals decreasing.
      • Physical examination:
        • Open os on speculum exam.
  3. Uterine rupture
    • Expect:
      • Unstable lady.
      • Very severe abdominal pain.
      • Tender uterus.
    • Fundal height:
      • Uterus is ruptured, so the question is:
        • “Is there a fundus to feel?”
      • You don’t go looking for the fundal height the same way when the uterus is ruptured.

Final list of differentials

  • Top three on your list:
    • Uterine rupture
    • Placental abruption
    • Preterm labor
  • You can also add:
    • Placenta previa
    • Vasa previa
  • Key difference for placenta previa:
    • “Placenta previa is famously known as the painless bleeding that you have.”
    • Painless bleeding in late pregnancy.

Brief management outline (as per lecture)

  1. Immediate investigations for this case
    • Urgent ultrasound.
    • Urgent CTG – to check baby’s well-being.
    • Blood group – and start considering anti-D:
      • Antepartum hemorrhage → most of the time, you give anti-D if mother’s blood group is negative.
  2. Domestic violence management – mini outline
    • Communication and empathy
      • Emphasise:
        • “Everybody deserves to feel safe at home.”
        • “No one deserves to be hit or be subjected to physical abuse.”
    • Police notification
      • You can notify police, but:
        • DV is not one of your mandatory notifications.
        • Not like child abuse.
        • It is not your job to notify compulsorily.
        • If the patient wants, she can notify.
        • If she doesn’t want, it’s her choice.
    • Safety and escape planning
      • “Have some cash ready.”
      • “Have some clothes ready in case you need to leave home.”
      • “Maybe inform your family members – your mother, sisters – about what is happening.”
    • Support services
      • Refer to White Ribbon (support group).
        • They can arrange financial support.
        • They can provide psychological support.
    • Core concepts of DV management here
      • Financial support.
      • Psychological support.
    • If the lady decides not to notify and wants to stay:
      • “You really can’t do anything about it. That’s her decision.”

✏️ Corrections

✏️ Domestic-violence support directs the patient to 'White Ribbon' as the referral service.
White Ribbon Australia is a men's-behaviour-change awareness campaign, not a victim support/referral line (it ceased operations in 2019 and relaunched with a different remit). For a woman disclosing family violence in NSW, document referral to 1800RESPECT (national, 1800 737 732), the NSW Domestic Violence Line (1800 656 463), and local Multi-agency/police options; call 000 if immediate danger.
✏️ Anti-D is mentioned only as 'give anti-D if mother's blood group is negative' without dose or Kleihauer.
For a sensitising event/APH after 12 weeks, give anti-D immunoglobulin 625 IU IM to RhD-negative women and perform a Kleihauer to quantify fetomaternal haemorrhage and determine if additional anti-D is required (NBA/RANZCOG). Document blood group, antibody screen and Kleihauer result.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Use Australian spelling (haemorrhage, caesarean, oedema, labour) to meet NSW Health/ACSQHC standards.
⭐ Add placenta praevia/low-lying placenta definitions explicitly: RANZCOG defines a low-lying placenta as placental edge <20 mm (2 cm) from the internal os on transabdominal/transvaginal scan at >=20 weeks, and praevia as covering the os - so a placenta 5 cm from the os is correctly normal and needs no repeat scan.
⭐ Document a clear ABC/resuscitation and escalation plan for APH (two large-bore IV cannulae, FBC, group and hold/crossmatch, coagulation, CTG, senior obstetric review) and the mandatory speculum-not-bimanual rule when praevia is not excluded.
⭐ Record that the unborn child is not subject to mandatory child-protection notification in NSW, but any other children in the home ARE; document a safety/escape plan and folate provision (she reports missing folic acid) - links to RACGP antenatal and NSW Health DFV screening policy.
