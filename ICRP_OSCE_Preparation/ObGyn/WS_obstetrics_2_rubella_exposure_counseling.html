Rubella Exposure in Early Pregnancy - Counselling

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — obstetrics / class 2 (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: obstetrics_gynaecology

Scenario and Task

Your next patient is a 24-year-old woman who has come to an urgent appointment. She thinks she may be pregnant and has recently had contact with a confirmed case of rubella at the childcare centre where she works.

  • Take a focused history.
  • Explain the relevant investigations.
  • Counsel the patient regarding rubella exposure in pregnancy and further management.

Focused History

The exposure:

  • Where and when the contact occurred, and how close/prolonged the contact was.
  • Confirmation that the index case was laboratory-confirmed rubella.

Symptoms in the patient (rubella can be mild or subclinical):

  • Fever, maculopapular rash, lymphadenopathy, malaise, arthralgia.

Pregnancy and antenatal context:

  • Last menstrual period and menstrual regularity, symptoms of pregnancy, whether the pregnancy is planned, partner and support.
  • Previous pregnancies and their outcomes.
  • Whether first-trimester/booking bloods have been done (including her rubella immunity status).
  • Past medical history, medications, allergies, lifestyle and psychosocial screen.

Investigations

  • Confirm the pregnancy (urine pregnancy test, and establish gestational age - dating is important because fetal risk from rubella is highest in the first trimester).
  • Rubella serology - IgG and IgM:
    • IgG positive indicates existing immunity (from past infection or vaccination) - reassuring, with low risk to the baby from this exposure.
    • IgM positive suggests recent/acute infection.
  • Because of the serological window period, if initial results are equivocal or IgM/IgG are discordant, repeat serology in 2-3 weeks to detect seroconversion or a rising titre.

Counselling and Further Management

Why timing matters: maternal rubella infection in early pregnancy can cause congenital rubella syndrome (cataracts, congenital heart disease, sensorineural deafness, and other defects). The risk is greatest with infection before 12 weeks and declines with advancing gestation.

  • If she is immune (IgG positive, IgM negative): reassure that there is a very low risk to the baby; continue routine antenatal care.
  • If non-immune / acute infection is confirmed: explain sensitively that infection has occurred, discuss the risk to the fetus depending on gestation, and involve the obstetric/maternal-fetal medicine team. Options such as diagnostic testing (CVS/amniocentesis for fetal infection) and, where the risk is high and gestation permits, discussion of continuation versus termination of pregnancy, are handled with specialist input and appropriate counselling.
  • Do NOT give MMR vaccine during pregnancy (it is a live vaccine). For non-immune women, arrange MMR postpartum and advise avoiding pregnancy for 28 days after vaccination.
  • Advise avoiding further contact with anyone with rash/febrile illness for the remainder of the pregnancy.
  • Provide psychosocial support and clear follow-up; break bad news sensitively if infection is confirmed.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The notes discuss confirming and managing rubella exposure but do not mention statutory public health notification.
Rubella (including congenital rubella) is a NOTIFIABLE disease in all Australian states/territories. A confirmed or probable case must be notified to the local public health unit. This should be documented as part of management, alongside contact-tracing considerations at the childcare centre.
✏️ Post-exposure management for a non-immune pregnant woman is not clearly stated beyond serology.
Clarify that there is no effective post-exposure vaccine or routine immunoglobulin that reliably prevents congenital rubella in pregnancy; management is serological surveillance, specialist referral, and postpartum MMR for non-immune women (with avoidance of pregnancy for 28 days after MMR). Avoiding further exposure is advised.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Quantify the gestation-dependent fetal risk to strengthen counselling: congenital rubella syndrome risk is very high (up to ~90%) with maternal infection before 11-12 weeks and falls substantially after the first trimester, becoming low after ~20 weeks - this makes accurate dating central to the consultation.
