Headache in Late Pregnancy (Pre-eclampsia / Eclampsia)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — obstetrics / class 5 (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: obstetrics_gynaecology

PREGNANCY HEADACHE

Your next patient is a 30 year old lady who is 36 weeks pregnant who is complaining of headache.

Task:

Take history from the patient

PEFE/Physical examination will be given to you on a card

Explain diagnosis and differentials

KEY CONCEPTS OF THE CASE

  • It is preeclampsia
  • But also a headache case
  • Must consider differentials:
    • Pregnant differentials → Preeclampsia
    • Non-pregnant differentials → meningitis, subarachnoid hemorrhage, brain tumor, migraines, etc.
  • Approach the case as a headache case (same habit as chest pain structure).

1. HISTORY TAKING

A. Immediate Step

Vitals / Dynamic stability
 Ask examiner:

  • Blood pressure
  • Pulse rate
  • Respiratory rate
  • Saturation
  • Temperature

Examiner gives BP 150/90 → preeclampsia prompt.

B. Opening

  • Sit down, open-ended question
  • Patient complains of headache at 36 weeks
  • Address concerns:
    • “I understand how concerning this is… assess your headache… ask questions… examine you… make management plan.”

C. Explore Headache

SIQORAA mnemonic used

  1. Site
    • Key aim: unilateral or bilateral
  2. Intensity
    • Scale 1–10
  3. Quality
    • Throbbing?
    • Pressure sensation/tightening?
    • Dull ache?
  4. Onset
    • When started? Duration? On/off or constant? Getting worse?
  5. Radiation
  6. Alleviating/Aggravating factors

D. Differential Questions – Preeclampsia + OB History

1. Preeclampsia Symptoms (top of list)

  • Vision problems
  • Dizziness
  • Abdominal pain
  • Nausea/vomiting
  • Yellowish discoloration of skin
  • Swelling in legs
  • Chest pain or shortness of breath
  • Oliguria (“decrease in amount of urine you are passing”)

2. OB Differentials / Obstetric History

(Do faster than usual)

  • Routine bloods normal?
  • Down syndrome screening?
  • Morphology scan 18–20 weeks?
  • Sweet ring test (depending on gestational age)
  • Baby kicking?
  • Any vaginal bleeding?
  • Any discharge/fluid leakage?
  • Previous pregnancies
    • Any high blood pressure before?
  • Past medical history (esp. kidney disease)

Important points often forgotten: baby movements, bleeding, discharge.

E. Non-Pregnancy Serious Differentials

1. Meningitis

  • Fever/chills
  • Rashes
  • Nausea/vomiting (if not yet asked)
  • Photophobia (“Do bright lights hurt your eyes?”)

2. Subarachnoid hemorrhage / ICH (thunderclap headache)

  • Sudden onset?
  • “Most severe headache of your life?”
  • Optional: head injuries, insect bites (not preferred)

3. Brain Tumor

Challenges in pregnancy: vomiting and weight loss not useful.
Ask instead:

  • Headache worse on coughing/sneezing (↑ICP)
  • Neurological deficit: weakness? numbness?
  • Sensory/motor deficit
  • Night pain: “Does the headache wake you up at night?”

F. Less-Important Differentials (only if time)

  • Migraine
    • Photophobia, nausea/vomiting, vision problems already asked
    • Family history
  • Tension
    • Stress at home/work
  • Sinusitis / Dental infections
    • Dental hygiene
    • Secretions behind throat

Stop history here.

2. PHYSICAL EXAMINATION (PFE) – Ask Examiner

A. General Appearance

  • Level of consciousness
  • Rashes
  • Jaundice

B. Vital Signs (again if needed)

  • BP (key)
  • Temperature (key)

C. Neurological Examination (priority)

  • Reflexes → hyperreflexia, clonus
  • Fundoscopy
    • Blurring of optic disc (papilledema)
  • Cranial nerves
  • Upper & lower limb neuro exam

D. ENT (quick)

  • Sinus tenderness
  • Dental hygiene / dental infection
  • (or throat exam; only one or two areas)

E. Cardiovascular

  • S1/S2 normal?
  • Any murmurs?
  • Any added sounds?

F. Respiratory

  • Air entry equal?
  • Added sounds? (bibasal creps is key concern)

G. Abdominal Examination

  • Liver tenderness
  • Uterine tenderness
  • Fundal height
  • Fetal heart rate
  • Lie/presentation
  • (If speculum asked earlier, fine)

H. Lower Limbs

  • Edema (pitting or non-pitting)

I. Urine Dipstick

  • Proteins (3+ or 4+)

3. TYPICAL FINDINGS GIVEN

  • BP 150/90
  • Blurring of optic disc (papilledema)
  • +Hyperreflexia and clonus
  • Fetal heart/lie/presentationnormal
  • Protein 3+ / 4+

4. EVENT IN EXAM – PATIENT STARTS HAVING A FIT

(Old face-to-face feature; now occurs again)

What to do:

  • Move to resuscitate room
  • Start magnesium sulfate
  • If ongoing: benzodiazepine (diazepam or midazolam)

5. ECLAMPSIA MANAGEMENT FRAMEWORK

1. Resuscitate

  • DRSA–ABC
  • Check airway, breathing, circulation
  • Left lateral position
  • Two IV cannulas

2. Stop seizures

  • IV magnesium sulfate
    • Loading dose ~4 g (memory trick: “sulfate is SO₄ → 4 g”)
  • If seizure continues:
    • Diazepam OR midazolam

3. Stop blood pressure

  • Severe BP → IV hydralazine / IV labetalol

4. Stop pregnancy

  • “Plan for delivery ASAP”

Additional Monitoring

  • Vital signs every 30 min
  • CTG where appropriate
  • Reflexes (for magnesium toxicity) check every hour

6. DIAGNOSIS

  • If no fit: Preeclampsia
  • If fit occurs: ECLAMPSIA

7. EXPLAIN DIAGNOSIS TO THE PATIENT

“Most likely you are having a condition called eclampsia.
This is a condition that involves multiple organs.
It causes high blood pressure, leaking of proteins from the kidney, and can lead to seizures.”

Reasons (as per findings):

  • Headache
  • High BP
  • Protein in urine
  • Swelling/papilledema on eye exam
  • Hyperreflexia/clonus
  • You had a seizure → therefore eclampsia

8. DIFFERENTIALS

Differentials of the complaint (headache), not of preeclampsia.

  • Meningitis / encephalitis
  • Subarachnoid hemorrhage / intracranial hemorrhage
  • Brain tumors
  • Migraine
  • Tension headache
  • Sinusitis
  • Dental infections
  • Cluster headaches
  • Refractive errors

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: clinical-documentation-expert (Australian teaching hospital clinical supervisor) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ Papilloedema ('blurring of optic disc') is listed as a typical finding of pre-eclampsia/eclampsia.
Frank papilloedema is NOT a characteristic finding in pre-eclampsia. Visual symptoms usually arise from retinal arteriolar vasospasm, retinal oedema/exudates or occipital cortical involvement (as in PRES). True papilloedema should instead raise suspicion of raised intracranial pressure from another cause (space-occupying lesion, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis). Reframe the expected fundoscopy findings as retinal vasospasm/oedema rather than papilloedema.
✏️ Magnesium sulfate is described only as a '~4 g' loading dose with toxicity monitoring limited to 'reflexes every hour'.
Specify the full eclampsia protocol: magnesium sulfate 4 g IV loading over 15-20 minutes, then a maintenance infusion (1-2 g/hour), continued for 24 hours after delivery or the last seizure. Monitor for magnesium toxicity via respiratory rate, deep tendon reflexes AND urine output, and keep calcium gluconate available as the antidote. Magnesium is first-line; benzodiazepines are second-line only if seizures persist.
✏️ American spellings throughout ('hemorrhage', 'tumor', 'edema', 'papilledema', 'discoloration').
Australian documentation requires haemorrhage, tumour, oedema, papilloedema, discolouration. Standardise terminology for AHPRA-compliant notes.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add safe analgesia guidance: paracetamol is first-line for headache in pregnancy; avoid analgesic-dose NSAIDs/aspirin in the third trimester (risk of premature ductus arteriosus closure) - noting this is distinct from low-dose aspirin pre-eclampsia prophylaxis.
⭐ Add pregnancy-specific neurological differentials to the headache list: cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (pregnancy/postpartum is prothrombotic) and posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES), which overlaps with eclampsia.
⭐ Add neuroimaging trigger: if headache features are atypical for pre-eclampsia (thunderclap, worst-ever, focal deficit, or seizure without hypertension/proteinuria), arrange urgent CT/MRI to exclude subarachnoid haemorrhage, CVST or space-occupying lesion.
⭐ Make the eclampsia escalation explicit: 000 / MET call, summon senior obstetric and anaesthetic help, left lateral position, magnesium, control severe hypertension (IV labetalol/hydralazine) and expedite delivery - and continue magnesium 24 hours post last seizure/delivery.
⭐ Add metadata linking to the sibling pre-eclampsia and abdominal-pain cases so the platform can present the shared pre-eclampsia red-flag screen as a reusable teaching module.
