Mitral Stenosis in Pregnancy (Rheumatic Heart Disease)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — obstetrics / class 4 (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: obstetrics_gynaecology

MITRAL STENOSIS ANTENATAL CASES

Your next patient is a 24 year old lady who has presented to you for her routine antenatal care.

Task:

Take history

• Physical examination will be given on a card

• Explain your diagnosis and differentials

• (Anyway lets talk about the Mx too)

1. Opening the Station

  • Sit down, introduce yourself.
  • “My name is Amir. How can I help you today?”
  • Open–ended question.
  • Patient: positive home pregnancy test, attending for routine pregnancy checks.
  • Planned pregnancy? → Yes.
  • “Congratulations… How are things going?”
  • Establish rapport.

2. Antenatal History – The 5 Ps

Period

  • LMP?
  • Are periods regular?
  • Pregnancy symptoms (tiredness, nausea/vomiting).
  • Vomiting once some days → normal.

Partner

  • Stable relationship?
  • Supportive?
  • Home situation?
  • Any past STIs?

Pregnancy

  • First pregnancy? → Yes.
  • Cervical screening up to date? (if >25 years).

Past Medical History

  • Any medical conditions? Medications?
  • Revealed here:
    • “When I was younger, my GP told me I have a murmur.”
    • Diagnosed in childhood after fever + joint pain.
    • Got monthly antibiotic injections for a long time.
    • Stopped follow-up; GP said no need.

Murmur – Four Mandatory Questions

  1. When were you diagnosed?
  2. Any treatment? (Antibiotics monthly.)
  3. Regular follow-ups? → No.
  4. Any complications? Looking for heart failure symptoms:
    • Chest pain?
    • Shortness of breath (esp. on exertion)?
    • Swelling in legs?
    • SOB at night or lying down?
    • Racing heart?

Family History

  • Any medical conditions?
  • Any genetic conditions?

Lifestyle

  • Diet, exercise, alcohol, drugs, coffee.

Psychological

  • Mood, coping with pregnancy.

Routine questioning complete.

3. History Findings

    • LMP 10 weeks ago
    • Childhood diagnosis of murmur after fever + joint pain.
    • Antibiotics for some time.
    • No follow-up.

Physical Examination Findings

    • Card findings:
      • Mid-diastolic murmur at the mitral area (5th intercostal space, mid-clavicular line).
      • Low-pitched, rumbling, “famous” mitral stenosis murmur (heard best with bell).

Teaching Point

  • Diastolic murmurs are never physiologic in pregnancy.
  • Only systolic murmurs can be physiologic.

4. Diagnosis Explanation

  • “Most likely you have a condition called mitral stenosis.”
  • Use gateway as the layman term:
    • “There is a gateway in your heart called the mitral valve.”
    • “There is narrowing in this gateway.”
  • Link to childhood:
    • “This is usually the damage of childhood throat infection called rheumatic fever.”

5. Differentials

  1. Other valvular problems (other gateways like aortic stenosis)
  2. Congenital heart disease
  3. Heart failure
  4. Cardiomyopathies (dilated, constrictive, hypertrophic – “we love HOCM”)
  5. Arrhythmias
  6. Anemia (thin blood → murmur)
  7. Thyroid disease (hyperthyroidism can give murmur)
  8. Physiologic murmur in pregnancy — but NOT diastolic (so not applicable here)

6. Counseling

Background

  • Mild mitral stenosis can remain asymptomatic until pregnancy (due to ↑ blood volume and ↑ LA pressure).
  • Moderate/severe stenosis → symptoms before pregnancy, worsen during pregnancy.
  • Heart is the system most stressed in pregnancy due to volume changes.
  • Complications ↑ with atrial fibrillation.
  • Valvular diseases often accompanied by:
    • Atrial fibrillation
    • Heart failure

7. Management Framework

Investigations — FIRST and MOST IMPORTANT

  1. Echocardiogram (Echo)
    • Ultrasound of the heart
    • Assess:
      • Ejection fraction
      • Degree of narrowing (mild, moderate, severe)
      • Amount of blood pumped
  2. ECG
    • Look for irregular heartbeats
    • Atrial fibrillation repeatedly emphasized
  3. Severity assessment dictates management

8. Management Based on Severity

If MILD Mitral Stenosis

  • Monitor for symptoms
  • Continuous monitoring during delivery
  • Good pain control
  • Regular ECGs
  • Prefer delivery in a tertiary hospital

If MODERATE / SEVERE Mitral Stenosis

  • Fluid and salt restriction
  • Stop exercise
  • May give beta blockers
  • If medical therapy fails and there are repeated AF episodes or persistent HF →
    Percutaneous valvotomy during pregnancy (if anatomy suitable)

9. Delivery Planning

  • Delivery is more challenging than pregnancy (pain → ↑ cardiac output).
  • Mode of birth decided by an obstetrician.

Mild, non-complicated

  • Tertiary hospital
  • Continuous ECG monitoring
  • Vaginal delivery possible (depends)

Moderate to Severe

  • Must deliver at a centre with:
    • Adult cardiac services
    • Invasive monitoring capacity
  • Decision: vaginal vs cesarean based on specialist advice
  • Most important: pain control
  • No blanket rule that severe cases must have C-section.

10. Final Key Teaching Points

  • Always ask murmur complication questions (HF symptoms).
  • Look for atrial fibrillation on ECG — affects management.
  • Remember:
    • Systolic murmur in pregnancy → OK.
    • Diastolic murmur → NEVER normal.
  • Linking cases:
    • Chest discomfort case = physiologic systolic murmur.
    • Antenatal screening case = diastolic murmur → pathological.

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: clinical-documentation-expert (Australian teaching hospital clinical supervisor) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The case repeatedly emphasises detecting atrial fibrillation on ECG but the management plan never mentions anticoagulation.
Mitral stenosis with atrial fibrillation carries a very high left-atrial thromboembolic/stroke risk and mandates therapeutic anticoagulation. In pregnancy this requires specialist-guided choice (therapeutic low-molecular-weight heparin such as enoxaparin, or warfarin with its known fetal risks weighed against efficacy for high-risk valve/AF). This must be added as a core management step, not just AF detection.
✏️ History notes childhood rheumatic fever with monthly penicillin injections that were stopped because 'GP said no need', and this is not challenged.
Under Australian RHD guidelines (RHDAustralia / ARF-RHD guideline), secondary prophylaxis with benzathine benzylpenicillin G IM every 3-4 weeks is long-term (often to age 21-40+ depending on severity) and premature cessation is frequently inappropriate. Rheumatic heart disease disproportionately affects Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities. The case should prompt reassessment of prophylaxis status and preconception/antenatal cardiology review.
✏️ Delivery/management section does not risk-stratify maternal cardiac risk or specify infective endocarditis prophylaxis guidance.
Add the modified WHO (mWHO) maternal cardiovascular risk classification - significant mitral stenosis is mWHO III (or IV if severe), requiring management by a multidisciplinary pregnancy heart team. Clarify that routine antibiotic IE prophylaxis for uncomplicated vaginal or caesarean birth is NOT recommended in current guidelines unless there is active infection, to prevent over-prescribing.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add anticoagulation/thromboprophylaxis detail (therapeutic LMWH vs warfarin trade-offs) and explicit VTE risk assessment - central to MS-in-pregnancy management and currently absent.
⭐ Add rheumatic heart disease secondary prophylaxis (benzathine benzylpenicillin) with the Australian RHDAustralia reference and note the high burden in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander populations.
⭐ Add preconception counselling and early referral to a joint cardiac-obstetric (pregnancy heart team) clinic, plus mWHO risk stratification, so severity drives the care pathway.
⭐ Specify pharmacotherapy detail: beta-blocker of choice is metoprolol/labetalol to slow heart rate and prolong diastolic filling; diuretics (frusemide) for pulmonary congestion; avoid ACE inhibitors/ARBs (contraindicated in pregnancy).
⭐ Add FBC (anaemia exacerbates a flow murmur and cardiac symptoms) and thyroid function tests to the investigations, given anaemia and hyperthyroidism appear in the murmur differential list.
