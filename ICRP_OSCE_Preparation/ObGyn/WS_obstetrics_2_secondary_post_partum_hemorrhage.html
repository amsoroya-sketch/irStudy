Secondary Postpartum Haemorrhage (Day 10 - Retained Products / Endometritis)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — obstetrics / class 7 (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: obstetrics_gynaecology

SECONDARY POST PARTUM HEMORRHAGE

Secondary Postpartum Hemorrhage – Structured Notes (Based ONLY on Transcript)

Anti-D (Before Starting the Case)

  • Not a key point of postpartum hemorrhages.
  • In primary PPH with massive bleeding (e.g., 1.5L), anti-D is not a priority.
  • Same for postpartum hemorrhage in general — more important things to worry about.
  • In 2nd and 3rd trimester, any obstetric hemorrhage creates the need to give anti-D.
  • For Rh-negative women:
    • If mother is negative and baby is positive, that is when it becomes serious.
    • Prophylactic anti-D offered to all Rh-negative women:
      • One dose at 28 weeks
      • One dose at 34 weeks
    • After delivery, if baby is positive → consider another dose.
    • Usually needs to be given within 72 hours.
    • Anti-D is an intramuscular injection.
  • For primary PPH cases: if blood group is negative, anti-D should NOT be brought up before finishing the entire 14-point management.

Definition

  • Secondary postpartum hemorrhage = bleeding beyond 24 hours.
  • Any bleeding beyond 24 hours up to 12 weeks after delivery (first 3 months postpartum).
  • Guidelines do not specify how much blood is significant.
  • Some lochia/discharge is normal, but it should not stay bloody.

Causes

  1. Retained Products of Conception (Main Important Thing)
    • Small piece remaining inside.
    • May not cause massive bleeding immediately.
    • Later (days to weeks) starts causing bleeding.
    • Anything left inside prevents uterine contraction and involution.
  2. Infections (Red Flag / Most Concerning)
    • Endometritis, myometritis, parametritis.
    • Infection in any layer of the uterus.
    • Can occur after vaginal delivery or cesarean section.
  3. Trauma
    • Missed vaginal laceration
    • Hematomas
    • Episiotomy that later starts bleeding
    • Same traumas and tears discussed previously.
  4. Bleeding Disorders
    • Pre-existing bleeding disorder.
    • Didn’t bleed badly initially, but bleeding doesn’t stop later.
  5. Non-Pregnancy-Related Uterine Problems
    • Fibroid
    • Cervical cancer
    • Cervical polyp
    • Bleeding may not be pregnancy-related.

Normal Uterine Involution

  • Immediately after delivery → firm uterus at level of umbilicus (≈20 weeks).
  • Around 10 days postpartum → at symphysis pubis.
  • Around 6 weeks → normal size, barely palpable on bimanual exam.
  • If uterus is palpable at 10 days → not good.

History

  • Explore bleeding.
  • Delivery history + obstetric history:
    • Previous postpartum hemorrhage?
    • Route/mode of delivery?
    • Retained products more common in NVDs.
    • Endometritis more common in cesarean sections.
  • Risk factors for retained products:
    • Multiple gestations
    • Preterm birth
    • Retained placentas
  • Ask for features of endometritis:
    • Fever
    • Foul-smelling discharge (different from normal lochia)
  • Ask about bleeding disorders:
    • Previous heavy periods
    • Nosebleeds
    • Other bleeding
    • Family history
    • Blood thinners (may have restarted postpartum)
  • Remember:
    • Bleeding might be her initial period.
    • In non-breastfeeding mothers, it may come earlier and may be heavy.
  • First week postpartum → think more about coagulopathy.
  • Ask about:
    • Parity
    • Labor
    • Mode of delivery
    • Temperature
    • Other vitals

Physical Examination

  • Look for:
    • Tears (vaginal/cervical sources)
    • Endometritis features
      • Very similar to PID
      • Uterine tenderness
      • Fever
      • Sepsis features
      • Abdominal pain
      • Bad foul-smelling vaginal discharge
  • Risk factors for endometritis:
    • Intercourse after delivery
    • Tampons
    • Mirena/IUD insertion increases risk
  • Key point #1: Uterus size
    • If size is abnormal (e.g., still in abdomen at 2 weeks) → either infection or something stuck inside.
  • Key point #2: Check the os
    • Os normally closes 1–2 days after delivery.
    • If still open → problem.

Investigations

  • Blood cultures
  • Swabs (significant part due to concern for endometritis)
  • Blood group and crossmatch
  • Full blood examination
  • Coagulation profile
  • Serum beta-hCG (maybe, especially if no ultrasound)
  • Ultrasound (very important)
    • To check what's inside
    • To ensure nothing is left behind

Important Line

  • All patients with suspected retained products of conception require antibiotic cover because there is always an element of infection/endometritis.

Emergency Department Approach

  • When she arrives:
    • Grab bloods
    • Grab swabs
    • Start antibiotics immediately
    • Do ultrasound afterward
    • Cause can be discussed later
  • For secondary postpartum hemorrhage:
    • Complete history
    • Good clinical assessment
    • Investigations
    • Order ultrasound (may be difficult)

Treatment

  • Includes:
    • Resuscitation if massive bleeding
    • Admission
    • Consider IV antibiotics
  • Do NOT memorize antibiotic names or doses.
  • Stating “I start this patient on antibiotics” is sufficient.

Most Important Exam Message

  • Show the examiner that you are considering the differentials of secondary postpartum hemorrhage.

Your next patient is a 25 year old lady who presented to the ED complaining of vaginal bleeding. She delivered a 3.9kg baby boy via NVD 10 days ago. She had a long labour and had an episiotomy during delivery.

Task:

• Take history from the patient - You will have a prompt time at 4 minutes

• Ask physical examination from the examiner - you will only be given findings if you ask specifically

• Explain your diagnosis and differentials

Anti-D (context before case)

  • Anti-D is not a key point in primary or secondary postpartum hemorrhage.
  • In a primary PPH with 1.5 L blood loss, anti-D is not a priority – there are more important things to manage first.
  • Same idea in secondary PPH – focus is on serious differentials; anti-D is low priority in the acute setting.
  • General reminders about Anti-D:
    • Done for Rh-negative (blood group negative) women.
    • Serious when mother is negative and baby is positive.
    • Prophylactic Anti-D:
      • Offered to all Rh-negative women.
      • One dose at 28 weeks.
      • One dose at 34 weeks.
    • After delivery:
      • If baby is Rh-positive, consider another dose.
      • Usually needs to be given within 72 hours if possible.
    • Anti-D is given as an intramuscular injection.
  • In primary PPH cases:
    • Even if blood group is negative, do not bring up Anti-D before finishing the entire 14-point management.
    • Only consider it after completing main management.

Secondary Postpartum Hemorrhage – Definition & Timeframe

  • Secondary postpartum hemorrhage:
    • Any bleeding beyond 24 hours after delivery.
    • Up to 12 weeks after delivery (first 3 months postpartum).
  • Guidelines do not specify how much blood is “significant”.
  • Some lochia/discharge is normal:
    • But expectation is that it should not stay bloody going down the track.
  • If a lady returns in the first 3 months with bleeding → must consider several causes.

Causes of Secondary Postpartum Hemorrhage

  1. Retained Products of Conception (RPOC) – Main Important Thing
    • Some products of conception still inside:
      • A small piece of membrane or placenta.
    • May not cause massive bleeding immediately after delivery.
    • After 3–4 days or 1 week, starts causing bleeding.
    • Any tissue left inside:
      • Uterus cannot contract properly.
      • Involution of the uterus does not occur.
  2. Infections – Red Flag / Most Concerning
    • Endometritis, myometritis, parametritis:
      • Infection in any layer of the uterus.
    • Usually we refer to it as endometritis.
    • Can occur after both:
      • Vaginal delivery.
      • Cesarean section.
  3. Trauma
    • Missed vaginal laceration.
    • Hematomas.
    • Episiotomy that later starts bleeding.
    • Same traumas and tears as previously discussed.
  4. Bleeding Disorders
    • Pre-existing bleeding disorder:
      • May not bleed much immediately after delivery.
      • Bleeding later does not stop.
  5. Non-pregnancy-related Uterine Problems
    • Bleeding may be unrelated to pregnancy:
      • Fibroid.
      • Cervical cancer.
      • Cervical polyp.
    • Because definition includes any bleeding from 24 hours to 3 months postpartum.

Normal Uterine Involution (Expected Uterus Size After Delivery)

  • Immediately after delivery:
    • Uterus still large but firm.
    • At the level of the umbilicus (about 20 weeks size).
  • Around 10 days postpartum:
    • Uterus should be at the level of the symphysis pubis.
    • If you still feel something in the stomach/abdomen at 10 days → not good.
  • Around 6 weeks postpartum:
    • Uterus should be normal size.
    • Barely palpable on bimanual examination.

Key History Points (General for Secondary PPH)

  • Main presenting complaint: bleeding after recent delivery.
  • Important to always consider:
    • Retained products of conception.
    • Endometritis/infection.
    • Trauma/tears.
    • Bleeding disorders.
    • Non-pregnancy-related causes (fibroid, cervical lesions, etc.).
  • Endometritis features:
    • Fever.
    • Abdominal pain.
    • Bad foul-smelling vaginal discharge – different from normal lochia.
    • May have sepsis features.
  • Risk factors for bleeding disorders:
    • Previous heavy periods.
    • Nosebleeds.
    • Other unusual bleeding.
    • Family history of bleeding problems.
    • Being on blood thinners:
      • Might have been stopped during pregnancy.
      • Restarted postpartum (e.g., warfarin, aspirin, other blood thinners).
  • First week postpartum:
    • If secondary PPH happens in the first few days, often put down to coagulopathy (bleeding disorder).
  • Remember:
    • Bleeding could be her initial period:
      • In some mothers (especially if not breastfeeding), may come earlier.
      • May be heavier and a bit different to regular periods.
  • Take history for complications in previous pregnancies:
    • Helps in suspecting similar complications now.

General Physical Examination – Key Points

  • Look for:
    • Tears – vaginal/cervical sources of bleeding.
    • Signs of endometritis:
      • Uterine tenderness.
      • Fever.
      • Abdominal pain.
      • Bad foul-smelling discharge.
      • Possible sepsis features.
  • Risk factors for endometritis:
    • Resumption of sexual intercourse after delivery.
    • Use of tampons.
    • Postpartum Mirena/IUD insertion (increases risk of endometritis).
  • Key point #1: Uterus size on examination.
    • If size is abnormal for expected time:
      • Example: 2 weeks after delivery, uterus still in abdomen.
      • Think infection or something stuck inside.
  • Key point #2: Os (cervical opening).
    • Os should close within 1–2 days after delivery.
    • If still open → indicates a problem.

Investigations (General for Secondary PPH)

  • Blood cultures.
  • Swabs:
    • Vaginal or high vaginal swabs.
    • Important for endometritis/infection evaluation.
  • Blood group and crossmatch:
    • She is bleeding anyway.
  • Full blood examination.
  • Coagulation profile/screening:
    • Looking for bleeding disorders.
  • Serum β-hCG:
    • Consider especially if no ultrasound available.
  • Ultrasound – very important:
    • To see what’s happening inside.
    • To make sure nothing has been left behind (e.g. retained products).

Key Principle – RPOC and Antibiotics

  • All patients with suspected retained products of conception require antibiotic cover.
    • Because there is always an element of endometritis/infection when tissue is left inside.
    • If a piece of tissue has been left there for two weeks, there will be some infection (significant or mild).

Emergency Department Approach (General)

  • In ED, when patient presents with secondary PPH:
    • Grab bloods.
    • Grab swabs.
    • Start antibiotics immediately:
      • “I don’t care what the cause is, give antibiotics first; by the time we do ultrasound, she already has antibiotics.”
    • Then:
      • Do ultrasound.
      • Then discuss and refine the cause.
  • Overall approach for secondary PPH:
    • Complete history taking.
    • Good clinical assessment.
    • Order investigations.
    • Order ultrasound (sometimes hard to arrange, but important).

Treatment (General Overview)

  • If massive bleeding:
    • Includes resuscitation.
  • Admit the patient.
  • Consider IV antibiotics:
    • Do not need to memorize specific antibiotic names or doses in the exam.
    • It is enough to say: “I will start this patient on antibiotics.”
  • Most important:
    • Show the examiner that you are considering the differentials of secondary postpartum hemorrhage.

AMC OSCE Case – Secondary Postpartum Hemorrhage (10 Days Post NVD)

Case Stem

  • 25-year-old lady, presents to Emergency Department with bleeding.
  • Delivered a 3.9 kg baby, normal vaginal delivery (NVD), 10 days ago.
  • Had a long labor.
  • Episiotomy was done (they always mention it).
  • Already in the secondary postpartum hemorrhage timeframe.

Task

  • Take history from the patient.
  • Ask for physical examination findings from the examiner (in face-to-face exam).
  • Explain diagnosis and differentials.
  • Lecturer also discusses management, although he hasn’t seen this as a specific management task often.

Initial Approach in the OSCE Room

  • Enter, examiner has already checked you.
  • Sanitize hands – done in all cases now.
  • Confirm patient: this is Mary, your patient.
  • Ask examiner:
    • “Do you have any questions before we start?”
    • Then ask for vital signs:
      • Blood pressure.
      • Pulse rate.
      • Oxygen saturation.
      • Respiratory rate.
      • Temperature – most important in this case.
        • More important than blood pressure here.
  • Examiner will tell you early:
    • Patient is febrile → gives away the case early.
    • Example vitals: BP 110/70, pulse 88, etc.
  • If vitals show semi-unstable:
    • You can say: “I would take this patient to the resuscitation cubicle because my task is history taking.”
    • But exam usually keeps them stable so you can focus on history.

Communication – Opening & Concern

  • Go beside the bed, lady lying on bed.
  • Use open-ended question:
    • “Mary, my name is Amir. I’ll be taking care of you today. How can I help you?”
  • She says:
    • “Doctor, I started bleeding. I delivered my baby 10 days ago. I’m very concerned why it’s not stopping.”
  • Address concern (empathic + reassuring component):
    • Acknowledge seriousness: “I understand it is very serious; thank you for coming in.”
    • Explain plan: “I’ll assess your situation, find the cause, and we’ll figure this out together.”
    • Reassure: “I promise to make the best management plan for you and take care of you. Is that okay?”

Component 2 – Explore the Bleeding

1. Timing and Pattern

  • “When did you start bleeding?”
  • Is the bleeding on and off or constant?
    • She may say it is constant.
  • Is it getting worse? (Yes/No – doesn’t change much, but still asked.)

2. Characteristics of Bleeding

  • Color:
    • “Is it bright red or dark red?”
    • To see if it’s fresh bleeding or more clots.
  • Content:
    • “Are you passing any clots?”
  • Discharge:
    • “Do you have any discharge?”
  • Volume:
    • “How many pads do you use every day?”
    • “Are they fully soaked?”

3. Smell

  • “Does it have a bad smell?”
    • This becomes important here:
      • Looking for foul-smelling discharge/bleeding → suggests infection/endometritis.

4. Red Flags of Bleeding

  • Ask about:
    • Dizziness.
    • Feeling tired.
  • Reason: checking for hypovolemia/anemia from ongoing bleeding.

Differential-Focused History Structure

1. Obstetric and Delivery History (Tissue/Trauma/RPOC)

  • Still obstetric history, but can call it delivery history taking.
  • Ask:
    • Mode of delivery:
      • “Did you deliver by vaginal delivery or cesarean section?”
      • Ask even if already written in the stem.
    • Gestation:
      • “Was it a term or preterm delivery?”
    • Labor:
      • “Was it a long labor?”
    • Instruments:
      • “Did they use any instruments?”
    • Cuts:
      • “Did they do any cuts?” (episiotomy).
        • Then: “Is the cut healing well?”
          • Very relevant 10 days after the cut.
    • Pregnancy details:
      • “Was it a single or multiple pregnancy?”
      • “Is this your first pregnancy?”
    • Previous postpartum hemorrhage:
      • If not first pregnancy:
        • “Did you have any bleeding after your last delivery?”

RPOC-related question (Tissue/Placenta)

  • Hard to ask directly to patient, so phrase as:
    • “Are you aware of any problems in delivering the placenta?”
      • She might say:
        • Cord snapped.
        • They had difficulty pulling it out.
        • It came out late.
  • These answers increase suspicion of retained products of conception.

Bleeding after delivery / prior surgery

  • Ask:
    • “Any surgeries or bleeding after delivery?”
    • Looking for primary PPH that stopped, and now bleeding again at day 3 or day 10.

2. Infections – Endometritis-Focused History

  • Symptoms:
    • Abdominal pain with the bleeding?
    • Any fever or chills?
    • Any vaginal discharge?
      • Can be asked earlier in CCVO, but repeated here in infection section.
  • Risk factors:
    • “Have you started sexual activity again?”
      • Even if only 10 days, still asked as a risk factor.
    • “Do you use tampons for the lochia/discharge instead of pads?”
    • You can ask:
      • “Did you put any intrauterine device in after delivery?”
    • If there is a “risk factor” box, these already tick it off.
  • Risk factors are added here due to significance, not just recall.

3. Bleeding Disorders

  • Ask about symptoms of bleeding disorder:
    • Easy bruising.
    • Recurrent nosebleeds.
    • Heavy periods:
      • Also helps pick up uterine problems like fibroid, so covers two things.
  • Family history:
    • “Any family history of bleeding disorders?”
  • Medications:
    • “Are you on any blood thinners?”
      • Lecturer likes to always ask this.

4. Other Relevant Questions

  • If you want cervical bleeding as a differential:
    • Ask:
      • “Are you up to date with your cervical screening?”
  • If still have time:
    • “How is the baby doing?”
    • “How are you coping with motherhood?”
      • Postpartum is not easy for either parent, especially the mother.
  • Optional:
    • “What is your blood group?”
      • Lecturer is not a fan of blood group, but you can ask it.

Physical Examination in This Case

General Appearance

  • Look for:
    • Is patient alert or drowsy?
    • Is patient pale?
    • Is patient in pain?
    • Signs of dehydration:
      • Important because dealing with infection and possible sepsis.
  • Vital signs:
    • Already asked at the start (including temperature).
    • Can add postural drop:
      • Bleeding case → adds extra value:
        • “Do I have any postural drop in this lady?”

Abdominal Examination

  • Start here before pelvic exam.
    • Pelvic bimanual exam is technically both abdomen + vaginal.
  • Inspection:
    • Look for distension:
      • 10 days after delivery, you don’t expect much distension because uterus should have gone down.
    • (Can also mention abdomen moving with respiration if you wish.)
  • Palpation:
    • Look for:
      • Any tenderness.
      • Any masses.
    • Examiner will say:
      • Tenderness in lower abdomen.
      • A mass in lower abdomen.

Clarifying the mass

  • When you ask, “Is that mass the uterus?” the examiner says:
    • “I don’t know, you have to tell me.”
  • Key specific question:
    • “Can I feel the lower border of the mass?”
    • Or:
      • “Can I go below the mass?”
    • If you cannot feel the lower border because of the symphysis pubis:
      • It suggests an abdominopelvic mass (like the uterus).

Pelvic Examination

Preparation

  • “With the consent of my patient, I would like to offer a chaperone.”
  • Then proceed.

Inspection

  • Key point many candidates forget:
    • First: assess episiotomy:
      • “Is the episiotomy healing well or is it bleeding?”
    • Then:
      • Look for bleeding.
      • Look for discharge.
      • Look for tears.
      • Look for swelling, redness, rashes, bruises, etc.
  • Episiotomy is treated as a tear and is the key inspection point.

Speculum Examination

  • Not contraindicated here.
  • Key point:
    • You must comment on the os.
  • Before listing everything else (bleeding, discharge, lesions, tears), say:
    • “I am looking at the os.”
  • Examiner tells you:
    • At 10 days after delivery, the os is open.

Bimanual Examination

  • Steps:
    1. Cervical motion tenderness (CMT):
      • Do CMT.
      • Positive in endometritis/cervicitis.
      • Whether positive or negative doesn’t change big picture much, but here assume positive for infection.
    2. Uterus:
      • Ask for size:
        • Examiner says: 10 weeks.
        • That is a bit above symphysis; involution is not complete.
      • Key question:
        • “Is it tender?”
          • Examiner: Yes, uterus is tender.
      • Border:
        • “Is the border regular?”
          • Answer: Yes, regular.
    3. Adnexae:
      • Ask for adnexal tenderness and masses:
        • Examiner: No adnexal tenderness and no adnexal masses.

Summary of Findings in This Patient

  • Open os, 10 days after delivery (not normal).
  • Uterus size 10 weeks – larger than expected at day 10.
  • Uterus is tender on palpation.
  • Positive cervical motion tenderness.
  • Patient has fever (e.g., 38.5°C).
  • She has ongoing bleeding.

Diagnosis and Explanation to Patient

Most Likely Diagnosis

  • Endometritis (postpartum endometritis).
  • Even if RPOC is present, the diagnosis here is endometritis.

Explanation in Simple Terms

  • “This is an infection in the inner lining of your womb.”
  • “This is an infection in the inner lining of the uterus (womb).”

Reasons for Diagnosis

  • On history:
    • You have fever.
    • You have bleeding.
  • On examination:
    • Uterus size is larger than expected 10 days after delivery.
    • When we touch your womb, it is painful to touch.
    • The os (opening of your womb) is still open:
      • This is not normal 10 days after delivery.
  • Putting all these together supports endometritis.

Differentials to Discuss with Patient

  1. Retained Products of Conception
    • “Some tissue and parts of the pregnancy were missed in the womb and are still inside.”
    • Includes:
      • Parts of the placenta.
      • Membranes.
      • Pieces of pregnancy tissue.
    • They were misplaced and left behind.
  2. Tears and Trauma
    • Any tears in:
      • The cervix (opening of the womb).
      • The vagina.
    • The episiotomy (cut made during delivery) might have started bleeding.
  3. Bleeding Disorders
    • A bleeding disorder could be causing prolonged bleeding.
  4. Non-pregnancy-related Causes (listed as number five in explanation)
    • Fibroids:
      • Some women have these, which cause higher risk of bleeding.
    • Cervical cancer.
    • Cervical polyp.
    • All these can cause bleeding.
  • Lecturer notes:
    • His key four differentials are:
      1. Endometritis (diagnosis).
      2. Retained products of conception.
      3. Tears/trauma (including episiotomy).
      4. Bleeding disorders.
    • Non-pregnancy-related causes (fibroid, cervical cancer, polyp) are also important but listed as fifth.

Management Plan (Explained to the Patient – Bullet Style)

Overall

  • “I need to admit you; you will be staying with us for a while.”
  • Management has two components:
    1. Investigate.
    2. Manage.
  • Investigations are part of the management.

1. Investigations

  • Blood tests:
    • Full blood examination.
    • Blood cultures – looking for infection.
    • Coagulation screening.
    • Blood group, and crossmatch if needed:
      • Depends on how bad the bleeding is.
  • Swabs:
    • High vaginal swabs “down below”.
    • Sent for microscopy, culture, and sensitivity (MCS).
  • Ultrasound:
    • “We are definitely going to do an ultrasound.”
    • Looking for tissue:
      • To see if any part of the pregnancy (placental tissue, membranes) is still inside.

2. Management (Treatment)

  • Because most probable cause is infection:
    • “We are going to start you on antibiotics.”
    • Specifically intravenous antibiotics.
    • Lecturer:
      • Says you don’t need to memorize names/doses for the exam.
      • If you want, you can memorize (e.g. ampicillin, metronidazole, gentamicin), but he personally wouldn’t.
  • Because she is still bleeding:
    • Will give IV fluids.
  • Because uterus is big and not going down:
    • May start massaging the uterus:
      • To push the uterus back down.
    • Later, may consider oxytocin.
  • If ultrasound shows retained products:
    • There is a possibility of surgical or manual removal.
    • “We may need to take you to the operation theatre (OT).”
    • Remove any components of the pregnancy and membranes that are still inside.

Closing Message of Case

  • Overall:
    • Most likely infection (endometritis).
    • But still a chance of retained pregnancy tissue.
    • If ultrasound confirms tissue, proceed with manual/surgical removal in OT.
  • Core exam aim:
    • Show that you recognize endometritis and are actively considering the full set of differentials for secondary postpartum hemorrhage.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ Secondary PPH is defined as bleeding from 24 hours 'up to 12 weeks after delivery (first 3 months postpartum)'.
The standard RANZCOG/RCOG definition of secondary PPH is abnormal/excessive bleeding from the genital tract from 24 hours to 6 weeks postpartum (some texts extend to 12 weeks). The 6-week endpoint should be documented as the conventional definition, as bleeding after this is investigated as gynaecological rather than obstetric.
✏️ The note deliberately avoids naming antibiotics for endometritis ('Do NOT memorize antibiotic names or doses. Stating I start antibiotics is sufficient').
For endometritis complicating secondary PPH, eTG recommends a documented regimen: amoxicillin (or ampicillin) PLUS metronidazole PLUS gentamicin IV (or amoxicillin-clavulanate) pending cultures. While the exam may not require doses, the record and safe practice should name the agents and check penicillin allergy.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Use Australian spelling (haemorrhage, oedema, caesarean, labour) and document estimated blood loss and haemodynamic status even though guidelines do not fix a 'significant' volume.
⭐ Add the named investigations the case lists into a clear order: FBE, group and hold/crossmatch, coagulation screen, beta-hCG (if no ultrasound and to exclude gestational trophoblastic disease), high vaginal + endocervical swabs, blood cultures if febrile, and pelvic ultrasound for retained products of conception.
⭐ Document a cause-directed management plan: uterotonics (oxytocin/ergometrine - checking BP for ergometrine) and tranexamic acid for ongoing bleeding, IV antibiotics for endometritis, and surgical evacuation (ERPC/EUA) for confirmed retained products, with senior obstetric involvement.
⭐ Record anti-D consideration for RhD-negative women (as the case notes it is lower priority) and postnatal-depression/psychosocial screening at the 10-day review - links RACGP postnatal care and eTG endometritis guidance.
