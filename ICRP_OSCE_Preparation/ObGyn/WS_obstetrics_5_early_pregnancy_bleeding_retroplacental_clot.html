Early Pregnancy Bleeding - Retroplacental (Subchorionic) Clot

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — obstetrics / class 3 (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: obstetrics_gynaecology

EARLY PREGNANCY BLEEDING – RETROPLACENTAL CLOT

Your next patient is a 27 year old lady who is 8 weeks pregnant and has presented to you çomplaining of vaginal bleeding.

Task:

• Take history for 6 minutes

• Investigation will appear on the screen at 6 minutes

• Explain your diagnosis and management plan

Key History Points Given by the Patient

  • Small amount of bleeding that has stopped
  • Used one pad
  • No passage of tissue
  • Some versions of the case: patient mentions alcohol, weed, etc.
    • Lecturer: “doesn’t make much of a sense… I don’t care about the marijuana… I don’t care about the alcohol.”
  • Blood group O positive
    • Lecturer: “doesn’t make much of a difference for you.”

Investigation Findings (Given on the Card)

  • Single intrauterine pregnancy
  • Fetal cardiac activity detected
  • Size correlates with gestational age
  • Retroplacental clot (added to “confuse the hell out of you”)

Discussion of Retroplacental Clot

  • Lecturer clarifies:
    • Not a placental abruption
    • A clot can appear in early pregnancy bleedings
    • Common after bleeding episodes
    • The question “good or bad?” → You don’t know, but
    • “Most of the time, you're fine with it”
  • Explanation to mother:
    • There is a small clot behind the placenta
    • Placenta described as:
      • “the tissue, or the bridge, that connects the baby or the fetus to the womb.”
    • Small and stable clots resolve by themselves
    • If the clot expands / increases in size, it can:
      • “danger the pregnancy”
      • “increase the risk of complications”

Diagnosis

  • Threatened miscarriage
    • Lecturer wording:
      • “Normal pregnancy after vaginal bleeding”
      • “It is a threatened miscarriage, a normal, viable, normal growing pregnancy.”
      • “Not really a miscarriage — just the name.”

Counselling Script (as emphasized in the lecture)

  • Tell the mother:
    • “I was very worried about miscarriage when you told me you have bleedings.”
    • “I've checked your investigations.”
    • “Based on this, I think you have a normal, viable, normal growing pregnancy.”
    • “I don’t think you've had a miscarriage — we call this a threatened miscarriage.”
  • Explain the clot:
    • “It is common to see a clot after a bleeding episode.”
    • “Small and stable clots resolve by itself.”
    • “If the clot expands, it can danger the pregnancy and increase the risk of complications.”

Management Plan

1. Serial / Regular Ultrasounds

  • “Regular or serial ultrasounds”
  • “Every week for now”
  • Purposes:
    • Check the clot
    • Check the pregnancy
    • Ensure pregnancy is continuing normally
    • Later: re-check fetal cardiac activity

2. Beta-hCG

  • To ensure it is rising normally
  • Repeat after two days
  • Purpose: confirm pregnancy is ongoing and hasn’t stopped

3. Advice Regarding the Clot

  • Rest for now
  • Avoid heavy exercise
    • May increase chance of clot getting bigger / expanding

4. Anti-D

  • “No need for anti-D” if the mother is positive
  • Even if negative, at this point “still don’t need it”
  • Because: “It’s a threatened miscarriage that you’ve had.”

5. Alcohol / Drugs

  • Advise:
    • Stop alcohol and marijuana
    • They “increase the risk of complications throughout pregnancy”

6. Full Blood Examination

  • “Yes and no”
  • Can be done “to make sure you haven’t lost excessive blood”

7. Progesterone

  • Not required
  • Retroplacental clot does not indicate progesterone

✏️ Corrections

✏️ In the first trimester the correct term for a clot adjacent to the gestational sac/placenta is a subchorionic haematoma. 'Retroplacental clot' is more properly a late-pregnancy abruption term; the note uses 'retroplacental clot' throughout, which can confuse candidates.
Label the finding a subchorionic (perigestational) haematoma at 8 weeks. The counselling that it is common after early bleeding and usually resolves is correct, but the terminology should be updated for accuracy.
✏️ The anti-D advice ('even if Rh-negative, still don't need it because it is a threatened miscarriage') is stated too absolutely.
Per National Blood Authority/RANZCOG: anti-D is not routinely required for a threatened miscarriage before 12 weeks, but is recommended if bleeding is heavy/recurrent or associated with pain, and for any first-trimester sensitising event give Rh(D) immunoglobulin 250 IU within 72 hours in Rh(D)-negative women. Document the blood group is O positive here (so anti-D not required) rather than implying anti-D is never needed for threatened miscarriage.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add safety-netting: advise presentation/000 for heavy bleeding, severe pain, dizziness or fever, and document written return advice - the current plan focuses on reassurance and rest.
⭐ Reframe 'rest and avoid heavy exercise' as reasonable pragmatic advice while noting there is limited high-quality evidence that activity restriction prevents miscarriage; avoid over-promising benefit.
⭐ Strengthen substance-use counselling: provide structured brief intervention and referral for alcohol and cannabis cessation (both have fetal risk) rather than the transcript's dismissive framing.
⭐ Add reference to RANZCOG early pregnancy guidance and eTG for threatened miscarriage/subchorionic haematoma as metadata; document folate/iodine continuation.
