Physical Examination for Heavy Menstrual Bleeding (PEFE)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — gynae / 1. period problems (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: obstetrics_gynaecology

Physical Examination From Examiner – Menorrhagia Case (PEFE)

Introduction

  • We want to build a structure to apply to all of our menorrhagia cases: the lady who's bleeding too much and for a long time.

1. General Appearance

  • Menorrhagia is a bleeding case → think about pallor.
  • Check if the patient is alert.
  • For cancers on the list → cachexia.
  • PCOS is still here → ask hirsutism and acne.
  • BMI skipped; cachexia more important here.
  • Do not stay long on general appearance; move on.

2. Vital Signs

  • Because the lady has been bleeding:
    • Blood pressure
    • Pulse rate
    • Temperature
  • Heavy periods → STIs and PID are still differentials → temperature important.
  • Respiratory rate optional, but examiner mentions doing all four together out of habit.

3. Thyroid Examination

  • Ask examiner for a thyroid examination → he will say it's normal.
  • Thyroid appears a lot because gynecological problems get tangled with thyroid diseases.

4. Pelvic Examination

(With the consent of patient and presence of a chaperone.)

A. Inspection

  • Bleeding (check if any visible)
  • Discharge (PID on differential list)
  • Rashes
  • Bruises
    • Because bleeding disorders, traumatic sexual activity, post-coital bleeding can relate
  • Swelling and lumps (optional; not very relevant to menorrhagia)

B. Speculum Examination

Two important areas:

  • Cervix
  • Vaginal wall

Cervix – Ask for:

  • Masses or abnormal growths (cervical cancer)
  • Polyps
  • Ectropion
  • IUD strings (if lady has IUD)
    • If strings absent → must find out where the IUD is

C. Bimanual Examination

  • Cervical motion tenderness
    • Positive in PID because of cervicitis
  • Uterus
    • Size
    • Border
    • Tenderness
    • Large uterus + menorrhagia → could be cancer, fibroid, or pregnancy
  • Adnexa
    • Masses
    • Tenderness

5. Urine Pregnancy Test

  • Routine testing in menorrhagia → No
  • Only if you have a very good reason
    • Example: uterus feels 10-week large → then you may do it to rule out pregnancy
  • Still a personal decision if someone insists on performing it

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: clinical-documentation-expert (Australian clinical supervisor, physical examination) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ American spelling 'gynecological' and general US usage.
Use Australian spelling: gynaecological, tumour, anaemia, oedema.
✏️ Consent/chaperone documentation reduced to '(with the consent of patient and presence of a chaperone)'.
Per AHPRA/RANZCOG, document the specific consent for the intimate examination, that a chaperone was offered (accepted/declined) and their name/role, and that the patient can stop at any time - not merely that a chaperone was present.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add sign-to-diagnosis mapping: a bulky, firm, irregular non-tender uterus suggests fibroids; a diffusely bulky tender ('boggy') uterus suggests adenomyosis; a fixed tender uterus with nodularity suggests endometriosis - the note lists 'size/border/tenderness' but does not link findings to diagnoses.
⭐ Add that pallor/tachycardia and postural blood pressure should trigger assessment for haemodynamic compromise and iron-deficiency anaemia (FBE + ferritin), connecting the general-appearance and vital-sign steps to investigations.
⭐ Add that absent IUD (Mirena) threads on speculum require locating the device (pelvic ultrasound, then AXR if not seen) and counselling that contraceptive protection is lost - the note flags 'find out where the IUD is' but omits the pathway.
⭐ Metadata: recommend adding an image-tag for the classic speculum photographs (ectropion, IUD threads, polyp, atrophic cervix) referenced in the paired history case, as these are common AMC visual prompts.
