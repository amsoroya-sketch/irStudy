Swelling in Late Pregnancy (Physiological vs Preeclampsia)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — obstetrics / class 6 (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: obstetrics_gynaecology

MATERNAL ADAPTATION TO PREGNANCY

Cardiovascular Changes

  • Plasma expands.
  • Increased RBC production.
  • Increased cardiac output because blood volume increases.
  • Heart rate increases.
  • Usually a drop in blood pressure.
    • Due to progesterone and vasodilation.
    • First trimester: blood pressure drops.
  • Dyspnea is common in pregnancy.
    • Because of progesterone.
    • Especially late into pregnancy.
    • Usually not too concerning, but always keep in mind possibility of pulmonary edema or other lung problems.

Physical Examination – Cardiovascular

  • Shift in apex beat – normal in pregnancy.
  • Louder heart sounds.
  • Might have splitting of S1.
  • Soft systolic murmurs over pulmonary and aortic area – acceptable.
  • Might have an S3 murmur (Gallop rhythm).
    • Does not necessarily mean heart failure.

Abnormal Findings

  • Any diastolic murmur is abnormal.
  • Loud holosystolic murmur is concerning.
  • Fourth heart sound is abnormal.
  • Exaggerated S2 / loud P2 is not normal (feature of pulmonary edema).

Previous Case References

  • Systolic murmur case → gastroesophageal reflux → not concerning.
  • Mid-diastolic murmur case (mitral stenosis) → not normal.

GI Changes

  • Change in taste perception (does not make much difference).
  • Increased incidence of gastroesophageal reflux.
    • Due to pressure and progesterone.
  • Liver: Alb decreases, CGT decreases, Increase in cholesterol and TG.
  • Pregnancy forms a good environment for gallstones.
    • Due to progesterone decreasing bowel motility and increasing risk of stones.
  • Constipation is very common (progesterone).
  • Bloating can happen.
  • Towards end of pregnancy:
    • Hemorrhoids common (big, heavy uterus).
    • Sometimes swelling in the vulva.

Edema

  • Normal in pregnancy, especially late stages.
  • Worse when standing; worse at end of the day.
  • Better in the morning after sleeping.
  • Concerning swelling:
    • Massive swelling present in the morning that does not change throughout the day. - preeclampsia

Clonus

  • Important in preeclampsia.
  • Neurological exam done due to concern for eclampsia and seizures (excitability).
  • Best place to check clonus: ankle.
  • Sustained clonus is abnormal.
    • More than three beats after sharp dorsiflexion.
  • Greater than three beats → concerning.
  • Less than three beats (one or two) → not concerning; does not mean excitability or preeclampsia.

Musculoskeletal Changes

  • Range of normal problems:
    • Back pain.
    • Pelvic pain / symphysis pubis.
    • Chest wall pain (late pregnancy).
    • Sore lower ribs (pressure of uterus).
    • Hip pain.
    • Thigh pain (meralgia paresthetica).
    • Knee pain.
    • Leg cramps.
    • Foot pain.
  • Pregnancy is a risk factor for carpal tunnel syndrome.
    • Many pregnant ladies end up having carpal tunnel syndrome.

SWELLING IN PREGNANCY

Your next patient is a 37 year old lady who is 36 weeks pregnant and has attended her antenatal visit today. She is complaining of swelling in the legs.

Task:

‪• Take history for 6 minutes

• Physical examination will be given to you on a card

• Explain your diagnosis to the patient

BEFORE STARTING THE CASE

Two things to clarify

  1. This is NOT preeclampsia.
    • Normal blood pressure
    • Clonus of 1 beat does not mean preeclampsia
    • Massive swelling, vulval swelling, hemorrhoids, varicose veins can be normal
    • Very low chance of preeclampsia even without urine protein
  2. This is NOT pelvic congestion syndrome.
    • Pelvic congestion syndrome = chronic pelvic discomfort, worse on standing, worse on coitus, 6 months pain, dyspareunia, dysuria
    • Not relevant to this case

HISTORY FINDINGS GIVEN

  • Swelling in both legs
  • Up to thigh
  • Painful
  • No Pulmonary embolism feature or clotting RF
  • Grumpy and wants pregnancy to be over
  • 3rd pregnancy

PHYSICAL EXAM FINDINGS GIVEN

  • BP 115/80
  • Swelling of the vulva
  • Hemorrhoids
  • Swelling of the legs
  • Varicose veins in the legs
  • Clonus: 1+
  • No urine dipstick provided

DIAGNOSIS

  • Normal physiological changes in late pregnancy
  • Case is designed to trick you into preeclampsia — but it is not

HISTORY TAKING (6 MINUTES)

1. INTRO AND COMMUNICATION

  • Patient described as grumpy, frustrated
  • Starts with: “I want the pregnancy to stop… I can’t take this anymore.”
  • Communication skill statements:
    • “I’m so sorry to hear that, but I’m here to help you.”
    • “You can open up and discuss your concerns with me.”
    • “You are a strong mother.”
    • “This phase will be over soon.”
    • “Your body is working hard to take care of your baby.”
  • Spend 30–40 seconds calming and acknowledging her

2. EXPLORE THE SWELLING

  • When did it start?
  • On and off or constant?
  • Is it getting worse?
  • One side or both sides?
  • Up to which level?
  • Morning swelling? (Red flag)
  • Anything that makes it better or worse?
  • Is it worse on standing and better on lying down? (Red flag)

3. OTHER SWELLING

  • Any other swelling in your body?
    • Vulval swelling (patient reports yes)
  • Any swelling around your eyes?

4. ASSOCIATED SYMPTOMS

  • Any rashes on your leg?
  • Is the swelling painful?
  • Is it itchy?

DIFFERENTIALS

Differential 1: PREECLAMPSIA

  • Must rule out
  • Ask:
    • Abdominal pain?
    • Nausea/vomiting?
    • Neurological: headache, blurring of vision, dizziness?

Differential 2: DVT / PULMONARY EMBOLISM

  • Pain in legs
  • Risk factors:
    • Long travel?
    • Recent surgeries?
    • Family history of clotting?
    • Personal history of clotting?
  • Pulmonary embolism:
    • Cough?
    • Shortness of breath?
  • Optional: “Have you noticed any dilated blood tubes on your legs?”

Differential 3: KIDNEY DISEASE

  • Oliguria:
    • “Have you noticed a decrease in the amount of urine you pass?”
  • Previous kidney disease?

Differential 4: HEART FAILURE

  • Chest pain
  • Shortness of breath (especially on exercise)
  • Shortness of breath on lying down (can still ask)

Differential 5: LIVER FAILURE

  • Yellowish discoloration of skin
  • Itchiness
  • Dark urine
  • Pale stools

Optional

  • Hypothyroidism: weather preference
  • Fever (infection)

OB HISTORY (towards the end if time allows)

  • Early pregnancy bloods normal?
  • Dating scan?
  • Down syndrome screening?
  • 18–20 week ultrasound (baby and placenta)?
  • Glucose/sweet test at 26 weeks?
  • Swab/bug test at 36 weeks (if appropriate)?
  • Is your baby kicking normally?
  • Previous pregnancies and complications

CASE SUMMARY FROM RECALL

  • Swelling both legs up to thigh
  • Painful or not (varied recalls)
  • No clotting risk factors
  • No pulmonary embolism features
  • Third pregnancy, no past complications

FINAL DIAGNOSIS

Normal physiological changes of late pregnancy

EXPLANATION TO PATIENT

  • “The changes you are experiencing are normal in late pregnancy.”
  • The womb and baby become very large and heavy
  • This puts pressure on the blood tubes at the back of the stomach
  • This can cause:
    • Swelling in the legs
    • Swelling in the private parts
    • Swelling around the back passage
    • Dilated blood tubes on the legs (varicose veins)

REASSURANCE AND DIFFERENTIALS (EXPLAINED TO PATIENT)

  • “I was thinking about a serious condition called preeclampsia, but you don’t have features of that.”
  • “I found a slight extra movement called clonus, but one beat is normal.”
  • Other things considered:
    • Kidney failure
    • Heart failure
    • Liver failure (like acute fatty liver of pregnancy)
    • Thyroid problems
    • Clotting in the legs, which can extend to the lungs
    • Infections like cellulitis or septic arthritis

FINAL MESSAGE

  • Case designed to distract you
  • Wants you to think preeclampsia
  • Do not fall for it
  • Stick to normal physiological changes of pregnancy

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The case concludes preeclampsia is very unlikely largely on the basis of a normal BP and 1-beat clonus, and notes 'No urine dipstick provided' without flagging this as a gap.
Per SOMANZ/RANZCOG, preeclampsia is diagnosed as new hypertension (BP >=140/90 mmHg) after 20 weeks PLUS evidence of maternal organ or uteroplacental dysfunction; proteinuria is NO LONGER mandatory. Urinalysis for protein remains a required observation at every antenatal visit and BP plus urinalysis should both be documented before excluding preeclampsia. The note should record that a dipstick was requested/performed rather than accept its absence.
✏️ 'Exaggerated S2 / loud P2 is not normal (feature of pulmonary edema)' attributes a loud P2 to pulmonary oedema.
A loud/palpable P2 is a sign of pulmonary hypertension, not pulmonary oedema itself. In late pregnancy new orthopnoea, PND, bibasal crackles and desaturation are the clinical markers of pulmonary oedema; loud P2 should be documented as a possible sign of pulmonary hypertension and warrants echocardiography.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Use Australian spelling throughout the record (oedema not edema, haemorrhoids not hemorrhoids, oesophageal reflux, caesarean) to meet NSW Health/ACSQHC documentation standards.
⭐ Add a red-flag that bilateral leg swelling in pregnancy still requires DVT to be considered when asymmetric, painful or calf-localised; document Wells-type risk factors and note that pregnancy is itself a hypercoagulable state (RANZCOG VTE guidance).
⭐ Document objective observations for the physiological-oedema conclusion: BP, urinalysis result, reflexes/clonus beats, and absence of facial/periorbital or sudden-onset oedema (the discriminators from preeclampsia).
⭐ Reference the RACGP/RANZCOG routine antenatal schedule (weight, BP, fundal height, urinalysis, fetal heart and movements each visit) to frame why this presentation is a normal 36-week review finding.
