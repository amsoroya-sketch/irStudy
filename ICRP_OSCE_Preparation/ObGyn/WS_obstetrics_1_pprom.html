Preterm Prelabour Rupture of Membranes (PPROM)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — obstetrics / class 8 (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: obstetrics_gynaecology

PPROM

PREMATURE RUPTURE OF MEMBRANES (PPROM)

Introduction

  • Two major concerns in early rupture of membranes:
    1. Infections
    2. Is it early to deliver the baby? (lung maturity)

Key Points of Management (Summary Before Topic)

  • Rule out infection.
  • If needing to keep the baby inside until lung maturity:
    • Prophylactic antibiotics (prevent infection).
    • Corticosteroids if very early (help lung maturity).

Definition

  • Rupture of membranes before 37 completed weeks = preterm premature rupture of membranes.

Risks

  • Preterm labor
  • Cord collapse
  • Placental abruption
  • Pulmonary/breathing problems
  • Perinatal mortality

When This Topic Applies

  • Any rupture of membranes before 37 weeks gestation.

Initial Steps

  • Take history.
  • Examine the lady to confirm diagnosis.
  • Confirm gestation.
  • Confirm fetal presentation.
  • Assess maternal and more importantly fetal well-being:
    • CTG to ensure no fetal distress.

GBS Consideration

  • Earlier rupture means earlier delivery.
  • Routine 36-week swab not yet done.
  • GBS in birth canal can cause sepsis.
  • Consider high vaginal swab for infections including GBS.

Speculum Examination

  • This is the only case where sterile speculum is emphasized.
  • Concern: infection risk.
  • Purpose: confirm amniotic fluid.
    • Test: Nitrazine test
    • Brand example: Amnisure
  • Digital vaginal examination:
    • Avoid unless needed.
    • Not contraindicated.
    • Do it only if there is a reason (e.g., assessing Bishop score before delivery).
    • Concern: increases infection rate.

Physical Examination Points

  • Routine pelvic exam.
  • Focus on speculum exam.
  • Avoid bimanual/digital exam unless necessary.
  • In a PFE:
    • Mention taking swabs during speculum exam:
      • Low vaginal
      • High vaginal
      • Perianal
      • Especially for GBS and infections.

CTG Categories

  • Two categories:
    1. No concerns / no risk factors
    2. Risk factors present
      • Non-reassuring CTG → deliver now
      • Features of chorioamnionitis → deliver
      • Meconium-stained liquor → deliver

Risk Factors → Active Management

  • If any risk factors:
    • Prefer active management (prepare for labor).

No Risk Factors → Options

  • Offer expectant or active management.
  • Benefits of waiting: lung maturity.
  • Risk of waiting: infection.

Expectant Management (No Risk Factors)

  • Can discharge home.
  • Give instructions:
    • Check temperature every few hours.
    • Observe fluid (ensure not pus-like).
    • Monitor fetal movements.
    • Check for contractions.
  • Red flags: return if
    • Contractions intensify
    • Color change in liquor
    • Foul smell
    • Fever
    • Reduced fetal movements
  • Alternatively: admit to antenatal ward if necessary.

Gestational Age Considerations

  • 30 weeks: consider how long to keep in hospital.
  • Corticosteroids
    • Usual number: 35 weeks
    • Under 35 weeks → corticosteroids recommended
    • Beyond 35 weeks → a bit late to give
  • Ruptured membrane cases:
    • <35 weeks: key point to give corticosteroids

Investigations and Interventions

  • High/low vaginal swabs
  • CTG
  • Fetal heart rate monitoring
  • Antibiotic prophylaxis
  • Rule out infection:
    • Full blood count
    • ESR
    • CRP
    • Swabs
  • Ultrasound

Cutoff for Waiting vs Delivery

  • Under 34 weeks:
    • Wait
    • Give corticosteroids
    • Give antibiotics
  • After 34 weeks:
    • Lung likely mature → weigh risks and benefits
  • Guideline number:
    • 36 weeks = cutoff
    • ≥36 weeks → go for labor

Indications for Active Management

  • Preterm labor (contractions)
  • Evidence of chorioamnionitis/infection
  • Beyond 36 weeks

Tocolytics

  • If very early (e.g., 30–32 weeks)
  • Used only if contractions present
  • Purpose: delay labor until corticosteroids work (about 2 days)

Expectant Management Limit

  • Up to 36 completed weeks.
  • After that → start labor.

Discharge Considerations

  • Sending home requires:
    • Single pregnancy
    • Cephalic presentation
    • Easy access to hospital
  • Rural setting may change decision.

Neonatal Considerations

  • Preterm birth (<35–36 weeks)
  • May need ICU
  • May need neonatologist involvement

Wrap-Up

  • Limited evidence on exact cutoff date.
  • Follow assessment pathway.
  • Focus on:
    • Sterile speculum
    • Cord prolapse check
    • Pooling of liquor
    • Swabs + nitrazine test
    • Ultrasound + CTG
    • Antibiotic prophylaxis
    • Corticosteroids if early
    • Tocolytics only if needed

CASE: Your nxt pt is 28 yrs old lady, 35 weeks pregnant who presents to u worried about her pregnancy.

TASK:

HISTORY TAKING STRUCTURE

Intro Phase

  • Good open-ended question:
    • “Can you tell me why you're concerned about your pregnancy or how can I help you?”
  • Patient response: leaking fluid, worried
  • Empathic reassurance: “I understand your concern… let me ask you a few questions… I'll take good care of you.”

MAIN COMPLAINT: LEAKING FLUID

Explore the Complaint

Timing

    • When did it start?
    • Is it ongoing or on and off? Continuous or constant?
    • Is it getting worse?
    • First time happening?

Characteristics (CCVO)

  • Color (imp)
    • Ask because you're looking for clear liquid
  • Content
    • Any blood in liquid?
    • Any tissues or clots?
  • Volume
    • How many pads used? Fully soaked?
  • Odor (imp)
    • Does it have a bad smell?
  • Importance:
    • Infection / meconium
    • Foul smell → concern for chorioamnionitis

DIFFERENTIALS

  • Essentially premature rupture of membranes

KEY QUESTIONS (PROM Symptoms & Concerns)

Symptoms

  1. Do u have any Abdominal pain?
    • Looking for contractions
    • If contractions: characterize (location, duration increasing, intervals decreasing)
  2. Any Vaginal bleeding?
  3. Fever or chills?
    • Rule out infection

Risk Factors of Rupture of Membranes

  1. Any Trauma or injuries to abdomen?
  2. Did it happen After sex/intercourse? (triggering event)
  3. Previous history of:
    • Early rupture of membranes
    • Cervix problems

Urine Question (Optional)

  • Any problems with urination?
  • Any past history of leaking urine?

OBSTETRIC HISTORY

  • Regular bloods at beginning of pregnancy – normal?
  • Dating scan?
  • Down syndrome screening?
  • Morphology scan 18–20 weeks – normal?
  • Sweet drink test 26–28 weeks
  • Swab test (usually 36 weeks but still ask)
  • Is the Baby kicking?
  • Preeclampsia questions
    • Headache, blurring vision, dizziness, swelling legs, nausea, vomiting
  • Previous pregnancies:
    • Complications?
    • High BP? Blood sugar?
    • PROM before?
    • Issues with delivery?

PHYSICAL EXAMINATION (FROM EXAMINER)

General Appearance

  • Is my patient in Pain from contractions?

  • Dehydration (not very relevant)

Vital Signs

  • Temperature most important (rule out infection)

Abdomen

  • Ask examiner: Leopold’s maneuver
    • Lie and position
    • Fundal height
    • Fetal heart rate (key points)
  • Look/feel for contractions (better than tenderness)

Pelvic Examination

  • With consent + chaperone
  • Inspection
    • Any bleeding or discharge?
    • Skin findings, rashes, ulcers, redness, lumps, swelling

Sterile Speculum Examination (Key Point)

  • Look at os:is my os open or closed?
  • Is there any Pooling of fluid / fluid collection in posterior fornix? > “ yes u have”
  • If pooling seen:
    • Examiner I want to Take sample for:
      • Nitrazine test
      • Low and high vaginal swab
  • Avoid digital/bimanual examination

Note: No need for vaginal/bimanual exam

DIAGNOSIS EXPLANATION

  • Most likely: Preterm pre-labor / premature rupture of membranes

    1. Which Means “you broke your water early before 37 weeks”
  • Reasons
    1. Characteristics of fluid: clear, non-smelly, etc.
    2. Examination:I found fluid collection in vagina, sample taken to confirm

Other Things to Differentiate

  • Preterm labor (labor has started)
  • Chorioamnionitis (infection)

MANAGEMENT

Risks of Early Rupture

  1. “Increased risk of infection in the fluid around baby (chorioamnionitis)
  2. Increased risk of breathing problems when baby is born
  3. Can lead to early labor
  4. Can lead to cord prolapse (optional to ask on speculum)”

INVESTIGATIONS

Categories

  1. Rule out infection
    • Full blood examination / full blood count
    • ESR, CRP
    • High and low vaginal swabs
  2. Ultrasound
    • Assess amniotic fluid
    • Baby’s position

  3. Baby’s well-being
    • CTG

Must Rule Out: I need to rule out:

  1. Infection
  2. Labor / contractions
  3. Baby distress

We have TWO METHODS OF MANAGEMENT:

  1. Expectant Management
    • We will Wait until 36 completed weeks
    • Wait for lung maturation
  2. Active Management
    • Proceed to induce labor / start labor

IMMEDIATE STEPS

  1. Prophylactic antibiotics> I will five u preventive antibiotics to Prevent infection
  2. Corticosteroids
    • If under 35 weeks
    • Speeds lung maturity
    • Decreases breathing problems
  3. Decide: Hospital or Home
    • Patient-centered
    • We have Two options:
      • U can choose to Stay in hospital
      • We can Send home once we r sure u r stable (no infection, no contractions)

MONITORING

  • Close monitoring:
    • Temperature
    • Regular bloods
    • Baby checks / CTG
    • Repeat ultrasound

Goal

  • Wait until 36 completed weeks
  • Then induce labor

RED FLAGS & INSTRUCTIONS

  • Avoid:
    • Intercourse and sex
    • Tampons
    • Swimming / going into water
  • Monitor:
    • Temperature (every few hours if at home)
    • Fluid/discharge smell and color
    • Baby kicking
    • Contractions / abdominal pain
  • If any present → come back to hospital

SUMMARY TO PATIENT

  • PROM increases:
    • Infection (chorioamnionitis)
    • Labor can start anytime
    • Early birth → breathing problems
    • Risk of cord prolapse
  • Investigations: bloods, swabs, ultrasound, CTG
  • If no infection, no distress, no contractions:
    • Expectant until 36 completed weeks OR
    • Induce labor now
  • Preventive antibiotics
  • Corticosteroids if under 35 weeks
  • Hospital or home: patient decides
  • Close monitoring in either case
  • Avoid infection risks
  • Watch red flags

SPECIAL CASE: CERVICAL CERCLAGE

  • Physical exam may show cervical sutures/circlage
  • Management question: remove or leave?

No Good Answer

  • Removing:
    • Increases risk of labor
  • Leaving:
    • Increases risk of infection

Gestational Age Important

  • 30-week case:
    • Most likely leave in place
    • Give corticosteroids
    • Maybe consider removing after 48 hours
  • Optidate suggestions:
    • Remove if:
      • Infection
      • Vaginal bleeding
      • After 32 weeks
    • Before 32 weeks:
      • If no infection/bleeding/preterm labor → leave in place

35-week case

  • Lung maturity no longer major concern
  • Likely specialist will remove sooner or later
  • Discuss with obstetrician

30-week case discussion

  • Risks with removing and leaving
  • Concerned about removing now
  • Give corticosteroids 48 hours
  • Then discuss removal with obstetrician

Antibiotics

  • Given to all cases to prevent infection
  • If fever (chorioamnionitis) → treat infection (not prophylaxis)

Tocolytics

  • Need contractions for tocolytics
  • 30-week + contractions:
    • Tocolytics for 48 hours while giving corticosteroids
  • How long to continue?
    • Depends on clinical context and patient preferences
    • Sooner or later must stop

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The notes state prophylactic antibiotics generically without naming the agent, and imply broad-spectrum cover. Amoxicillin-clavulanate is specifically avoided in PPROM.
Per RANZCOG/RCOG PPROM guidance, latency antibiotic of choice is oral erythromycin 250 mg four times daily for 10 days (or until labour). Co-amoxiclav (amoxicillin-clavulanate) must be AVOIDED because of the increased risk of neonatal necrotising enterocolitis. Separately, intrapartum GBS prophylaxis (benzylpenicillin, or clindamycin/lincomycin if penicillin-allergic) is given once in labour.
✏️ The notes use inconsistent corticosteroid cut-offs ('usual number 35 weeks', 'under 35 weeks give corticosteroids') and apply this to a 35-week patient.
A single course of antenatal corticosteroids (betamethasone 11.4 mg IM, two doses 24 hours apart) is routinely recommended for threatened preterm birth up to 34+6 weeks. Between 35+0 and 36+6 weeks it is only selectively considered (e.g. before planned caesarean). For this 35-week patient routine steroids are NOT clearly indicated — this should be stated explicitly rather than defaulting to give.
✏️ Neuroprotection is not mentioned.
For anticipated very preterm birth <30 weeks (RANZCOG allows up to 34 weeks in some protocols), magnesium sulfate should be offered for fetal neuroprotection. This is a standard Australian omission worth flagging even though this case is 35 weeks.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Name the confirmatory test more precisely: sterile speculum visualisation of pooling in the posterior fornix is first-line; if equivocal use a PAMG-1 (Amnisure) or IGFBP-1 (Actim PROM) immunoassay. Nitrazine/ferning have high false-positive rates and should not be relied on alone.
⭐ Add an explicit red-flag / escalation list for the History and Diagnosis explanation: fever/tachycardia, uterine tenderness, offensive or purulent liquor (chorioamnionitis), fresh vaginal bleeding (abruption), reduced fetal movements, cord felt at speculum (cord prolapse - call 000/obstetric emergency).
⭐ Add anti-D consideration: if the woman is Rh D negative with any antepartum bleeding, give anti-D immunoglobulin and perform a Kleihauer.
⭐ Cite the governing Australian source (RANZCOG 'Term Prelabour Rupture of Membranes' and preterm birth statements) in the teaching notes so candidates map to Australian rather than generic practice.
⭐ Add safe-prescribing formatting to the medication lines (spell out 'units', avoid trailing zeros) consistent with ACSQHC standards, since these notes double as an EMR-documentation exemplar.
