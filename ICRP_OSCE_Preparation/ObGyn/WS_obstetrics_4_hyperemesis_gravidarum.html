Hyperemesis Gravidarum - History, Examination and Management

Differential Diagnosis

Nausea and vomiting in early pregnancy is common, but the excessive/persistent pattern requires excluding other causes before diagnosing hyperemesis gravidarum (a diagnosis of exclusion):

  • Obstetric: hyperemesis gravidarum; molar (gestational trophoblastic) pregnancy and multiple pregnancy (both raise hCG and worsen vomiting).
  • Gastrointestinal / acute abdomen: appendicitis, gastroenteritis, bowel obstruction.
  • Urinary: urinary tract infection / pyelonephritis (dysuria, frequency, fever, loin pain).
  • Neurological / infective: meningitis (headache, fever, neck stiffness, rash).
  • Endocrine: hyperthyroidism (heat intolerance, tremor, palpitations, weight loss); diabetic ketoacidosis.

Focused History

  • Vomiting characterisation: onset and duration, frequency, volume, content (bile, blood/haematemesis), relation to food, ability to keep down fluids.
  • Dehydration symptoms: dizziness/light-headedness, reduced urine output, weight loss.
  • Pregnancy details: last menstrual period and gestation, whether the pregnancy is confirmed/planned.
  • Screen the differentials: abdominal pain (appendicitis), diarrhoea/constipation (gastroenteritis), dysuria/frequency/fever (UTI), headache/fever/rash/neck stiffness (meningitis), heat intolerance/tremor/palpitations (thyroid).
  • Past medical history, medications, allergies and psychosocial impact.

Examination and Investigations

Examination: assess vital signs and hydration status (tachycardia, postural hypotension, dry mucous membranes, reduced skin turgor). Abdominal examination for distension, tenderness, bowel sounds; the notes flag caution around, and consideration of, pelvic assessment for other causes (e.g. cervical motion tenderness/uterine tenderness) only if clinically indicated.

Bedside/initial investigations:

  • Urine dipstick: ketones (ketonuria supports significant hyperemesis) and leucocytes/nitrites (UTI).
  • Full blood examination, urea/electrolytes/creatinine (electrolyte disturbance, e.g. hypokalaemia), thyroid function tests.
  • Ultrasound to confirm viable intrauterine pregnancy and exclude molar or multiple pregnancy.

Diagnosis and Management

Diagnosis: hyperemesis gravidarum - severe nausea and vomiting of pregnancy with dehydration, ketonuria and weight loss, once other causes are excluded.

  • Admit if unable to tolerate oral fluids, significant dehydration, ketonuria or electrolyte disturbance.
  • Intravenous rehydration with an appropriate crystalloid; correct electrolyte abnormalities (particularly potassium).
  • Antiemetics in a stepwise fashion (see correction for the recommended Australian ladder); the source notes reference ondansetron among others.
  • Thiamine supplementation to prevent Wernicke encephalopathy, especially with prolonged vomiting.
  • Reassess, monitor fluid balance and ketones, and provide reassurance and follow-up.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ Antiemetic therapy is referenced (e.g. ondansetron) without a stepwise, pregnancy-appropriate order.
Per eTG/RANZCOG, first-line antiemetics in pregnancy are pyridoxine (vitamin B6) with or without doxylamine, then dopamine antagonists (metoclopramide, prochlorperazine) or antihistamines (promethazine). Ondansetron is generally reserved as second-line, with documented counselling about a small possible increase in cleft palate risk if used in the first trimester. The plan should follow this ladder rather than defaulting to ondansetron.
✏️ IV fluids and thiamine are mentioned but the sequencing/glucose caveat is not specified.
Rehydrate with normal saline or Hartmann's; AVOID dextrose-containing fluids as the sole/initial resuscitation fluid and give THIAMINE before any glucose load, to prevent precipitating Wernicke encephalopathy. Monitor and replace potassium.
