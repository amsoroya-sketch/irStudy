Abdominal Pain in Pregnancy (>20 Weeks)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — obstetrics / class 5 (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: obstetrics_gynaecology

PREGNANCY ABDOMINAL PAIN

Your next patient is a 24 year old lady who is X (>20 ) weeks pregnant and has presented to your general practice complaining of worsening abdominal pain.

Task:

Take history from the patient for 6 minutes

PEFE/Physical examination will be given to you on a card

Explain your diagnosis and differentials

1. DIFFERENTIALS

(First step in approach)

Clusters of Differentials

A. OB Differentials

  • “Three Ps” to rule out
  • Preterm labor
  • Placental abruption
  • Preeclampsia
  • Optional additions:
    • Uterine rupture (prior C-section)
    • Chorioamnionitis
    • PID (rare)

B. GI Differentials

  • Acute abdomen Appendicitis
  • Bowel obstruction
  • Hernias
  • Constipation (due to progesterone in pregnancy)
  • Upper GI
    • Peptic ulcer disease
    • Gastroesophageal reflux

C. Liver & Gallbladder

  • Acute fatty liver of pregnancy
  • Cholecystitis

D. KUB Differentials

  • Pyelonephritis
  • Cystitis / UTIs
  • Renal colic

2. HISTORY TAKING STRUCTURE

A. Vitals

  • Ask examiner: BP, pulse, RR, temperature

B. Opening

  • Sit down, open-ended question.
  • Example: “How can I help you today?”
  • Address concern: supportive statement.

C. Explore Pain – SIQORAA

  1. Site (RUQ → liver; RLQ → appendicitis; LUQ → GERD/PUD)
  2. Intensity (1–10 scale)
  3. Quality (dull ache, burning, etc.)
  4. Onset
    • When did it start?
    • Constant or on and off? (Key question)
    • Is it getting worse?
    • Key question:
      • If on and off: Are the duration increasing and intervals decreasing? → contractions (preterm labor).
      • Constant pain → suggests abruption.
  5. Radiation
  6. Aggravating/Relieving
    • Relation to eating? Worse after eating? → PUD/cholecystitis.

D. GI Screening (Acute Abdomen Structure)

  • Nausea/vomiting?
  • Diarrhea/constipation?
  • Bloating?
  • Passing wind?
  • Fever

E. OB DIFFERENTIALS SECTION

1. Symptoms (imp)

  • Any bleeding
  • Any vaginal discharge / fluid leakage

2. Key Question

  • Is the baby kicking as usual?

3. Preeclampsia Questions

  • Headache
  • Vision problems
  • Dizziness
  • Yellowish discoloration of skin
  • Swelling in legs
  • Chest pain / shortness of breath
  • Decrease in urine output

F. GI DIFFERENTIALS SECTION

  • Loss of appetite? (Anorexia (key question for appendicitis))
  • Hernia question: lump in groin?
  • Pain worse after eating fatty meals? (cholecystitis)
  • Medication use? (Peptic ulcer disease, e.g., aspirin → gastritis)
  • Optional:
    • Acidic taste / bitter taste for reflux?

G. Liver Questions

  • Already covered within preeclampsia + eating-related questions.

H. KUB DIFFERENTIALS SECTION

  • Pain on passing urine?
  • Are you passing urine more frequently?
  • Blood in urine?

I. Routine OB History

  • Routine bloods at start of pregnancy
  • Dating scan
  • Down syndrome screening
  • 18–20 week ultrasound
  • Sweet drink test
  • Swab/“bug test”
  • Previous pregnancies and complications

3. PFE STRUCTURE (Physical Examination)

A. General Appearance & Vitals

  • Level of consciousness
  • Jaundice
  • Rashes (HELLP syndrome)
  • BP significant
    • If high → think preeclampsia
    • If normal/low → ask for postural drop
  • Temperature

B. Abdominal Examination

1. Inspection

  • Cullen sign
  • Grey Turner sign
  • Distension & respiratory movement less helpful in 36-week abdomen

2. Palpation

  • Any tenderness? (RUQ, LUQ, etc.)
Specific to Uterus
  • Fundal height?
  • Fetal heart rate?
  • Uterine tenderness? (abruption)
  • Contractions?
  • (Low-palpable maneuvers later—less priority vs key points)

3. Auscultation

  • Bowel sounds – present/absent

4. Other Exams

  • Inguinal area exam
  • Digital rectle exam (yes—because abdominal pain)

C. Pelvic Examination

  • With consent + chaperone
  • Inspection for bleeding/discharge
  • Speculum exam – KEY POINT: Is the OS OPEN OR CLOSED?
    • Fluid/discharge/blood from os optional
    • Main question = open or closed

D. If BP High → Preeclampsia Key Points

  • Fundoscopy
  • Reflexes
  • Clonus
  • Urine dipstick (protein)

4. CASE EXAMPLE

History Finding

  • 34 weeks pregnant
  • RUQ pain
  • Nausea
  • No jaundice
  • No itchiness
  • Headaches
  • No blurring of vision but floaters/flashing lights
  • Leg swelling

PE Findings

  • BP 150/90
  • RUQ tenderness
  • Fundoscope not done
  • Fetal heart & fundal height and lie normal
  • Urine dipstick: 3+ protein

Diagnosis

  • Preeclampsia
    • Not HELP syndrome (would require raised LFTs, low platelets, hemolysis)
  • Explanation to patient as per lecture:
    • You have a condition called preeclampsia
    • Condition in pregnancy with high BP, kidney protein leak, seizure risk
    • Can cause right upper quadrant/liver pain
    • Liver swelling/pain possible; rupture is a concern

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: clinical-documentation-expert (Australian teaching hospital clinical supervisor) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The worked example concludes 'Preeclampsia, not HELP syndrome' on clinical grounds when no bloods were taken (only a urine dipstick), and misspells the syndrome as 'HELP'.
HELLP syndrome (Haemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelets) cannot be excluded clinically. Right-upper-quadrant pain with pre-eclampsia mandates urgent FBC with film, LFTs, LDH, haptoglobin, serum urate and coagulation studies before HELLP can be excluded. Correct the abbreviation to HELLP throughout.
✏️ The examination plan lists a routine digital rectal examination ('yes - because abdominal pain') and digital pelvic assessment without addressing placenta praevia.
Routine digital rectal examination is not indicated for undifferentiated abdominal pain in pregnancy. More importantly, in any woman with vaginal bleeding/antepartum haemorrhage, digital vaginal examination must be AVOIDED until placenta praevia is excluded on ultrasound (risk of catastrophic haemorrhage). A speculum examination is acceptable, but this caveat should be explicit.
✏️ American spellings/terms throughout ('labor', 'hemolysis', 'diarrhea', 'gastroesophageal', 'discoloration', 'tumor').
Australian documentation requires labour, haemolysis, diarrhoea, gastro-oesophageal, discolouration, tumour. Standardise for AHPRA-compliant notes.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add an explicit investigations block for the worked example (currently only a dipstick is done): FBC with film, LFTs, LDH, haptoglobin, UEC, serum urate, coagulation profile, group and hold, MSU, CTG and ultrasound (placental location, fetal growth, liquor).
⭐ Strengthen the placental abruption safety-net: constant pain with a hard/tender uterus and/or bleeding (which may be concealed) is an obstetric emergency - 000 / urgent obstetric review, large-bore IV access, group and hold, continuous CTG.
⭐ Emphasise that appendicitis presents atypically in later pregnancy (the gravid uterus displaces the appendix upward, so pain may be peri-umbilical or RUQ) - RUQ pain is therefore not automatically pre-eclampsia/liver, and appendicitis and cholecystitis must stay live.
⭐ Add urinary tract infection / pyelonephritis as a common and important cause of abdominal pain and a trigger for preterm labour - send an MSU and treat per eTG; note routine antenatal screening for asymptomatic bacteriuria.
⭐ Add a preterm labour pathway: antenatal corticosteroids if birth anticipated before ~34-35 weeks, intrapartum GBS prophylaxis considerations, and analgesia advice (paracetamol first-line; avoid NSAIDs in the third trimester).
