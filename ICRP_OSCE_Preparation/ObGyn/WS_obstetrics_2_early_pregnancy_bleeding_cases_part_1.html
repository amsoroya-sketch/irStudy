Early Pregnancy Bleeding Cases - Part 1 (Incomplete, Ectopic, Threatened)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — obstetrics / class 3 (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: obstetrics_gynaecology

EARLY PREGNANCY BLEEDING CASES – PART 1

Your next patient is a 25 year old lady who has presented to your General Practice complaining of vaginal bleeding. She has missed her period and the LMP is 6/8 weeks ago. Home pregnancy test has been done and is positive.

Task:

• Take history from the patient for 4 minutes

• Ask physical examination from the examiner, you will be given findings only if you ask specific questions

• Explain your diagnosis and differentials to the patient

NOTE: If unstable vitals appear:

    • Mention: “I would like to transfer this patient to the treatment room(GP) or the resuscitation cubicle.(ED)”
    • Do NOT start management steps (IV lines, fluids, blood tests). Examiner will say “stick to your task.”
    • Task does not include management.

CASE 1

History

  • LMP: 6 weeks ago
  • Bleeding started today; ongoing
  • Pregnancy symptoms: tired, nausea (no vomiting)
  • Home pregnancy test: positive
  • Passed tissue? Patient unsure

Physical Examination

  • General/vitals/abdomen/pelvic examination
  • Vitals unstable: BP 90/60, pulse 110, RR 12, temp 36.5
  • Examiner allows you to say you will transfer patient to treatment room/resus cubicle, but no further management
  • Abdominal tenderness (suprapubic)
  • Pelvic exam:
    • Os open
    • Products of conception visible at os
    • Key point: Say: “I want to remove the tissue now.”
    • Examiner: ok you removed the tissue
  • Uterus size: 5 weeks, regular, tender: yes/no
  • No adnexal tenderness or masses

Diagnostic Formulation

  • Diagnosis: Incomplete miscarriage
  • Inevitable miscarriage possible when os is open, but in this case tissue is already visible → prefer incomplete miscarriage
  • Not “cervical shock” (not your diagnosis) even if vitals unstable coz its a consequence.

Explaining Diagnosis to Patient (exact structure/emphasis)

  • “You have a condition called an incomplete miscarriage.”
  • Add empathy:
    • “I'm sorry to tell you.”
    • “I'm so sorry to tell you that the miscarriage process has started.”
  • “Some of the pregnancy tissue has passed or has been expelled from your womb.”
  • “I had to remove part of the tissue from the opening of your womb.”
  • “The rest of the pregnancy tissue is still in the uterus, still in the womb.”
  • “You're having a condition called incomplete miscarriage, and again I'm really sorry that I have to give you this bad news.”
  • “You’ve already passed a part of the pregnancy tissue; I had to remove part of it from the cervix because that was the reason you were bleeding so bad and we had to stop the bleeding.”
  • “The rest… will pass on later; in the next few hours we expect the rest of the pregnancy tissue to pass on too.”

CASE 2

History

  • LMP: 6 weeks
  • Bleeding started today, getting worse
  • Pregnancy symptoms: tired, nausea

Physical Examination

  • Vitals stable
  • Bimanual exam:
    • Os closed
    • Cervical motion tenderness: positive
    • Uterus empty / normal size / small / not 6-week size
  • Adnexal tenderness on right side
  • Reminder: ectopic pregnancy in exam never gives a mass
  • UPT: negative
    • Key teaching: Urine test has detection threshold; ectopic has low beta-hCG; early pregnancy → negative UPT does not rule out ectopic pregnancy

Diagnostic Formulation

  • Diagnosis: Ectopic pregnancy
  • Even with negative UPT, diagnosis remains ectopic pregnancy

Explaining Diagnosis to Patient

  • “You have a condition called ectopic pregnancy.”
  • Add empathy before and after giving news:
    • “I'm really sorry that I'm giving you this bad news, Jenny.”
  • Explanation wording:
    • “In this condition, the pregnancy or the pregnancy sac grows or forms outside of the womb.”
    • “In your case, it has formed in your tubes.”
    • “This is an abnormal pregnancy and will not and cannot continue normally.”
    • “That is why you are bleeding and we need to treat you ASAP.”
  • Optional supportive phrasing used:
    • “I can see you were really excited and looking forward to this pregnancy.”
    • “Do you think you want your partner here when I discuss this with you?”
  • “This pregnancy cannot continue to grow and usually ends up with bleeding and we need to treat it ASAP.”
  • Mention that other conditions considered are the differential list.

CASE 3

History

  • LMP: 8 weeks
  • Bleeding started today
  • Bleeding is getting better (settling)
  • Pregnancy symptoms: nausea, morning sickness

Physical Examination

  • Vitals stable
  • Os closed
  • CMT negative
  • Uterus size: 8 weeks, non-tender, normal/border/regular, not contracting
  • No adnexal masses or tenderness
  • UPT: positive (not required)

Diagnostic Formulation

  • Diagnosis: Threatened miscarriage
  • Features leading to provisional diagnosis:
    • Os closed
    • No tissue passed
    • Bleeding settling
    • Uterus size exactly expected
    • Everything else normal

Explaining Diagnosis to Patient (very specific sequence required)

Build understanding before saying the words “threatened miscarriage.”

  1. “I examined you today because I was concerned about a miscarriage.”
  2. Reassure first:
    • “I’m happy to tell you that the pregnancy is normal.”
    • “The pregnancy findings are normal.”
    • “I ruled out a miscarriage.”
  3. Only after reassurance:
    • “Whenever we have bleeding in early pregnancy and the findings on examination are normal, we call this a threatened miscarriage.”

Expanded version as demonstrated:

  • Repeat explanation in slightly varied sequence:
    • “Based on the findings… your pregnancy features are normal.”
    • “I’ve been able to rule out a miscarriage.”
    • “The good news is that you’re not having a miscarriage.”
  • “Whenever we have bleeding in early pregnancy, but we see the pregnancy continuing normally and there is no concerns, we call that a threatened miscarriage.”
  • Stress:
    • “Although we call it a miscarriage, although we call it a threatened miscarriage, the pregnancy is normal.”
    • “The pregnancy is normally growing and there is no concern.”

✏️ Corrections

✏️ Case 2 teaches that 'ectopic pregnancy in exam never gives a mass'. This is an exam-simplification, not a clinical truth - a palpable adnexal mass is a recognised (though not universal) sign of ectopic pregnancy.
Reframe as: an adnexal mass may be absent, so its absence does not exclude ectopic; conversely a palpable/tender adnexal mass with an empty uterus supports ectopic. Do not teach that ectopic 'never' produces a mass.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ For Case 1 (incomplete miscarriage with BP 90/60, HR 110): document the recognition of cervical shock and that emergency management (call for help/000 in community, IV access, fluids, removal of products from os) would be indicated - even though the station restricts candidates to 'transfer to resus'. Flag the must-not-miss haemodynamic instability explicitly.
⭐ For Case 2 (ectopic): add that a negative urine pregnancy test does not exclude ectopic and that serum beta-hCG plus transvaginal ultrasound are the confirmatory investigations; add anti-D 250 IU for Rh(D)-negative women.
⭐ Add safety-netting/red-flag counselling script (shoulder-tip pain, collapse, worsening pain, heavy bleeding -> call 000) to each case, which the current empathy-focused scripts omit.
⭐ Add eTG and RANZCOG 'Ectopic Pregnancy' and 'Miscarriage' guideline references as metadata for each sub-case.
