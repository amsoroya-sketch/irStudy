Microcytic Anaemia in Pregnancy (Iron Deficiency vs Thalassaemia)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — obstetrics / class 6 (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: obstetrics_gynaecology

ANEMIA IN PREGNANCY

Your next patient is a 24 year old lady who is 15 weeks pregnant. She has come to you to discuss her blood test results.

On FBE:

• Low Hb

• Low MCV

• Low MCH

• (No Iron Given)

• Task:

• Explain results to the patient

• Take history

• Discuss possible causes and management

Anemia in Pregnancy – OSCE Case Notes

  • Two main differentials:
    1. Iron deficiency anemia
    2. Thalassemia
  • Thalassemia considered only because iron is not available
  • If iron was present, thalassemia doesn’t come into play

Task 1: Explain Investigations

  • Introduce yourself and open the case (don’t jump straight into results)
  • Suggested opening:
    “How can I help you today?”
  • Patient will mention coming to discuss bloods

Explanation (fast and efficient)

  • On your Full blood examination we checked your red blood cells
  • Red blood cells carry oxygen using a component called hemoglobin
  • Findings:
    • Amount of hemoglobin is decreased
    • Size of red blood cells decreased
    • Amount of hemoglobin in each cell decreased
  • Conclusion:
     Microcytic anemia (or simply anemia)
  • Ask permission:
    “Is it okay if I ask you a few questions?”

Task 2: History Taking

Opening Question

  • “How is the pregnancy going so far?”

    • Was this a planned pregnancy?
    • Congratulations (if appropriate)

Group 1: Anemia Symptoms

  • Any Shortness of breath on exercise
  • Any Racing heart (especially on exercise)
  • Feeling tired? (tricky because she is pregnant)

Group 2: Rule Out Thalassemia

Only questions available:

  1. Ethnic background
    • “one possible cause is thalaessmeia that is common in some ethnic groups .Can I know your background / ethnic background?”
  2. Family history
    • “Are you aware of any family history of thalassemia?”

Group 3: Iron Deficiency Anemia

Four points of iron pathway:

  1. Decreased intake
    • “Can you describe your diet?”
    • Specifically: Do you take red meat?
  2. Blood loss
    • Any recent surgery?
    • Heavy periods before pregnancy?
    • Blood donations?
    • GI blood loss:
      • Dark stools?
      • Abdominal pain?
      • Medications? (NSAIDs like ibuprofen)
      • Changes in appetite?
      • Lumps and bumps?
      • Any loss of weight before pregnancy?
  3. Usage (using too much iron)
    • Any Previous pregnancies?
    • Go deeper:
      • How many pregnancies?
      • When was the last one?
      • How old are your children? (looking for back-to-back pregnancies)
    • Delivery details:
      • Cesarean or vaginal?
      • Any significant blood loss during delivery?
  4. Malabsorption
    • Celiac questions:
      • Greasy stools?
      • Stick to the pan?
      • Hard to flush?
      • Float on water?
    • IBD questions:
      • Blood in stools?
      • Diarrhea?
    • Family history of celiac or IBD

Antenatal / OB History

  • Regular pregnancy bloods normal?
  • Dating scan?
  • Down syndrome screening?

Stop here and start antenatal history coz 15 weeks pregnant

  • Past medical history
  • Medications
  • Family history of genetic disorders or other conditions
  • Lifestyle
  • Mood / coping with pregnancy

Task 3: Diagnosis

  • Condition: Microcytic anemia
  • Two main causes:
    1. Iron deficiency anemia
    2. Thalassemia

Explain causes

  • Iron deficiency:
    • Being low on iron makes it hard to build new red blood cells for pregnancy → leads to anemia
  • Thalassemia:
    • Genetic condition
    • Runs in families
    • Causes anemia

Case Example Conclusion

  • Most likely u r having iron deficiency anemia
  • Reasons:
    • Vegetarian
    • Massive blood loss in previous pregnancies
    • Multiple pregnancies

Task 4: Management

Focus on Iron Deficiency Anemia

  • Iron supplements
    • Oral or intravenous
    • Oral side effects:
      • Constipation
      • Tummy upset
      • Dark stools
    • IV risks:
      • Allergic reaction
      • Skin staining
  • Increase iron intake in diet
    • Increase red meat
    • Increase iron-rich food

Monitoring

  • Recheck hemoglobin and ferritin
    • 2 weeks: check hemoglobin rising
    • 3 months: check ferritin storage replaced

Further Investigation

  • Iron studies (checking ferritin)
  • Hemoglobin electrophoresis (optional to mention)

If not rising

  • Consider other causes
  • Look for malabsorption
  • May include celiac serology or other tests
    (Not going deeper in this case)

✏️ Corrections

✏️ Haemoglobin electrophoresis is listed as 'optional to mention' when working up microcytosis, and thalassaemia is framed as relevant 'only because iron is not available'.
If iron studies are normal (ferritin not low) in a microcytic patient (MCV <80 fL, MCH <27 pg), haemoglobinopathy screening (Hb HPLC/electrophoresis) is indicated, NOT optional, and partner testing should be arranged if a trait is confirmed (KEMH/RANZCOG antenatal haemoglobinopathy pathway). Ferritin is the key discriminator and should be documented before commencing iron.
✏️ No pregnancy-specific anaemia thresholds or ferritin cut-off are documented.
Document Australian/WHO thresholds: anaemia = Hb <110 g/L in the first trimester and <105 g/L in the second/third trimester; iron deficiency = ferritin <30 microgram/L (a more sensitive cut-off in pregnancy). These define the diagnosis and the response target on recheck.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Use Australian spelling (anaemia, oesophageal) and PBS context: oral ferrous sulfate/fumarate is first line; consider intravenous iron (ferric carboxymaltose) if oral iron is not tolerated/absorbed or in the second-third trimester when rapid correction is needed - document counselling on IV skin-staining risk.
⭐ Add the pregnancy-specific reason microcytic anaemia matters: untreated iron deficiency is associated with preterm birth, low birth weight and postpartum haemorrhage tolerance - strengthening the management rationale.
⭐ Structure the iron-balance history the note already uses (intake/loss/increased-use/malabsorption) and add coeliac serology (tissue transglutaminase) as the named malabsorption test if Hb fails to rise on iron.
⭐ Document the monitoring plan with explicit targets: recheck Hb in 2-4 weeks expecting a rise of ~10-20 g/L, and ferritin at ~3 months to confirm stores replenished (RACGP iron-deficiency guidance).
