Vulvar Itchiness (Candidiasis, Atrophic Vaginitis, Lichen Sclerosus)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — gynae / 3. Vulvar problems (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: obstetrics_gynaecology

VULVAR ITCHINESS

1. Case Introduction

  • 60-year-old lady
  • Complaint: itchiness in the genital area (vulvar itchiness)
  • 6-minute history + diagnosis + differentials
  • Aim: build structure of history taking + differentials
  • Think: menopausal age → what differentials to rule out

2. Differential Diagnosis Framework

Two major inflammatory categories:

A. Dermatitis

  • Eczemas
  • Allergic dermatitis
  • Contact dermatitis
  • Lichen dermatitis versions:
    • Lichen sclerosus (main one in exam)
    • Lichen planus
    • Lichen simplex
    • Lichen planus described as “pruritic, papular, polygonal, purple lesions”

B. Vaginitis

  1. Vaginal infections
    • STIs: chlamydia, gonorrhea
    • Non-STIs: candida, bacterial vaginosis
    • Candida & BV can present with itchiness
    • Chlamydia may give “a little bit of itch”
  2. Atrophic vaginitis (important in menopausal lady)
  3. Cancers
    • Skin cancers
    • Vaginal cancers (rare but exist)
    • SCC in vulvar area

Goal: Rule out dermatitis + vaginitis + cancers.

3. History Taking Structure

Open-ended question

  • “How can I help you today?”

Initial Complaint Exploration

  • Can u describe is it itchy on skin or itchiness inside the vagina

Timing

  • Since when u have this itchiness?
  • On/off or continuous?
  • Getting worse?

Alleviating/Aggravating

  • Any factors making it better/worse?
  • Shower → consider scabies or contact dermatitis
  • Ask about any treatment tried and effect
  • Impact on life

4. Dermatitis Questions

  1. Any rashes or redness in the private parts?
  2. Any History of eczema or skin allergies
    • Considering urticaria possibility
  3. Contact dermatitis screening
    • Any new soap, body wash, cream, underwear?

5. Vaginitis Questions

A. Atrophic Vaginitis

  • Do you feel dry in the vagina?
  • Look for urinary symptoms:
    • Dysuria
    • Frequency

B. Non-STI Infections

  • Ask about discharge
  • If discharge present → explore using CCVO (as taught previously)
  • Characteristics help identify cause

C. Cancers (Red Flags)

  • Weight loss
  • Loss of appetite
  • Lumps/bumps (especially groin → inguinal lymph nodes)
  • Tiredness or night sweats

D. STI Screening

  • Symptoms: any abdominal pain, fever (PID considerations)
  • Sexual history:
    • Are you sexually active?
    • Ever been sexually active?
    • Stable relationship?
    • Multiple partners?
    • Practice safe sex? Use condoms?
    • Previous STI diagnosis?

6. Menopausal 5-Piece Structure

Used because patient is menopausal.

1. Period History

  • When was your last final menstrual period?

2. Menopausal Symptoms

  • Hot flushes
  • Mood disturbances
  • Sleep disturbances
  • Reminder: atrophic vaginitis sits here as menopause feature

3. Partner History

  • Dyspareunia
  • Post-coital bleeding

4. HRT Question

  • “Have you used HRT or are you on HRT?”

5. Pregnancies

  • Number of pregnancies
  • Breastfeeding link to cancers (mentioned in broader structure)

Additional two for menopausal ladies

  • Prolapse: Do you feel a lump down below?
  • Incontinence: have u ever leaked urine?
    • Leak on coughing, sneezing, sudden leaking?

Screening

  • Cervical screening up to date?
  • Mammogram

Past Medical History + Family History

  • Family history of cancers important

7. CASE VERSIONS

CASE VERSION 1 — Candidiasis

Findings from scenario:

  • Itchiness for two weeks
  • Vaginal discharge
  • White, cheesy, non-smelly
  • Frequency of urine
  • No rashes
  • No high-risk sexual history

Diagnosis:

  • Vaginal candidiasis / vaginal infection called candidiasis
  • Explanation: this is a fungal infection in the vagina

Reasoning:

  • Characteristics of discharge> White discharge
  • Non-smelly
  • Itchiness
  • Symptoms match
  • Other differentials listed but this fits best

Note from recalls:

  • Urine dipstick showing 4+ glucose sometimes included
  • Brings link to diabetes and candidiasis
  • Does not change diagnosis or approach

CASE VERSION 2 — Atrophic Vaginitis

Findings:

  • Itchiness
  • Feels dry and itchy in the vagina
  • No rash
  • No discharge
  • Physical exam: thin, dry vaginal walls

Diagnosis:

  • Atrophic vaginitis

Explanation:

  • Due to hormonal changes in menopause
  • Vaginal wall becomes thin and dry
  • Causes irritation and itchiness

Management (two things):

  1. Topical estrogen cream/gel
    • Helps inner lining grow back and regenerate
  2. Lubricants on sex
    • Helps dryness, dyspareunia, reduces friction and irritation

CASE VERSION 3 — Lichen Sclerosus (Old Version)

Findings:

  • White rash on private parts
  • Past medical history: Graves’ disease
    • Important because lichen sclerosus is autoimmune and linked to autoimmune conditions
  • Used estrogen + antifungals → minimal effect
  • Stopped sex due to dyspareunia
  • Physical exam (face-to-face style):
    • White rash in figure-of-eight appearance
    • Involves perivaginal + perianal area
    • Whitish wrinkled plaque
    • Adhesions, stenosis possible

Review of Lichen Sclerosus

  • White wrinkled plaque
  • Extremely itchy
  • Causes dyspareunia
  • Associated with autoimmune disorders:
    • Alopecia
    • Vitiligo
    • Autoimmune thyroid disorders (Hashimoto, Graves)
  • Diagnosis is clinical
  • Biopsy only if cancer suspected or rash unclear

Complications

  • Scarring
  • Stenosis
  • Can change into SCC

Treatment

  • Potent corticosteroid (very strong)
    • Clobetasol
    • Once at night
    • 12 weeks (3 months)
  • Then maintenance:
    • 2–3 nights per week
    • Lifelong
  • Ointments preferred (greasy, better for chronic skin inflammation)

Cancer surveillance

  • Examine for skin cancers
  • Every 6–12 months

Diagnosis Explanation (verbatim concept)

  • “Most likely you have a condition called lichen sclerosis.
    This is an autoimmune condition.
    Your own immune system damages the cells in the skin and creates inflammation.”

8. Final Summary (from transcript)

  • Three case types used in the exam:
    1. Candidiasis
    2. Atrophic vaginitis
    3. Lichen sclerosus
  • Stick to a good list of differentials to pull out the right diagnosis.

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: clinical-documentation-expert (Australian clinical supervisor, O&G / AMC OSCE) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ Lichen sclerosus is stated to be diagnosed clinically with 'biopsy only if cancer suspected or rash unclear'. This is broadly correct but understates biopsy thresholds.
Biopsy any lichen sclerosus that is atypical, fails to respond to adequate potent topical steroid, or shows a thickened/eroded/ulcerated or fixed area, because of the ~4-5% lifetime risk of vulval squamous cell carcinoma. Diagnosis is usually clinical but a low threshold for biopsy is appropriate in menopausal women.
✏️ Version 1 candidiasis lists no pharmacological management (only diagnosis), and recurrent candida is not addressed within this itchiness case.
Add eTG first-line: clotrimazole 1% vaginal cream or 100 mg pessary intravaginally at night for 6 nights, OR fluconazole 150 mg orally as a single dose. For >=4 episodes/year (recurrent), use fluconazole 150 mg on days 1, 4 and 7 then weekly for 6 months, confirm on culture, and screen for diabetes (relevant to the 4+ glycosuria note).
✏️ American spellings 'estrogen' and 'lichen sclerosis' are used.
Use Australian spelling: oestrogen; and the correct disease name is lichen sclerosus (not 'sclerosis').

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add a clear management plan for atrophic vaginitis with Australian products: topical vaginal oestrogen (e.g. oestriol cream or estradiol pessary) plus non-hormonal vaginal moisturisers/lubricants; note systemic MHT is an option if vasomotor symptoms co-exist.
⭐ Add the must-not-miss vulval neoplasia differentials explicitly (VIN, squamous cell carcinoma, extramammary Paget disease, melanoma) with the safety-net that any persistent, ulcerated, pigmented or non-healing vulval lesion needs biopsy - especially in a 60-year-old.
⭐ Add examination/investigation step: vulvovaginal swab (candida/BV/trichomonas + STI NAAT) and vaginal pH; and describe the 'figure-of-eight' peri-vulval/peri-anal white atrophic plaques of lichen sclerosus for the physical-exam component.
⭐ Add lichen sclerosus maintenance and surveillance detail per Australian dermatology guidance: potent topical corticosteroid (clobetasol propionate 0.05% ointment) nightly for 4 weeks, then taper to maintenance, with 6-12 monthly review for SCC; provide patient-education resource.
