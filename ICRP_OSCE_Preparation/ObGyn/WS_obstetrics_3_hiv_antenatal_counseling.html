HIV-Positive Antenatal Result - Counselling and Management

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — obstetrics / class 2 (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: obstetrics_gynaecology

Scenario and Task

A pregnant woman has had her routine antenatal booking bloods. Results show: syphilis, hepatitis B and hepatitis C negative; HIV screening positive, confirmed on Western blot / confirmatory testing.

  • Explain the results to the patient (breaking bad news).
  • Take a focused risk-factor history.
  • Outline management to reduce mother-to-child (vertical) transmission and further care.

Breaking the News

Use a structured breaking-bad-news approach:

  • Ensure a private, unhurried setting and ask if she would like a support person present.
  • Give a warning shot, then deliver the result clearly and empathically: "I'm sorry, I don't have the news we'd hoped for - the HIV test has come back positive, and it has been confirmed."
  • Allow silence; offer tissues and a glass of water; do not dismiss her distress.
  • Reassure realistically: HIV is now a manageable chronic condition. With modern antiretroviral therapy and the right care in pregnancy, the risk of passing HIV to the baby can be reduced to very low levels, and her own long-term health outlook is good.

Focused Risk-Factor History

  • Blood transfusions, needle sharing / injecting drug use, tattoos or needle-stick/occupational injury.
  • Sexual history: number of partners, unprotected sex, partner risk factors, previous sexually transmitted infections (within a non-judgemental, confidential framework).
  • Symptoms suggestive of immunosuppression or seroconversion: fever, rash, weight loss, night sweats, recurrent infections.
  • Whether the partner has been tested; consider partner testing and contact tracing.

Management to Reduce Vertical Transmission

  • Refer urgently to a specialist multidisciplinary HIV-in-pregnancy service (infectious diseases / HIV physician and obstetrics).
  • Commence combination antiretroviral therapy (cART) and monitor viral load and CD4 count regularly through the pregnancy; the goal is an undetectable viral load by delivery.
  • Avoid invasive procedures that can increase transmission risk (amniocentesis, chorionic villus sampling, fetal scalp electrodes and fetal scalp blood sampling) where possible.
  • Plan mode of delivery by viral load near term: if the viral load is high (commonly quoted threshold greater than 1000 copies/mL) plan elective caesarean section around 38 weeks; if the viral load is well suppressed, vaginal birth may be appropriate.
  • Neonatal care: the baby receives antiretroviral post-exposure prophylaxis after birth and HIV testing (PCR) over the first weeks to months of life.
  • Infant feeding: advise against breastfeeding because of the risk of transmission; formula feeding is recommended in the Australian setting.
  • Provide psychosocial support, confidentiality reassurance and clear follow-up.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The notes advise against breastfeeding as an absolute, without acknowledging current Australian nuance.
In the Australian setting, formula feeding remains the recommended default and 'avoid breastfeeding' is the standard AMC answer. However, updated ASHM/BHIVA-aligned guidance (2023-2024) recognises that a woman on effective cART with a sustained undetectable viral load who chooses to breastfeed should be supported through a shared decision-making pathway with specialist input and enhanced monitoring. Document the recommendation to avoid breastfeeding while noting specialist-led shared decision-making exists.
✏️ The result is acted on clinically but there is no mention of statutory/partner-notification obligations.
Newly diagnosed HIV is NOTIFIABLE to the public health unit in Australia, and partner notification/contact tracing (with the patient, often via the specialist service or a designated contact-tracing framework) is part of management. This should be documented alongside the clinical plan.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add 'U=U' (Undetectable = Untransmittable) counselling: an undetectable viral load on treatment means HIV is not sexually transmitted and dramatically reduces vertical transmission - a powerful, accurate reassurance message.
⭐ Add the Australian success context: with universal antenatal HIV screening, cART, appropriate delivery planning, avoidance of breastfeeding and neonatal prophylaxis, mother-to-child transmission in Australia is now well below 1% - concrete figures support informed, hopeful counselling.
⭐ Note prescribing/access detail: antiretrovirals are PBS-subsidised and prescribed via specialist/s100 pathways; document referral to an HIV s100 prescriber and the multidisciplinary team.
⭐ Metadata to add: tag as breaking-bad-news and cross-link to the routine antenatal screening card (HIV is a routine, consented booking test) and to hepatitis B/syphilis antenatal screening.
