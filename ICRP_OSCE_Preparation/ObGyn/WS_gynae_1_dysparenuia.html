Dyspareunia (Vulvodynia / Vaginismus) - History and Management

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — gynae / 2. intercourse and cervical problems (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: obstetrics_gynaecology

DYSPAREUNIA

  • Term: “Dyspareunia” = painful sex.
  • Common association: endometriosis often causes dyspareunia, but cases may not be endometriosis.

Key Distinction

Superficial vs Deep Dyspareunia

  • Superficial: pain at beginning/entry of intercourse → suggests external problems (skin conditions, dryness/atrophy, hormonal causes, postpartum changes, surgical cuts).
  • Deep: pain on deep penetration → suggests internal pathology (endometriosis, adenexal masses, PID).
  • Both are dyspareunia; this distinction guides differential.

Differentials (as listed in the video)

Superficial:

  • Hormonal contraception
  • Postpartum
  • Reduced ovarian function
  • Antiestrogen therapy

Deep:

  • Endometriosis
  • Adnexal masses (Fibroid, ovaries cancer)
  • PID
  • Uterine fibroids
  • Cervical cancers
  • Vaginismus
  • Vulvodynia

Psychosocial factors increasing pain

Intimate partner violence

Impact of mental health

Decreased lubrication

  • Hormonal contraception
  • Decreased physiologic arousal
  • Surgery

Infection

More common

  • Candidiasis: trial of suppression is worthwhile if chronic infection is suspected

Less common

  • Bacterial vaginosis or trichomoniasis: can sometimes cause burning
  • Genital herpes: usually episodic and distribution unilateral

Dermatoses

More common

  • Eczema, lichen simplex chronicus, contact dermatitis: abnormal examination, address skin care and consider a trial of topical corticosteroid

Less common

  • Psoriasis, lichen sclerosus, lichen planus, plasma cell vulvitis: abnormal examination and need specific treatment

Vaginal atrophy and atrophic vaginitis

Abnormal examination, trial of vaginal oestrogen

Lactational amenorrhoea can result in vival dryness and dyspareunia due to hypoestrogenisation

Neoplastic

Vulval intra-epithelial neoplasia, Paget's disease, squamous cell carcinoma: all uncommon but not to be missed, abnormal examination

Vulvodynia and Vaginismus — Definitions & Relationship

Vulvodynia

  • Vulvar pain for at least three months without a clear identifiable cause (diagnosis of exclusion).
  • Types: spontaneous and localized provoked (provoked typically at the vaginal opening; triggered by penetration or touch).
  • Patients may report pain even on touching the area.
  • Diagnosis of exclusion

Common Triggers & Contributory Factors

  • frequent skin inflammation
  • candida (thrush), even if itch and discharge are subtle
  • urinary infections
  • difficult to control dermatitis
  • very occasionally genital herpes may be involved

Vaginismus

  • Caused by spasm of the pelvic floor muscles during intercourse.
  • Includes marked difficulty with intercourse, pain, and fear/anxiety about intercourse.
  • Exam may show higher pelvic floor muscle tension.
  • Often associated with provoked vulvodynia; both commonly coexist and can be hard to differentiate without targeted questions.

Management: Physical therapy

Physiotherapy addresses pelvic muscle dysfunction. In our experience this may be the single most important intervention.

It involves down-training, biofeedback and desensitisation (NOT Kegel exercises). Not all physiotherapists are trained in this area so check before referring.

find out where your pelvic floor muscles are, how to turn them on and more importantly, how to turn them off

the next step is to train them to respond differently to the anticipation of intercourse or tampon use

The ultimate aim is to regain control of your mind and body's response to vaginal penetration

There are underlying negative feelings of anxiety associated with vaginal penetration.

effective treatment addresses any emotional triggers, so a full pain-free and pleasurable sexual relationship

Psychological and behavioural interventions (relaxation, mindfulness or meditation). Counselling is often helpful

Topical treatments

lignocaine 2% gel or 5% ointment

Pain-modifying medications: neuromodulators

Low-dose tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs)

endometriosis - a condition where cells that line the uterus (womb) grow in other parts of the body

adenomyosis — a condition where cells that line the uterus (womb) grow in the muscle around the uterus

prolapsed uterus - when the womb drops down towards the vaginal opening

fibroids - growths in the womb

ovarian cysts - fluid-filled sacs in the ovary

irritable bowel syndrome

bladder infection

Urinary tract infection

STI such as chlamydia or gonorrhoea

pelvic inflammateasdiseas cPID) - a condition where the uterus, fallopian tubes, and ovaries may become inflamed

having a dry vagina recent childbirth

ectopic pregnancy. — where the foetus grows outside of the womb

previous surgery or radiation treatment to the pelvis

Common causes of superficial pain include:

  • skin irritation or a condition like eczema
  • thrush
  • a condition called vaginismus (see below), which causes the muscles around the vagina to spasm
  • injury to part of your vagina
  • a foreign body being inside the vagina
  • having a dry vagina (this often happens around the time of menopause or after childbirth)
  • not enough lubrication (this may be due to lack or arousal, hormone changes or certain medicines)
  • • STI’s such as genital herpes or trichomoniasis

History Taking Framework

Your next patient is a 22 year old lady who presents to your General Practice complaining of pain during sex.

Task:

  • Take history from the patient
  • Physical examination will appear on the screen (In some cases Investigations given)
  • Explain your diagnosis with reasons
  • Explain your management plan

1. Introduction / Opening

  • Open-ended start: e.g., “How can I help you today?”
  • Reassure: acknowledge concern; explain plan (ask questions, examine, find cause, make management plan).

2. Explore Presenting Complaint (SIQORAA)

  • Site: superficial vs deep — ask “Is the pain at the beginning of entry or on deep penetration?”
    Pain when not having sex: ask do u have pain when u are not having sex.
  • Intensity: scale 1–10
  • Quality: burning vs dull/deep ache.
  • Onset/Timing: since when, on and off vs every time, getting worse?
  • Radiation: does pain drag anywhere?
  • Alleviating/Aggravating: anything makes it better/worse; does touch trigger pain (vulvodynia feature); any specific positions; do lubricants make it better?
  • Impact: how is this affecting your relationship?

3. “Five Ps” Differential Screen

  • Periods: LMP?, regularity?, excessive pain? (dysmenorrhea=endometriosis), heavy bleeding?, any bleeding or pain between periods?. (Helps assess fibroids, uterine masses.)
  • Partner: symptoms of PID (abdo pain?, vaginal discharge?, fever?); stable partner vs multiple partners; condom use; prior STIs; any bleeding after sex (post-coital bleeding=cervical cancer cue).
  • Pills (Contraception): are you using contraception?> if yes> any side effects? do you feel your vagina is dry? (atrophic vaginitis).
  • Pregnancies: recent deliveries, > if yes > any cuts made at delivery or tears, any recent surgeries on private parts, breastfeeding (can cause dryness , mgt give lubricants).
  • Pap smear: r u up to date with cervical screening?

4. Red Flags / Cancer Questions

  • Weight loss, appetite change, lumps/bumps, tiredness.
  • Do u have any bleeding after sex?, any abnormal vaginal discharge.
  • Ovarian cancer cues: bloating, abdominal pain, change in bowel motions.

5. Endometriosis Screen (if suspected)

  • Excessive period pain (dysmenorrhea), pain on passing urine, pain on passing bowel motions, occasional blood in urine or stools.
  • Note: DRE tenderness/nodularity may be given in exam variants.

6. Other Differentials to Check

  • UTI: dysuria (burning), urinary frequency.
  • Dermatology: any rash or redness on private parts.

7. Mini Psychiatric Assessment

  • Mood: “How’s your mood been lately?”
  • Relationship: any stresses or problems?
  • Sexual history specifics: first partner?
  • Apologies to ask/ sorry to ask, have you ever been subjected to violent sex? exposure to violent sex?
  • any Fear/anxiety about sex/intercourse (vaginismus cue).

HISTORY FINDINGS

  1. Pain from beginning of intercourse
  2. Better when stops
  3. Regular partner, LMP 2 weeks ago
  4. No discharge, no bleeding
  5. No pain on defecation
  6. Started sex 3 months ago

PE or Investigation

  • Normal
  • Does not allow speculum examination due to pain

Diagnosis

Vulvodynia if pain, on touch, NO fear or anxiety of intercourse

Vaginismus if pain, on touch, YES fear or anxiety of intercourse

But any one can be acceptable and managed same way

  • Diagnosis reasoning: with normal investigations and entry pain, likely vulvodynia (diagnosis of exclusion). If marked fear/anxiety and pelvic floor spasm present → vaginismus. Both may be acceptable; lecturer prefers vulvodynia unless fear/anxiety present.

Management

First-line / Core Interventions (for vulvodynia and vaginismus)

  • Pelvic floor physiotherapy: teach identification of pelvic muscles, exercises to “turn them off”, retrain control for intercourse/tampon use.
  • Psychological treatments: cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), sexual counselling, couple-based therapy, relaxation/meditation/mindfulness.
  • Aim: address muscle control and emotional triggers; restore pleasurable, pain-free sexual relationship.

Pharmacological / Adjunct Options

  • Topical: lignocaine gels to numb area.
  • Systemic: tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) for pain regulation if severe.
  • Lifestyle: lubricants for dryness; general advice (exercise/diet, relaxation).

How to Explain Diagnosis to the Patient (phrasing from video)

  • Vulvodynia: “You have a condition called vulvodynia — a condition that causes chronic pain in the private parts, usually triggered by intercourse or touching.”
  • Vaginismus: “You have a condition called vaginismus —in this condition, fear and anxiety of intercourse causing spasm/contraction of the muscles around the vagina, which causes pain.”
  • Emphasise that examinations/investigations have ruled out other problems.

Endometriosis Variant

  • History suggesting endometriosis: severe dysmenorrhea (pain during periods, lasting 7+ days), dyspareunia, pain on urination/bowel motions, occasional blood in urine/stools. DRE tenderness or nodularity (other PE can be found or normal)
  • Investigations: start with non-invasive (ultrasound may show endometrioma); laparoscopy is both diagnostic and therapeutic when pharmacotherapy/investigations fail.
  • Management highlights: regulate pain(dysmenorrhea) with NSAIDs and regulate periods with OCPs; consider laparoscopy if indicated.

Lecturer’s Practical Tips / Exam Points (from video)

  • Always differentiate superficial vs deep dyspareunia early.
  • Use the five Ps to screen and rule out several differentials.
  • Ask about pain on touch and fear/anxiety of intercourse to distinguish vulvodynia vs vaginismus.
  • Emphasise that vulvodynia is a diagnosis of exclusion — confirm investigations/physical exam are normal when making this diagnosis.

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: clinical-documentation-expert (Australian clinical supervisor, O&G / GP standards) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The endometriosis variant states investigations start with ultrasound and that laparoscopy is used 'when pharmacotherapy/investigations fail', implying laparoscopy is required to confirm the diagnosis.
Current RANZCOG/ESHRE guidance is that laparoscopy is no longer mandatory to diagnose endometriosis. Diagnosis can be made clinically (typical history of cyclical dysmenorrhoea, deep dyspareunia, dyschezia/dysuria) supported by transvaginal ultrasound, and empirical medical therapy (analgesia plus hormonal therapy - COCP or progestogen) can be commenced without laparoscopic confirmation. Laparoscopy is reserved for diagnostic uncertainty, failure of medical therapy, or when surgical treatment/fertility management is indicated.
✏️ The localised provoked subtype described (entry pain triggered by touch/penetration at the vaginal opening) is labelled generically as 'vulvodynia'.
Per ISSVD 2015 terminology this presentation is more precisely termed provoked vestibulodynia (localised provoked vulvodynia). Naming the subtype improves diagnostic accuracy and guides first-line therapy (topical local anaesthetic, pelvic floor physiotherapy, psychosexual therapy). The teaching point that it is a diagnosis of exclusion remains correct.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add a mandatory beta-hCG / urine pregnancy test and safety-netting for any woman of reproductive age presenting with pelvic/gynaecological symptoms before commencing hormonal therapy - not currently stated.
⭐ Explicitly list ectopic pregnancy and PID as red-flag 'not-to-be-missed' causes of deep dyspareunia in the differential summary (both appear in the Murtagh pelvic-pain diagnostic strategy model but are only implied in the case).
⭐ Reference Australian sources for management (eTG - 'Vulvodynia and vestibulodynia', RACGP/Jean Hailes for Women's Health resources) and note that topical lignocaine and low-dose TCAs (e.g. amitriptyline) are off-label/PBS-general prescribing - useful to add to metadata for citation.
⭐ Add a documentation prompt: record consent and that speculum examination was declined/not tolerated (as in this case) - important medicolegally and to justify deferring the examination.
