Large for Gestational Age (LGA) in Pregnancy

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — obstetrics / class 4 (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: obstetrics_gynaecology

LGA

Your next patient is a 28 year old lady who is 20 weeks pregnant. She has been travelling with her partner and had an unplanned pregnancy and had not done her antenatal screening or checkups. She has a history of irregular periods.

On her examination today her FH is 30cm.

Task:

• Take history

‪• Explain diagnosis with reasons and differentials

• (Request further investigation)

Key Points in the Stem

  • Stem clearly indicates large for gestational age.
  • FH +/- 3 cm difference is acceptable
  • Another significant point: history of irregular periods → suggests wrong dates as a differential.

Brainstorm: Differentials for Large for Gestational Age

Concept:
 Either

  • fluid is too much, or
  • baby is too big

If baby is too big

  • Macrosomia
  • Most common cause: diabetes

If fluid is too much

  • Polyhydramnios

Other causes

  • Birth defects / malformationss
    • System: GI malformations
      • Child urinates amniotic fluid → requires proper kidneys
      • Later swallows fluid → if GI malformations, → LGA
  • Wrong dates (top of list)
  • Multiple pregnancy
  • Fibroid (mass increases uterine size)
  • TORCH infections
    • Can cause SGA or LGA
  • Placental problems (e.g., chorioangiomas) – rarest differential

Handbook Case Summary (AMC Handbook Content)

  • Lady in GP for shared care.
  • All normal until 26-week visit → SFH was 28.
  • Four weeks later, at 30 weeks → SFH 40 cm.
  • Weight gain: 6 kg in four weeks.

Information given

  • Exact weights provided:
    • 56 kg at beginning of pregnancy
    • 76 kg now
  • Not enough data to conclude BMI issues.

History Findings

  • No family history of diabetes
  • Glucose tolerance test normal
  • Ultrasound: single pregnancy
  • No edema, no headaches, no visual disturbances
    • These questions → screen for preeclampsia
  • No prior diagnosis of fibroids (cannot ask directly if told no one has mentioned fibroids before)

Physical Examination (from examiner’s findings)

  • Uterus: tense, not tender
  • No edema
  • Blood pressure normal
  • Urine test: negative for protein
  • 18-week morphology scan:
    • Single baby
    • No malformations at that point

Handbook Discussion Point

  • Likely large for gestational age due to excessive amniotic fluid → polyhydramnios
  • Emphasis on “probably”:
    • Diagnosis not confirmed
    • Need amniotic fluid index (AFI) on ultrasound to confirm
  • AFI high → confirms polyhydramnios
  • Keep in mind: uncertainty until AFI is done

Differentials (Handbook List)

  • Multiple pregnancy
  • Macrosomia
  • Fibroids
  • Fetal malformations (CNS, GI)
  • Diabetes
  • Placental chorionangioma
  • TORCH infections (toxoplasmosis, CMV)

Management

  • Advise extra bed rest
  • Premature delivery is likely
  • Must first confirm diagnosis
  • Refer to specialist
  • Possible place for prophylactic steroid therapy

Most Important Case Message (Exam Key Point)

  • Knowledge of causes of excessive uterine enlargement in pregnancy is key
  • Critical error:
    • Failure to consider possible causes of LGA
    • Must rule out differentials
    • If you don’t rule out differentials → no passing

Common Candidate Problems

  • Failure to inquire about diabetes
  • Failure to inquire about 18–20 week ultrasound results

Confirmation of Diagnosis

  • Diagnosis of polyhydramnios → by AFI
    • AFI > 24 → labelled as polyhydramnios

HISTORY STRUCTURE

Mnemonic for differentials

  • IM4DD (teacher used both terms): reminder to include Infections plus the following:
    • Four M's
      • Macro baby / Macrosomia
      • Multiple (twins / multiple pregnancy)
      • Masses (fibroids)
      • Malformations (GI malformations commonly)
    • D, D
      • Dates (wrong/incorrect dates)
      • Diabetes
    • (Optional: chorioangioma as a least important differential)

Opening / Initial approach

  • Start with an open-ended question.
  • If patient raises concern (e.g. "my stomach looks big"), use a concern statement: acknowledge concern, then proceed: “I understand how concerning that is, but let's go through a few questions together.”
  • Ask about planned pregnancy and offer brief congratulations if yes.

OB history structure (second & third trimester sequence)

(Use this sequence depending on gestational age; ask appropriate items down to matching gestational age)

  1. Routine pregnancy blood tests — did you do them at the beginning of pregnancy? Were they normal?
  2. Dating scan — did you have a dating scan? (ticks off wrong dates)
  3. Down syndrome screening — did you have it?
  4. Ultrasound (18–20 weeks morphology scan) — did you have it? If yes: single or multiple pregnancy?
  5. Sweet drink test — did you do your sweet drink test at ~26–28 weeks?
  6. Baby movements — (>20 weeks) are you feeling your baby kicking normally?
  7. Preeclampsia screening (for any 3rd trimester case) — ask for headache, leg swelling (brief set of questions).
  8. Previous pregnancies — ask about complications relevant to this case (e.g. history of large baby, delivery problems, gestational diabetes).
  • Optional: GBS / swab test around 36 weeks (not usually in routine list but can be included if relevant).

Applying OB history to this case (20 weeks gestation)

  • At 20 weeks ask through the sequence up to baby kicking and previous pregnancies.
  • Specific focused questions for previous pregnancies: history of high blood sugar, history of big baby, delivery complications.

Differential review using mnemonic (detailed questions / prompts)

1) Infections

  • Ask: fever or rash during pregnancy?
  • Ask: contact with sick people?
  • Ask: pets at home?
  • Ask: ate raw meat during pregnancy?
  • (Link to TORCH — toxoplasmosis, CMV, Rubella)

2) Macrosomia

  • Ask: family history of big babies?
  • Ask: rapid/unusual weight gain during pregnancy?
    • (prefers asking for rapid weight gain rather than raw numbers — relate to BMI baseline.)

3) Masses / Fibroids

  • Ask: ever been diagnosed with fibroids?
  • Ask: history of heavy periods (prior to pregnancy)?

4) Multiple pregnancy

  • Confirm: what did your morphology ultrasound say — single or multiple?
  • Ask for signs: excessive nausea/vomiting (hyperemesis)
  • Ask risk factors: family history of twins, assisted pregnancy / IVF

5) Malformations

  • Main assessment: morphology ultrasound (18–20 week scan)

6) Dates

  • Ask: When was your LMP? Are you sure about the dates?
  • Ask: Were your periods regular or irregular?

7) Diabetes

  • If OGTT not yet done (usually 26–28 weeks), screen for symptoms:
    • Polyuria
    • Polydipsia
    • Family history of diabetes

8) Substances / Medications (less important for LGA but to tick off)

  • Ask: smoking, alcohol, recreational drugs, current medications

Typical stem findings

  • Friend commented tummy looks bigger.
  • Patient hasn't done checkups/tests.
  • Sister had a large baby previously.
  • No signs of infections given.
  • No signs pointing clearly to diabetes, macrosomia, or multiple pregnancy from history alone.

Prioritising differentials

Note: this case doesn’t have one diagnosis, we need to tell possible differentials and say we need further investigations

  • Top consideration: Wrong dates — patient has irregular periods and no dating scan.
  • Second: Family tendency to large babies / Macrosomia — sister had a large baby.
  • Also consider: Polyhydramniosis (polyhydramnios) — possible but cannot conclude without investigations or PE (lecturer emphasised handbook cases that ranked polyhydramnios high only when PE showed a tense uterus).
  • Cannot conclude polyhydramnios here because no investigations and no PE findings provided in stem.

Physical examination structure (general points lecturer emphasised)

  • May be given on a card in the station (fundal height on mannequin) or examiner may ask you to perform fundal height on mannequin.
  • Fundal height measurement technique: tape from top of symphysis pubis to highest point of fundus; measure and record.
  • General appearance: level of consciousness (LOC), oedema, pallor (examiner highlighted oedema/preeclampsia concern and pallor).
  • Vitals: blood pressure is key (for preeclampsia concerns).
  • Abdominal / uterine exam (second/third trimester):
    • Fundal height
    • Uterine border — regular vs irregular (irregular → think mass/fibroid)
    • Tenderness
    • Tenseness — ask for tense if suspecting polyhydramniosis / polyhydramnios
    • would not do Leopold’s routinely here; ask examiner to state lie / presentation and importantly record fetal heart rate
  • Office tests during visit / immediate tests
    • Urine dipstick — glucose, protein
    • Blood sugar level (capillary / random)
    • (PFE task not common for this case; fundal height often supplied in stem.)

Investigations

  1. Ultrasound — specify reasons:
    • Amniotic Fluid Index (AFI) — look at amount of amniotic fluid (to assess polyhydramniosis/polyhydramnios)
    • Look for fibroids / masses in the uterus
    • Confirm single vs multiple pregnancy
  2. CTG — as part of fetal wellbeing check (especially in 2nd/3rd trimester)
  3. OGTT — do sugar test if not yet done (lecturer suggested possibly earlier if not yet performed)
  4. Toxo / CMV serology — maybe consider if infections suspected
  • (Lecturer concluded: ultrasound first, then targeted tests per findings)

How to explain the condition to the patient

  • “U have a condition that we call Large for gestational age” which means “your womb is larger than we expect in this age of pregnancy.”
  • there can be various causes — proceed step by step.

Causes / differential list

  1. It can be becoz of Macrosomia which means baby is large— family history of big babies noted in stem
  2. It can be Polyhydramniosis / Polyhydramnios which means there is an increase amount of fluid around the baby
  3. Wrong / incorrect dates > irregular periods , doubt about LMP, not done dating scan
  4. Sometimes Infections — TORCH (toxoplasmosis, CMV highlighted)
  5. Sometimes Masses like fibroids which r bening lumps can cause it.
  6. Birth defects and Malformations GI tract like mouth, stomach, foodpipe
  7. Diabetes (maternal high blood sugar → large baby)
  8. Multiple pregnancy (twins)
  9. Chorioangioma / lumps in the placenta which is a tissue that connect baby to the womb

Final lecturer summary / exam tips (exact emphasis)

  • Do not panic about making a single definitive diagnosis — list differentials and give reasons for each.
  • Rule out differentials systematically using history, PE (or PE card), and investigations (especially ultrasound with AFI).
  • If you have fundal height provided in stem, the case design often already points to LGA and you proceed with targeted questions/investigations.
  • Pass the case by: getting a good structured history, ticking off differentials (using the mnemonic), reasonable reasoning, and requesting appropriate investigations (ultrasound ± OGTT ± CTG ± serology as indicated).

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: clinical-documentation-expert (Australian teaching hospital clinical supervisor) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ Management advises 'extra bed rest' as a routine measure for suspected polyhydramnios/LGA.
Routine bed rest is not evidence-based and is not recommended in current Australian antenatal practice (RANZCOG). It increases venous thromboembolism risk without benefit. Management should focus on confirming the cause (ultrasound with AFI/single deepest pocket, OGTT, fetal morphology review), maternal blood group and antibody screen, and referral to an obstetrician / maternal-fetal medicine service, with serial fetal surveillance.
✏️ Case lists 'prophylactic steroid therapy' as a general management step for LGA/polyhydramnios.
Antenatal corticosteroids (betamethasone) are indicated only when preterm birth is anticipated within 7 days before approximately 34-35 weeks gestation, not as routine prophylaxis for LGA. It should be tied to a specific risk of preterm delivery (e.g. polyhydramnios precipitating preterm labour), not given empirically.
✏️ OGTT ('sweet drink test') is described as being performed at ~26-28 weeks.
In Australia the routine 75 g oral glucose tolerance test is performed at 24-28 weeks (ADIPS/RANZCOG). An early OGTT (first trimester) should be offered when risk factors are present (previous macrosomia/GDM, BMI >30, family history, high-risk ethnicity), which is relevant given this patient had no early screening.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add fetal anaemia/hydrops (Rh alloimmunisation, parvovirus B19) as a differential for excess uterine size/polyhydramnios, and include maternal blood group and red-cell antibody screen plus parvovirus serology in the investigation list.
⭐ Surface polyhydramnios red flags/safety-netting for the counselling task: risk of preterm labour, malpresentation, cord prolapse at rupture of membranes, placental abruption and postpartum haemorrhage - patient should be advised to present urgently (000 / birth unit) with contractions, fluid loss or reduced fetal movements.
⭐ State the Australian diagnostic thresholds explicitly: polyhydramnios = AFI >24-25 cm or single deepest vertical pocket >=8 cm; macrosomia = estimated fetal weight >4000-4500 g / >90th centile on customised growth chart.
⭐ Add eTG/RANZCOG-aligned metadata: link to the shared antenatal care schedule (dating scan, first-trimester combined screening, 20-22 week morphology scan, 28 week OGTT) so candidates can justify which screening steps were missed in this unbooked patient.
⭐ Reinforce that TORCH screening includes rubella and that rubella (MMR) is a National Immunisation Program vaccine - document rubella immunity status as part of routine antenatal bloods.
