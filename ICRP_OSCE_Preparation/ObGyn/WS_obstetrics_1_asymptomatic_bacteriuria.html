Asymptomatic Bacteriuria in Pregnancy

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — obstetrics / class 2 (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: obstetrics_gynaecology

Scenario and Task

A pregnant woman at approximately 13 weeks gestation has completed her first-trimester antenatal booking investigations. Her results include: syphilis serology negative, rubella IgG positive (immune), and a midstream urine (MSU) culture showing significant growth of a single organism at low colony count risk category in an otherwise well woman.

  • Review and explain the investigation results to the patient.
  • Explain the finding of asymptomatic bacteriuria and its significance in pregnancy.
  • Outline management and follow-up.

Focused History

Confirm the patient is genuinely asymptomatic by screening for urinary and systemic symptoms - the presence of any of these would reclassify the picture as symptomatic infection (cystitis or pyelonephritis) rather than asymptomatic bacteriuria:

  • Lower urinary tract / cystitis: dysuria (burning on passing urine), urinary frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain.
  • Upper urinary tract / pyelonephritis: fever/rigors, loin (flank) pain, nausea and vomiting, feeling systemically unwell.
  • Brief antenatal context: last menstrual period and gestation, symptoms of pregnancy, past medical history, medications and allergies, and cervical screening status.

Interpreting the Result - What Asymptomatic Bacteriuria Means

Explain in plain language: the urine sample grew a significant amount of a single type of bacteria, but because there are no symptoms (no burning, no frequency, no abdominal pain, no fever), this is called asymptomatic bacteriuria - "there are some bugs there, but they are not currently causing you any symptoms."

Emphasise why this matters specifically in pregnancy: untreated asymptomatic bacteriuria in pregnancy carries a substantial risk of progressing to acute pyelonephritis (an upper kidney infection), which can require intravenous antibiotics and is associated with pregnancy complications such as preterm birth and low birth weight. This is why - unlike in non-pregnant adults - asymptomatic bacteriuria is screened for and treated in pregnancy.

Management

  • Treat with antibiotics guided by the urine culture susceptibilities. Suitable options in pregnancy include nitrofurantoin, cephalexin, or amoxicillin depending on sensitivities (source notes reference nitrofurantoin / cephalexin, typically a short course of around 5 days).
  • Confirm cure: repeat the MSU culture (test of cure) 1-2 weeks after completing antibiotics to ensure the infection has cleared; periodic re-screening for the remainder of the pregnancy.
  • Advise the patient to return promptly if she develops burning, frequency, fever, loin pain or feels unwell (progression to symptomatic UTI/pyelonephritis).
  • Continue routine antenatal care, including iodine supplementation and other standard supplements.

Group B Streptococcus Consideration

The source notes mention giving antibiotics during labour to protect the baby. Clarify: this applies specifically if the organism cultured is Group B Streptococcus (GBS). GBS bacteriuria in pregnancy is an indication for intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis during labour to prevent early-onset neonatal GBS sepsis. It is not a routine step for every organism causing asymptomatic bacteriuria - it depends on the culture result.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The source notes imply intrapartum ('during your delivery') antibiotics will be given to protect the baby as a general consequence of asymptomatic bacteriuria.
Intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis is indicated only when the organism is Group B Streptococcus (GBS bacteriuria) - not for every organism causing asymptomatic bacteriuria. The immediate treatment of asymptomatic bacteriuria is a susceptibility-guided oral antibiotic course now, with a test-of-cure MSU afterwards; intrapartum prophylaxis is a separate, organism-specific consideration.
✏️ Nitrofurantoin is listed as a treatment option without gestational or safety caveats.
Per eTG (Therapeutic Guidelines), nitrofurantoin should be avoided at term (from 36 weeks and during labour) due to the risk of neonatal haemolysis, and is contraindicated in G6PD deficiency. Trimethoprim should be avoided in the first trimester (folate antagonist). Antibiotic choice must be culture-directed and gestation-appropriate.

⭐ Add the Australian diagnostic threshold: asymptomatic bacteriuria is defined as significant growth (generally 10^8 CFU/L, i.e. 10^5 CFU/mL) of a single uropathogen on MSU in the absence of symptoms - documenting the numerical threshold aids clinical reasoning.
