Early Pregnancy Bleeding - Assessment Structure

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — obstetrics / class 3 (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: obstetrics_gynaecology

EARLY PREGNANCY BLEEDING STRUCTURE

Initial Scenario

  • Pregnant lady in early pregnancy presents with vaginal bleeding.
  • Key questions:
    • What do we need to do for her?
    • What are we thinking about?

Ultrasound Timing Landmarks

  • Important for new cases: knowing when findings should appear on ultrasound.
  • Gestational sac visible: ~4.5–5 weeks
    • 5 weeks = safe mark
    • Earlier → sac too small to see
  • Cardiac activity: 6 weeks
  • Fetal pole / embryo: 7 weeks
  • If a woman is ~6 weeks and no sac is seen:
    • Could mean no sac OR simply too early
    • This timeframe helps form expectations for new cases

Review of Key Conditions

Ectopic Pregnancy

  • Vaginal bleeding, ± abdominal pain
  • Uterus: empty, normal size
  • Ultrasound: adenexal mass with gestational sac (usually in the tubes)
  • Diagnosis: Ultrasound + β-HCG
    • β-HCG rises abnormally (does not rise normally)
  • Risk factors:
    • Previous ectopic
    • IUD
    • Smoking
    • Previous STIs
    • Medical terminations of pregnancy

Complete Miscarriage / Complete Pregnancy Loss

  • All products of conception have been expelled
  • Uterus: very small, contracted, almost normal size
  • Os does not need to be open
  • Pain and bleeding settle once tissue expelled
  • If os closed but uterus small → still complete miscarriage

Incomplete Miscarriage / Incomplete Pregnancy Loss

  • Some tissue has passed, not all
  • Cervix: os open
  • Increasing vaginal bleeding
  • Uterine contractions
  • Uterus size:
    • Large due to retained tissue
    • But smaller than expected for gestational age
    • Example: 8-week pregnancy → uterus feels ~6-week size
  • Tissue may be seen in os
    • ALWAYS remove tissue (otherwise continued bleeding → cervical shock)

Tissue in the Os

  • If tissue visible during speculum exam:
    • Remove immediately
    • Prevents bleeding and cervical shock

Threatened Miscarriage / Threatened Abortion

  • Term can upset patients → careful communication
  • Provisional diagnosis (must later confirm)
  • Findings:
    • Vaginal bleeding
    • Os closed
    • Uterus size matches gestational age
    • Bleeding stopped
    • Ultrasound shows:
      • Sac present
      • Fetal cardiac activity present
  • Initial diagnosis is only a guess until investigations confirm

Implantation Bleeding

  • Diagnosis of exclusion
  • Mild spotting, 10–14 days after fertilization
  • Not common; minimal spotting only
  • Only consider after ruling out every other differential
  • Not to be used as a provisional diagnosis without investigations

Non-pregnancy Sources of Bleeding

  • Bleeding may originate from:
    • Vagina
    • Cervix
  • Important non-pregnancy causes:
    • Vaginal laceration
    • Cervical polyps
    • Cervical neoplasm
    • Other cervical abnormalities

Heterotopic Pregnancy

  • Increasingly discussed but still rare
  • Twin pregnancy:
    • One normal intrauterine pregnancy
    • One ectopic pregnancy in tube
  • Can be brought up but lecturer prefers stronger differentials first

Molar Pregnancy (Hydatidiform Mole)

  • Abnormal pregnancy with characteristic features:
    • Very high β-HCG
    • Uterus size larger than gestational age
    • Causes hyperemesis (from high β-HCG)
  • Ultrasound:
    • Snowstorm appearance
    • No fetal pole
    • No amniotic fluid
    • No fetal cardiac activity
  • Should be part of differential list

Algorithm for Assessing Early Pregnancy Bleeding

  1. Confirm pregnancy test positive
  2. Ask: Has she passed tissue?
    • Yes → incomplete or complete miscarriage
      • Determine which one
    • No → proceed to ultrasound
  3. Ultrasound
    • If gestational sac in uterus → good; evaluate further
    • If no sac in uterus → look for adenexal mass
      • If adenexal mass present → ectopic
      • If no mass:
        • Consider gestational age
        • May be too early to see sac
  4. If sac present:
    • Look for fetal pole
    • If fetal pole present → check cardiac activity
    • If no fetal pole → sac size helps determine if early or abnormal
  5. If early (<6 weeks):
    • Ultrasound may not be conclusive
    • May need to repeat in 7–10 days

Cardiac Activity & β-HCG

  • Cardiac activity reliably seen after 6 weeks
  • Requires β-HCG ≥10,000
  • Diagnosing ectopic:
    • Abnormal β-HCG rise (not doubling every 48 hrs)
    • Lower-than-expected absolute β-HCG levels
    • Distinguish from incomplete/complete miscarriage via ultrasound

RACGP Guidance

Initial Assessment

  • First step: Assess hemodynamic stability
    • If unstable → possible:
      • Ruptured ectopic
      • Incomplete miscarriage with cervical shock due to tissue in os

Speculum Examination

  • Must be performed
  • Remove any products from the cervical os
  • Clarification on bimanual exam:
    • Some medical officers claim it’s contraindicated in ectopic
    • Lecturer disagrees:
      • You cannot diagnose ectopic without bimanual exam
      • Must assess uterus size and adenexal tenderness

Risk Factors for Ectopic (review)

  • IUD
  • Mini-pills
  • Assisted reproduction
  • History of STIs
  • Previous ectopic
  • Cervical smear history relevant (cervical causes of bleeding)
  • Diabetes/thyroid not very helpful for exams

Uterine Size Landmarks

  • Uterus palpable above symphysis pubis at 12 weeks
    • Symphysis pubis = 12-week mark
  • Umbilicus = 20-week mark
  • If uterus palpable earlier:
    • Multiple pregnancy
    • Molar pregnancy
    • Pelvic/uterine masses (fibroid, ovarian cyst)

Examination

  • Inspect vagina and cervix
  • Remove any tissue in os
  • Send tissue for histopathology
  • Perform bimanual exam:
    • Uterus size
    • Pelvic/adenexal tenderness
    • Cervical motion tenderness

Investigations

  • Ultrasound (primary)
  • β-HCG (monitor rise every 48 hrs)
  • Blood group and antibody screen
    • Anti-D indications:
      • Ectopic pregnancy
      • Complete miscarriage
      • Incomplete miscarriage
      • Medical/surgical termination
    • Do NOT give anti-D in threatened miscarriage before 12 weeks
      • Insufficient evidence
    • Give only if mother is Rh-negative

Diagnosing Non-viable Pregnancy

  • Must meet criteria:
    1. No fetus seen in a gestational sac large enough to expect one
      • Small sac → cannot diagnose non-viability
    2. For fetal pole: follow crown–rump standards (details imply caution)
  • Warning:
    • Do not make premature non-viability diagnoses
    • Many women later show normal pregnancy after initial “miscarriage” diagnosis
  • If uncertain:
    • Explain two possibilities
    • Repeat ultrasound in 1 week

Management Notes

Threatened miscarriage

  • No specific treatment needed
  • Do not refer to ED
  • Give red flags
  • Arrange ultrasound next morning
  • If cardiac activity normal and size good → reassurance

Incomplete / Missed Miscarriage

  • Options:
    • Expectant (wait; will pass eventually)
    • Medical (similar to termination)
    • Surgical (dilate + curette)

Complete Miscarriage

  • Evaluate ongoing bleeding
  • Os should be closed
  • If bleeding continues:
    • Possible retained products
    • Do a TVS to check

Anti-D Summary

  • Give for:
    • Ectopic pregnancy
    • Complete miscarriage
    • Incomplete miscarriage
    • Termination of pregnancy
  • Do not give for threatened miscarriage before 12 weeks
  • Only for Rh-negative mothers

Key Takeaways

  • Ultrasound timing matters:
    • Sac at 5 weeks, heartbeat at 6, embryo at 7.
  • Tissue in cervical os must always be removed.
  • Threatened miscarriage = provisional diagnosis; confirm later.
  • Implantation bleeding = diagnosis of exclusion.
  • Ectopic diagnosis requires both ultrasound and bimanual exam.
  • Uterus size landmarks: 12 weeks (symphysis pubis), 20 weeks (umbilicus).
  • Non-viable pregnancy should not be diagnosed early unless criteria clearly met.
  • Anti-D not indicated for threatened miscarriage <12 weeks.
  • Repeat ultrasound in 7–10 days when early pregnancy findings are inconclusive.

Your next patient is a 25 year old lady who has presented to your General Practice complaining of vaginal bleeding. She has missed her period and the LMP is 6/8 weeks ago. Home pregnancy test has been done and is positive.

Task:

• Take history from the patient for 4 minutes

• Ask physical examination from the examiner, you will be given findings only if you ask specific questions

• Explain your diagnosis and differentials to the patient

HISTORY TAKING

1. Initial Approach & Patient Stability

  • After entering, perform ID check and hand sanitisation.
  • Before starting history:
    • Ask examiner for vital signs: blood pressure, pulse rate, (optionally respiratory rate and temperature).
  • Begin with a nice open-ended question.

2. Addressing the Patient’s Concern

  • Patient is pregnant and bleeding; often distressed or teary.
  • Acknowledge and validate feelings:
    • “I’m really sorry to hear that… I understand your concern… I know how overwhelming this can be.”
  • Bridge to history:
    • “Let me ask you a few questions, I’ll find the cause of the bleeding, and make sure you get the best care.”
  • 3. Planned Pregnancy
  • Ask: “Is this a planned pregnancy?”
     (No congratulations in this context.)

4. Exploring the Bleeding

A. Timing

  • When did it start?
  • Is the bleeding getting worse or improving?

Incomplete abortion bleeding gets worse

Complete/threatened abortion bleeding gets better

B. Characteristics – CCVO

  1. Color
    • Bright red or dark?
  2. Content
    • Key question: Have you passed any tissue?
    • Passed any clots?
    • Any grape-like tissue (optional; for molar pregnancy but rarely positive)?
    • Any discharge?
  3. Volume
    • How many pads used?
    • Were the pads fully soaked or just spotting?
  4. Odour (less important but may ask to maintain structure)

5. Abdominal Pain (Linked to Bleeding)

  • Must ask: “Do you have abdominal pain?”
  • If yes, perform a mini pain assessment:
    • Site
    • Intensity
    • Pattern: on/off or in waves vs constant
    • Optional final question: radiation
  • Do not perform full SOCRATES; avoid questions about quality or aggravating/alleviating factors.

6. Early Pregnancy 5Ps

P1: Periods

  • When was your last menstrual period (LMP)?
  • Any irregular periods or heavy periods before pregnancy?

P2: Pregnancy Symptoms

  • Feeling tired?
  • Any nausea/vomiting?
    • If vomiting: ask how many times per day (to differentiate normal vs hyperemesis).

(Reordered for Time Management) Pregnancy Before Partner

P3: Pregnancy (Current & Previous)

Current Pregnancy

  • Is this your first pregnancy?
  • Any assisted conception (e.g., IVF)?

Previous Pregnancies

  • Any previous miscarriages?
  • Any terminations?
  • Any previous caesarean sections?
  • Any previous uterine surgery?

(These assess risk factors—especially for ectopic pregnancy.)

P4: Partner

  • Are you in a stable relationship?
  • Is your partner supportive?
  • Any history of sexually transmitted infections?

P5: Cervical Screening

  • Have you had your cervical screening?
  • Are you up to date?
  • Was it normal?

7. Instructor Summary Points

  • Do not forget to fully characterise the bleeding—especially asking about tissue.
  • Must ask about abdominal pain before moving to the 5Ps.
  • Follow the early pregnancy 5Ps consistently.
  • Give the pregnancy-related risk factors priority over partner questions if time is short.

PHYSICAL EXAMINATION AND COUNSELLING

Section 1 — Asking the Examiner for Permission to Perform Physical Examination

A. General Appearance

Ask the examiner:

  • “On general appearance, is the lady alert or drowsy?” (Level of consciousness?)
  • “Do I see pallor?”
  • May add: “Features of dehydration.”

B. Vital Signs

  • Can revisit vital signs here.
  • Add: “Do I have any postural hypotension or orthostatic hypotension?”

C. Abdominal Examination

Ask the examiner:

Inspection

  • “Do I have any distension or any visible masses?”
  • Optional: “Is movement symmetric with respiration?”

Palpation

  • “Do I have any tenderness?”
  • “Do I have any masses?” (specifically looking for palpable uterus or lower abdominal masses)

D. Pelvic Examination

Use the lecturer’s OSCE-style phrase:

  • “Examiner, I want a pelvic exam. I want to do it with the consent, with the presence of a chaperone.”
     (The lecturer notes: unclear if AMC requires it, but “always safer to say.”)

Ask the examiner:

Inspection

  • “Do I see any bleeding or discharge?”
  • Optional: “Any redness, rashes?”

Speculum Exam
 Lecturer emphasises the key point:

  • “Tell me the os. Is my os open or closed?” (Most important)
  • “Do I see any tissue or bleeding from the os?”
  • Optional/less important: “Looking at vaginal wall and cervix for lesions.”

Bimanual Examination
 Follow the exact structure:

  1. Cervical Motion Tenderness
    • “Is cervical motion tenderness positive?” (Expected positive in ectopic)
  2. Uterus — ask:
    • “Tell me the size.”
    • “Tell me the border of the uterus.”
    • “Is it tender?”
    • “Do I feel any contractions?” (Nice to add)
  3. Adnexa
    • “Do I have any adnexal masses or tenderness?”

G. Office Tests

  • Optional: “I want a urine pregnancy test.”
    • Expect UPT positive.

Section 2 — Counselling a Mother Regarding Early-Pregnancy Bleeding

A. Investigations

Explain (only what the transcript states):

  • “We want an ultrasound.”
  • Prefer transvaginal ultrasound: early pregnancy; abdominal US not very accurate.
  • “We want a beta-HCG.”
  • Often consider serial beta-HCG to look for the normal rise.
    • If not rising normally → likely missed miscarriage, ectopic, or another abnormal pregnancy.

B. Differential Diagnoses for Early Pregnancy Bleeding

Explain to the mother (only using lecturer’s list):

  1. Miscarriages
    • Complete miscarriage
    • Incomplete miscarriage
    • Threatened miscarriage (“not exactly a miscarriage but in the same group”)
  2. Abnormal Pregnancies
    • Ectopic pregnancy
    • Molar pregnancy
    • (Optional mention only if needed) Heterotopic: ectopic with a normal pregnancy
  3. Bleeding from Other Sources
    • Cervical causes (e.g., cervical cancer, cervical polyp)
    • Infections (pelvic inflammatory disease)
    • Vaginal lacerations / vaginal trauma
  4. Implantation Bleeding
    • Diagnosis of exclusion, “at the bottom of your list.”

C. Reassurance About the Process

  • History taking is straightforward and structured.
  • Examination is simple if structure is followed.
  • Main challenge is diagnostic formulation, not examination.

D. Communication Style Matching the Transcript

  • Explain findings in the same practical, step-by-step style.
  • Use phrases mirroring the lecturer (e.g., “push it forward,” “tell me,” “this is the key finding”).

Section 3 — Explanatory Notes (Based Solely on Transcript Content)

General OSCE Structure the Lecturer Uses

  • Always maintain a clear, repeated sequence.
  • Begin with general appearance → vitals → abdomen → pelvic.
  • Emphasis on asking specific questions; examiner won’t give findings unless asked.

OSCE-Style Phrases Highlighted

  • “Examiner, I want a pelvic exam with the presence of a chaperone.”
  • “Tell me the os. Is my os open or closed?”
  • “Do I have any tenderness?”
  • “Do I have any masses?”
  • “Tell me the size… tell me the border… is it tender… do I feel any contractions?”
  • “Do I have any adnexal masses or tenderness?”

Key Teaching Points from the Video

  • Examiner will NOT volunteer findings — must explicitly ask.
  • In early pregnancy: transvaginal ultrasound preferred.
  • Serial beta-HCG used to assess normal rise, not just positive/negative.
  • Most challenging part is top diagnosis formulation, not the exam steps.

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: clinical-documentation-expert (Australian clinical supervisor, O&G documentation standards) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The note states cardiac activity 'requires beta-HCG >=10,000'. Detectability of fetal cardiac activity is determined by gestational age / crown-rump length (visible from ~6 weeks or CRL >=7 mm on TVS), not by a fixed hCG threshold. Tying cardiac activity to an hCG of 10,000 is inaccurate.
Frame cardiac activity by ultrasound landmarks: expected once CRL >=7 mm (approx 6-6.5 weeks). Per ASUM/RCOG non-viability criteria, a CRL >=7 mm with no cardiac activity, or a mean gestational sac diameter >=25 mm with no embryo, indicates a non-viable pregnancy (confirm on a second scan / second sonologist).
✏️ The 'discriminatory zone' concept is implied loosely (sac visible at 5 weeks). No numeric hCG discriminatory value is given, which is a key AMC reasoning point when the uterus looks empty.
Add the Australian discriminatory zone: an intrauterine gestational sac should normally be visible on transvaginal ultrasound once beta-hCG is approximately 1500-2000 IU/L. Below this, an empty uterus may simply be too early; above it, consider ectopic or failed pregnancy.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Specify anti-D dosing per National Blood Authority / RANZCOG: for a sensitising event before 12 weeks give Rh(D) immunoglobulin 250 IU, and after 12 weeks 625 IU, within 72 hours, for Rh(D)-negative women (the note lists indications but omits doses and the 72-hour window).
⭐ Add a haemodynamic-instability / cervical shock action step: if a woman with tissue in the os is bradycardic and hypotensive, removal of products from the os (which the note correctly emphasises) plus IV access, fluids and 000/emergency escalation should be documented as the immediate priority.
⭐ Add explicit red flags for triage: shoulder-tip pain, syncope, heavy soaking bleeding, and unilateral pelvic pain (ectopic must-not-miss), and note that a negative urine pregnancy test does NOT exclude ectopic.
⭐ Add metadata linking to eTG (Therapeutic Guidelines) 'bleeding in early pregnancy' and RANZCOG 'Management of Early Pregnancy Complications / Miscarriage' for referencing.
