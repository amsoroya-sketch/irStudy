Abdominal Pain in Pregnancy - Multiple Case Variants

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — obstetrics / class 5 (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: obstetrics_gynaecology

Abdominal Pain in Pregnancy

PRACTICE & MULTIPLE CASE VERSIONS (30-second practice reminders)

  • Practice task: write a prioritized list of differentials (grouped).
  • Emphasis: include the three Ps (preeclampsia, placental abruption, preterm labor) in your priority list; do not dump many GI differentials without grouping.

1. CASE VARIANTS & KEY POINTS

Case Variant A – Pyelonephritis (upper UTI)

Your next patient is a 24 year old lady who is X (>20 weeks) pregnant and has presented to your general practice complaining of worsening abdominal pain.

Task:

  • Take history from the patient for 6 minutes
  • PEFE/Physical examination will be given to you on a card
  • Explain your diagnosis and differentials

History (stem):

  • 20 weeks pregnant
  • Pain in right side of stomach, drags to right lower side
  • 2 days, on-and-off or constant, getting worse
  • No nausea/vomiting reported
  • Fever: yes
  • KUB: pain on passing urine, frequency

Diagnosis:

  • Pyelonephritis (upper UTI) — systemic features + UTI symptoms → pyelonephritis rather than cystitis
  • Cystitis doesn’t give that much systemic symptoms

PE findings (card):

  • BP 110/70 good
  • Temp 39°C
  • Fundal height / FHR / lie okay
  • Renal angle tenderness positive
  • Urine dipstick: blood +, protein +, nitrites +

Teaching points:

  • Nitrites confirm urinary infection → do not change diagnosis to preeclampsia solely because of protein, if BP is normal and neuro exam is okay.
  • Blood can occur with infection.
  • Always ask for renal angle tenderness in structure.

Diagnosis (Explanation to Patient)

  • “Most likely you have a condition called pyelonephritis… which is an infection in your kidneys.”
  • Reasons – Symptoms
    • “You have pain.”
    • “The pain characteristic seems to be from your kidney because it's on the right side and dragging down.”
    • “You have dysuria.”
    • “You have pain.”
    • “You have frequency.”
    • “You have fever.”
    • “If you have nausea or vomiting, good. If you don't, I don't care.”
  • Reasons – Signs
    • “Your urine is showing signs of infection because it has nitrates in it.”
    • “When I tapped on your kidneys… it was painful and tender.”
    • “Because of your renal angle tenderness, I am thinking about pyelonephritis.”


Management (bullet points):

  • Admit to hospital / transfer to ED — do IV antibiotics (do not treat with oral antibiotics in pregnancy).
  • Take urine samples for MCS before antibiotics (culture & sensitivity).
  • Give analgesia and antipyretics safe for pregnancy.
  • Give IV fluids as needed.
  • Monitor vitals and baby (CTG where appropriate).
  • Do not attempt IV/complete management in GP — needs hospital resources (ESR/CRP, FBC, CTG, ongoing IV therapy).
  • Post-treatment: do a post-treatment MCS (follow-up).

Case Variant B – Constipation with Travel-related presentations (mini travel history added)

Your next patient is a 30 year old lady who is 28 weeks pregnant. She is complaining of abdominal pain while travelling.

Task:

  • Take history from the patient for 6 minutes
  • PEFE/Physical examination will be given to you on a card
  • Explain your diagnosis and differentials

History Taking Structure

  1. Vitals and Hemodynamic Stability
  2. Open-Ended Question / Explore the Concern
    • “How can I help you today?”
    • She will say she has pain while traveling and is worried.
  3. Address the Concern
    • “I Understand that………..”.
  4. Explore the Pain
    • Explore the pain with SIQORAA.
  5. General GI Questions
    • A good structure includes general GI questions.
  6. OB Questions
    • Bleeding
    • Discharges
    • The baby kicking
    • Preeclampsia
    • Routine OB history (“Tell me about your OB history,” all usual steps)
  7. GI Group
  8. KUB Group
  9. Travel History (Mini Travel History)
    • Added at the end of the list.
    • The main objective is to rule out differentials, so travel history is kept short.
    • Mini travel history components:
      • Where did you travel to?
      • How long
      • Activities
      • Bushwalkings and insect bites
      • Street food
      • Tattooing or piercing
      • Contact with sick people
      • Seeing GP before traveling for vaccination
    • Sex question skipped.
    • Better to run out of time here than run out of time for differentials.

Case Findings

  • First pregnancy
  • Abdominal pain for one week
  • Initially “all over my stomach”
  • Later radiates and drags down to the left lower side
  • On and off
  • Constipation
  • Anemia and taking iron tablets

Physical Examination Card

  • Blood pressure normal
  • Abdominal examination normal
  • Leopolds: Breech position (doesn’t matter)
  • Urine dipstick normal

Diagnosis

  • Most likely cause of the pain is constipation.
  • Also consider side effects of iron tablets (they cause constipation and tummy upset/gastritis).

Differentials

  • The usual list of differentials plus travel-related differentials.
  • Travel-related differentials mentioned:
    • Hepatitis (Hepatitis A)
    • Malaria
    • Blood-borne viruses
    • Tick-borne viruses like Ross River fever
    • Dengue fever
    • Depends on where the travel has been
  • Stick to the same list of differentials from different groups, definitely having your three Ps in it.
  • GI differentials
  • KUB differentials
  • Plus, as she was traveling, concerns about:
    • Infections in the liver
    • Food-borne disorders like Hepatitis A
    • Mosquito-borne diseases like malaria
    • Tick-borne diseases such as dengue fever, Ross River fever

Case Variant C – Constipation and bowel obstruction

Your next patient is a 30 year old lady who is 20 weeks pregnant. She is complaining of abdominal pain.

Task:

  • Take history from the patient for 6 minutes
  • PEFE/Physical examination will be given to you on a card
  • Explain your diagnosis and differentials

Findings from History

  • “I was on holidays and I started having this pain.”
  • Pain is on and off
  • Radiates to the left side
  • Loss of appetite
  • Last bowel motion one week ago - Constipation
  • Travel history: “I’ve been eating street food.”
  • Frequency
  • No dysuria
  • No fever
  • Multiple positive findings (“multiple finding dramas”)

Physical Examination Card

  • Distension ++
  • Bowel sounds weakly heard
  • Lower limb edema
  • Blood pressure normal

Diagnosis

  • “The most likely cause of your pain is constipation.”
  • Concern about the possibility of a bowel obstruction
    • “Constipated stools blocking your bowels”
    • Also referred to as fecal impaction / bowel obstruction
  • Diagnosis is a combination:
    • Most likely constipation
    • But concerned about bowel obstruction because bowel sounds are not heard properly

Differentials

  • KUB causes (because of frequency):
    • Urinary tract infection
    • Stones in the kidneys
  • OB differentials:
    • Preeclampsia
    • Preterm labor
    • Placental abruption
  • Other GI conditions:
    • Hernias (can cause obstruction)
    • Acute abdomens like:
      • Diverticulitis
      • Cholecystitis
      • “And so on”
  • Travel-related problems

Case Variant D – Preterm labour and Placental abruption (concealed type)

Your next patient is a 34 weeks pregnant lady who has presented to the emergency department complaining of generalized abdominal pain.

Task:

  • Take history from the patient for 6 minutes
  • PEFE/Physical examination will be given to you on a card
  • Explain your diagnosis and differentials

Key History Finding

  • First pregnancy
  • Everything has been normal so far
  • Vaginal bleeding

Instruction:

  • Even if vaginal bleeding is found early, still stick to the full history structure for the six minutes.
  • Do OB history, preeclampsia question, and still rule out other differentials.

Initial Diagnostic Thinking

  • Either:
    • Preterm labor, or
    • Placental abruption
  • Depends on physical examination.

Physical Examination Findings

  • Contractions every 5 minutes
  • Fetal heart rate normal
  • Speculum: cervical os dilated 3 cm

Diagnosis

  • Preterm labor

Explanation to patient:

  • “The labor has started.”
  • Reasoning:
    • Pain
    • Bleeding
    • Opening of the womb has started to open for delivery
  • “Once this happens, there is no going back.”

Differentials

  • The rest of the Ps:
    • Placental abruption
    • Preeclampsia
  • GI causes
  • KUB differentials
  • Liver and gallbladder

AMC Note

  • Sometimes the stem does not mention pregnancy.
  • You take a full abdominal pain history, and only at the end she says her last period was 34 weeks ago.
  • AMC has done this a few times.
  • It “ruins the structure,” but:
    • You still need a good approach to abdominal pain.
    • Big group of pelvic conditions must be ruled out.
    • Always ask LMP, even in acute abdomen.

Your next patient is a 34 weeks pregnant lady who has presented to the emergency department complaining of generalized abdominal pain.

Task:

  • Take history from the patient for 6 minutes
  • PEFE/Physical examination will be given to you on a card
  • Explain your diagnosis and differentials

Presentation

  • Abdominal pain
  • Started acutely 4 hours ago
  • Constant
  • Severe (9/10)
  • No vaginal bleeding
  • No fluid leakage
  • No preeclampsia features

Physical Examination

  • Fundal height 39 cm (patient was 36 weeks)
  • Uterus tender
  • No contractions
  • Rest of examination normal
  • No vaginal bleeding

Diagnosis

  • Placental abruption

Key Teaching Point

  • “I thought abruption always gives bleeding.”
    • Not always.
    • There are concealed versions where bleeding is trapped behind the placenta.

Reasoning for Diagnosis

  1. Uterus larger than expected
  2. Uterus tender on palpation
    • No contractions
    • The tenderness represents the abruption
  3. Bleeding is concealed (trapped behind placenta)

Explanation to Patient

  • The placenta is the bridge/tissue that connects the baby to the womb.
  • It has detached / disconnected from the womb.
  • This causes pain.
  • Size of womb increased because bleeding is trapped.
  • Womb is tender because of the abruption.
  • Normal blood pressure does not rule out placental abruption.

Cluster Summary

  • These cases wrap up the group of abdominal pains.
  • Main message:
    • You don’t know which case you’re getting.
    • The only way to pass is having a good structure.
    • At least 90% of the structure must be asked in six minutes.
    • Even with travel history, you still must step into travel history.
    • Purpose of the case is differentials, not diagnosis.

Clusters of Differentials

A. OB Differentials

  • “Three Ps” to rule out
  • Preterm labor
  • Placental abruption
  • Preeclampsia
  • Optional additions:
    • Uterine rupture (prior C-section)
    • Chorioamnionitis
    • PID (rare)

B. GI Differentials

  • Acute abdomen Appendicitis
  • Bowel obstruction
  • Hernias
  • Constipation (due to progesterone in pregnancy)
  • Upper GI
    • Peptic ulcer disease
    • Gastroesophageal reflux

C. Liver & Gallbladder

  • Acute fatty liver of pregnancy
  • Cholecystitis

D. KUB Differentials

  • Pyelonephritis
  • Cystitis / UTIs
  • Renal colic

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: clinical-documentation-expert (Australian teaching hospital clinical supervisor) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The travel variant classifies Ross River virus and dengue as 'tick-borne' diseases.
Both Ross River virus and dengue are MOSQUITO-borne, not tick-borne. In the Australian context, Ross River virus (and Barmah Forest virus) and dengue (northern Queensland/returned travellers) are spread by mosquitoes. Correct the vector, and if a genuine tick exposure exists consider tick-borne conditions separately.
✏️ In the preterm-labour and abruption variants there is vaginal bleeding, yet a speculum vaginal examination is performed without addressing placenta praevia.
In any woman with antepartum haemorrhage, digital vaginal examination is contraindicated and even speculum examination should be deferred until placenta praevia is excluded on ultrasound (risk of provoking catastrophic haemorrhage). Make this safety caveat explicit before any vaginal assessment.
✏️ The pyelonephritis explanation says the urine has 'nitrates' and implies nitrite positivity is required to confirm UTI.
The dipstick detects NITRITES (not nitrates), produced by gram-negative bacteria reducing urinary nitrate. Importantly, a negative nitrite result does NOT exclude UTI (some organisms are nitrite-negative and urine must dwell in the bladder). Send an MSU for microscopy, culture and sensitivity to confirm.
✏️ American spellings/terms throughout ('labor', 'hemodynamic', 'anemia', 'fecal', 'gastroesophageal', 'edema', 'generalized').
Australian documentation requires labour, haemodynamic, anaemia, faecal, gastro-oesophageal, oedema, generalised. Standardise for AHPRA-compliant notes.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add ovarian cyst complications (torsion, haemorrhage, rupture) as a pelvic/adnexal differential for abdominal pain in pregnancy - currently omitted and supported by the RAG source; torsion gives severe cramping lower abdominal pain radiating to flank/back with vomiting.
⭐ Add placental abruption emergency management: 000/resuscitation, two large-bore IV cannulae, group and crossmatch, coagulation studies (DIC risk), continuous CTG, urgent senior obstetric review and plan for delivery - and anti-D immunoglobulin for any RhD-negative woman with an antepartum haemorrhage (Australian guideline). Emphasise a concealed abruption can cause major hidden blood loss and shock despite normal BP.
⭐ Add preterm-labour management: antenatal corticosteroids (betamethasone) if birth anticipated before ~34-35 weeks, magnesium sulfate for fetal neuroprotection if before 30 weeks, intrapartum GBS prophylaxis, tocolysis to buy time for steroids/transfer, and in-utero transfer to a centre with neonatal intensive care.
⭐ Add specific Australian antimicrobial guidance for pyelonephritis in pregnancy (eTG): IV therapy (e.g. ampicillin plus gentamicin, or ceftriaxone), switch to oral per sensitivities; note nitrofurantoin is avoided near term (neonatal haemolysis) and trimethoprim in the first trimester (folate antagonist); asymptomatic bacteriuria in pregnancy is screened for and treated.
⭐ Reinforce the AMC teaching to always ask LMP/exclude pregnancy in any woman with abdominal pain (ectopic pregnancy is a not-to-be-missed diagnosis when the pregnancy is early or undeclared).
