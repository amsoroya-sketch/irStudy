Routine Antenatal Counselling (First Trimester, Irregular Periods)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — obstetrics / class 1 (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: obstetrics_gynaecology

ROUTINE ANTENATAL COUNSELLING

Your next patient is a 24 year old lady who has presented to you for further advice. She has had a positive home pregnancy test. She is a known case of irregular periods.

Task:

• Take history

• Explain investigations you are going to do

• Give antenatal advice to the patient

Antenatal History-Taking Steps (First Trimester / Early Pregnancy)

1. Opening the Station

Step / Action

  • Sanitize hands, greet the patient, introduce yourself, confirm identity, sit.

Purpose

  • Establish rapport and meet communication-skills criteria early.

Examiner Tips / Insights

  • Approach and communication appear to be weighted more strongly in recent exams.
  • Examiner expects a warm, professional introduction before moving into history.

2. Open-Ended Question

Step / Action

  • Start with an open-ended question, even if the patient already volunteered information.

Examples

  • “How can I help you today?”
  • If patient already talked: “Thank you for that information—can you tell me a little more about that?”

Purpose

  • Allows the patient to provide the initial narrative.
  • Demonstrates good communication technique.
  • Ensures you're not missing key parts the patient wants to add.

Common Mistake

  • Jumping directly to closed questions because the patient started talking first.

3. Address Patient’s Concern & Planned Pregnancy Question

Step / Action

  • Clarify pregnancy intention and respond empathetically.

Key Question

  • “Was this a planned pregnancy?”

Purpose

  • Helps assess psychosocial context and readiness.
  • If planned and welcomed, acknowledge positively.

Communication Requirement

  • Offer congratulations and explore feelings:
    • “Congratulations—that’s lovely news. How are you feeling?”

Tip

  • Examiner looks for a positive emotional response because this is a good-news scenario.

4. Obtain Permission to Ask Questions

Step / Action

  • “Is it okay if I ask you a few questions?”

Purpose

  • Patient-centred communication.
  • Smooth transition into structured history-taking.

5. The “5 Ps” Structure for Early Pregnancy History

(Used ONLY in first trimester cases <14 weeks, different structure for 2nd and 3rd trimester)

5.1 First P – Period History (MOST IMPORTANT IN THIS CASE)

Key Questions

  • “When was your last menstrual period?” (Exam-critical)
  • “Are your periods regular or irregular?”
  • “How often do you get them?” (Cycle length / frequency)
  • Assess pregnancy symptoms:
    • “Do you feel more tired than usual?”
    • “Any nausea or vomiting? Morning sickness?”

Purpose

  • Establish gestational age.
  • Identify need for dating scan (critical in irregular cycles).
  • Assess severity of early pregnancy symptoms (hyperemesis).

Examiner Insights

  • With irregular cycles, you must mention the need for a dating scan or risk failing.
  • Breast tenderness is not essential—tiredness + nausea/vomiting are key.

5.2 Second P – Partner History

Key Questions & Their Purpose

Relationship status

  • “Are you in a stable relationship?”
    → Determines support and involvement.

Support system

  • “Do you have good support from your partner?”
    → Early psychosocial assessment.

Domestic violence screening

  • “How are things going at home?”
    → Subtle screening without confronting the patient.

STI risk

  • “Have you ever been diagnosed with any sexually transmitted infections?”
    → STI history influences antenatal screening.

Notes

  • Keep questions limited due to time—avoid detailed sexual history.

5.3 Third P – Pills (Contraception)

Exam Tip

  • Usually skipped because patient is already pregnant.
  • Only relevant if:
    • Recent IUD use
    • Emergency contraception failure
    • Known teratogenic medication exposure

5.4 Fourth P – Pregnancy History (Obstetric History)

Key Questions

  • “Is this your first pregnancy?”
  • “Any previous pregnancies?”

If previous pregnancies:

  • Antenatal complications:
    • “Did you have any issues like high blood pressure or high blood sugar?”
  • Delivery history:
    • “How did you deliver—vaginal birth or caesarean section?”
    • “Any complications during delivery?” (obstructed labour, PPH, etc.)

Purpose

  • Identify obstetric risk factors (GDM, pre-eclampsia, prior CS).
  • Helps plan management and risk stratification.

Common Mistake

  • Asking “Do you have any other children?” → misses miscarriages/terminations.

5.5 Fifth P – Pap smear / Cervical Screening

Key Questions

  • For age ≥25:
    • “When was your last cervical screening test?”
    • “What was the result?”

Purpose

  • Ensures routine screening is current during pregnancy.

6. Past Medical History

General Question

  • “Do you have any long-term medical conditions such as asthma, diabetes, hypertension?”

Specific Conditions to Ask About (Examiner wants these explicitly):

  1. Thyroid disease
  2. Heart disease
    • Even mild valvular disease (mitral stenosis) becomes relevant in pregnancy.
  3. Diabetes (pre-existing DM)

Purpose

  • Identifies conditions requiring specialist input or modified antenatal care.

Insight

  • Patients may think a condition is minor (“subclinical,” “mild”)—still clinically significant.

7. Medication & Supplement History

Key Questions

  • “Are you taking any medications?”
  • “Any supplements or over-the-counter medicines?”

Purpose

  • Identify teratogens.
  • Identify whether patient already takes folic acid.

8. Family History

Key Question

  • “Is there any family history of medical conditions or genetic disorders?”

Purpose

  • Determine inherited risks requiring additional testing (anomalies, genetic counselling).

9. Lifestyle History

Priority Questions (time-efficient)

  • “Do you drink alcohol?”
  • “Do you smoke?”
  • “Do you use any recreational drugs?”

Additional

  • “Do you drink coffee?” (caffeine intake)
  • “Do you exercise? How much?”

Purpose

  • Identifies modifiable teratogenic exposures.
  • Important for antenatal health promotion.

Transcript Insight

  • The case includes a positive alcohol history → must address in counselling phase.

10. Psychosocial Screening

Key Questions

  • “How has your mood been?”
  • “How are you coping with the pregnancy?”

Purpose

  • Screen for anxiety/depression.
  • Gauge emotional response to pregnancy.

Insight

  • Initial “How are you feeling?” early in the encounter helps cover part of this domain.

Critical Interpretation of Findings

Key Takeaway

  • Irregular periods + unknown LMP = MUST recommend a dating scan
     (Failure to mention this risks failing the station.)

Common Examiner Insights & Mistakes

Must-Do

  • Warm communication: congratulate patient, express positivity.
  • Always start with open-ended question.
  • Use structured 5 Ps for early pregnancy (<14 weeks).
  • Ask about nausea/vomiting (hyperemesis relevance).
  • Explore support & domestic violence subtly.
  • Ask specifically about thyroid, heart disease, and diabetes.
  • Clarify STI history.
  • Confirm cervical screening status (if ≥25).

Avoid

  • Skipping open-ended question because patient started talking.
  • Asking “Do you have children?” instead of “Previous pregnancies?”
  • Spending too long on sexual history (time-waster).
  • Forgetting psychosocial components.
  • Ignoring supplements and OTCs.
  • Omitting detailed assessment of past obstetric complications.

Summary of Key Positive Findings

  • Positive pregnancy test
  • Irregular periods
  • LMP unknown
  • Cycles spaced months apart
  • Planned, welcomed pregnancy
  • Drinks alcohol
  • No domestic violence
  • First pregnancy
  • Cervical screening not yet due (24 years old)

How to Explain Antenatal Investigations to a Patient

Opening Principle

“we r going to arrange for routine antenatal screening

Group 1 – Confirming the Pregnancy

How to explain:

  • “Your urine pregnancy test is positive. Lets confirm your pregnancy.
  • We can repeat a urine test today in the clinic.”
  • “We can also do a blood test called serum β-hCG to confirm the pregnancy.”
    • (Optional in this OSCE – do not overemphasize.)

Group 2 – Dating the Pregnancy (Key Point)

Most important early investigation.

How to explain:

  • “Because your periods are irregular and we don’t know exactly when the pregnancy started, I’d like to arrange an ultrasound scan called a dating scan.”
  • “This will help us get a better estimate of the age of pregnancy, so we can plan your care accurately.”

Group 3 – Routine Blood Tests

Explain as “we want to do regular bloods we do for all pregnant women.”

Include:

  1. Full Blood Examination
    • “Checks for anaemia—meaning low iron or thin blood.”
  2. Blood Group & Rhesus Type
    • “We need to know your blood group and whether you are Rhesus positive or negative.”
  3. Antibody Screen (Indirect Coombs Test)
    • “Checks if you have any antibodies that might affect the baby.”

Group 4 – Immunity Screening

Explain as “I need to check your immunity to certain infections.”

  1. Rubella serology
    • “To see if you are immune to rubella.”
  2. Varicella (if no past chickenpox/shingles)
    • “If you’ve never had chickenpox or shingles, we’ll also check your immunity to varicella.”

Group 5 – Sexually Transmitted Infection (STI) Screening

Mandatory: obtain consent.

How to explain:

  • “As part of routine antenatal care, we can check for some STIs.
    If you’re happy and give consent, we can screen for:”
    • Syphilis
    • HIV
    • Hepatitis B ± C
    • Chlamydia (especially for younger patients)

Explain method:

  • “These are mainly blood tests.
    For chlamydia, we use a urine test or a self-collected vaginal swab—no internal examination needed during pregnancy.”

Ask explicitly:

  • “Would you be happy for us to do these tests today?”

Group 6 – Urine Tests

Explain clearly:

  • “I’ll need a midstream urine sample—let the first part of urine go, collect the middle part.”
  • “We will send it for culture and check for infection, and also for protein, which can signal kidney issues in pregnancy.”

Additional Tests (When Relevant)

1. Cervical Screening

  • “If your cervical screening is due now or next few months, we can safely do it in the first trimester.”

2. Thyroid Function (TSH)

  • “we can do thyroid tests Because thyroid issues can affect pregnancy, if you have a history of thyroid problems—or a family history—we can check a TSH level.”

Genetic / Down Syndrome Screening (Offer)

How to offer:

  • “We can also arrange tests to check for genetic conditions such as Down syndrome.
    Would you like to hear more about these options?”

(Explanation of options occurs in the next case, so keep this brief.)

Final Clinical Assessments

Include as part of completing work-up:

  • “Today I’ll also check your blood pressure, measure your height and weight, and calculate your BMI.”

(While not strictly “investigations,” they are essential early-pregnancy assessments.)

End of Explanation

  • Summarise briefly.
  • Ask: “Does this all make sense?”
  • Check comfort: “Do you have any questions or concerns about any of these tests?”

ANTENATAL COUNSELLING

1. Precautions / Avoidances (FIRST PRIORITY)

Open phrase:
“Before we go into details, the most important things are what to avoid to keep you and baby safe.”

Substances

  • No smoking.
  • No alcohol at all (“No amount is safe in pregnancy”).
  • No recreational drugs.

Medications

  • “If anyone prescribes you medication—even at the pharmacy—please tell them you’re pregnant.”
  • Avoid teratogenic medications; check with GP/pharmacist first.
  • Include OTC medications.

Imaging / Radiation

  • Avoid X-rays and CT scans unless essential.
  • “Always tell the radiographer or doctor you’re pregnant before any imaging.”

Key safety-net phrase:

“Any time you see a doctor, pharmacist, or have a scan, please tell them immediately that you’re pregnant so we can avoid anything harmful to the baby.”

2. Supplementation

Open phrase:
“There are two supplements recommended for all pregnant women.”

Folic Acid

  • Low-dose / regular dose (0.4 mg)
  • Take for first 3 months of pregnancy. (and 1 month before conception if patient came pre-conception)

Iodine

  • Daily iodine supplement (exact dose not required).

3. Vaccinations

Open phrase:
“There are three vaccines recommended to protect you and the baby.”

Influenza vaccine

  • Safe any time in pregnancy.
  • Given around flu season.

Third-trimester vaccines:

  1. Whooping cough (Boostrix / pertussis)
  2. RSV vaccine

4. Lifestyle Advice

Open phrase:
“Next, let’s talk about day-to-day lifestyle to keep you healthy.”

Diet — Healthy Balanced Diet + Avoidances

Avoid:

  • Raw or undercooked meat
  • Pre-packed salads / vegetables
  • Soft cheeses
  • Excessive fish / seafood

Coffee / Caffeine

  • Limit to 1–2 cups per day.

Exercise

  • Moderate-intensity exercise is safe.

5. Model of Care / Follow-up Arrangement

Open phrase:
“Let me explain how your pregnancy care will be organised.”

  • I will Refer u to pregnancy clinic.
  • Taken Care by a team of midwives + obstetricians.
  • Options:
    • Public hospital
    • Private obstetrician
    • GP shared care

6. Routine Antenatal Screening Schedule

Open phrase:
“These are the key tests we routinely do throughout pregnancy.”

  • 20 weeks: morphology scan
  • 26–28 weeks: “sweet drink test” / GDM screening
  • 36 weeks: GBS swab test + plan for delivery

7. Closing / Safety-Netting

  • “If you have bleeding, severe pain, decreased movements, fever, or anything that concerns you, please seek urgent care.”
  • “At every visit, we’ll continue to check on both you and the baby.”
  • “Any doubts about medication, food, or tests—just ask. We’re here to support you.”

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: clinical-documentation-expert (Australian clinical supervisor, O&G/antenatal standards) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ Vaccination section places both third-trimester vaccines (whooping cough and RSV) without gestational windows, implying pertussis is a late third-trimester vaccine.
Document the correct windows: dTpa (pertussis/Boostrix) is recommended each pregnancy at 20-32 weeks (optimal), RSV vaccine (Abrysvo) at 28-36 weeks, and influenza at any gestation. Under the National Immunisation Program these are funded for pregnant women. Giving pertussis only in late third trimester reduces transplacental antibody transfer to the newborn.
✏️ Folic acid is given as '0.4 mg' and iodine dose omitted; no high-dose (5 mg) indications are mentioned in this counselling script.
State folic acid 0.5 mg (400-500 microgram) daily and iodine 150 microgram daily. Even in a low-risk 24-year-old, best practice is to screen for the 5 mg high-dose indications (pre-existing diabetes, BMI over 30, previous NTD/family history, anticonvulsants) before defaulting to standard dose.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ For irregular periods with unknown LMP, explicitly document that the dating ultrasound is most accurate in the first trimester (crown-rump length up to ~13+6 weeks) - this operationalises the 'must mention dating scan' teaching point and gives a defensible timeframe.
⭐ Add a first-trimester GDM risk stratification note: this patient's screen at 26-28 weeks (75 g OGTT) is correct for average risk, but list the early-testing indications (previous GDM, BMI over 30, prior macrosomia, strong family history, high-risk ethnicity) so learners know when to bring screening forward per ADIPS.
⭐ Strengthen safety-netting into a numbered red-flag list with the emergency number 000: heavy PV bleeding, severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting with inability to keep fluids down (hyperemesis), fever, and severe headache/visual changes.
⭐ Cite Australian sources within the card: RANZCOG routine antenatal assessment guidance and the KEMH Antenatal Shared Care Guidelines for GPs (the latter directly supports the shared-care model-of-care counselling in section 5).
