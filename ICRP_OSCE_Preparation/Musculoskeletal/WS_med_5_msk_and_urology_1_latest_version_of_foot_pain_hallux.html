I. Closure

Physical Examination Trap in This Case

The recall said:

“Swelling of the first metatarsophalangeal joint.”

That is the base of the big toe.

the joint is:

A. Red, warm, tender, painful to move

Findings:

  • Swelling
  • Red
  • Warm
  • Tender
  • Painful movements

Diagnosis:

“This looks like gout.”

If patient has:

  • Alcohol
  • Red meat

→ Gout diagnosis

B. Swelling + Restricted movements

People recalled:

  • Flexion restricted
  • Extension restricted

This is:

Hallux Rigidus

Which is:

Osteoarthritis of the first MTP joint

Features:

  • Swelling
  • No redness
  • No warmth
  • Usually not tender
  • Pain on movement
  • Restricted movement

Explanation:

“You have osteoarthritis in the joint. It is a wear-and-tear problem causing pain and stiffness.”

C. Swelling / deformity + normal movement

Findings:

  • Swelling or deformity
  • No redness
  • No warmth
  • No tenderness
  • Normal range of movement

Diagnosis:

Hallux Valgus

Explanation:

“The base of the big toe is deformed. This causes pressure when wearing shoes, which causes pain.”

Diagnosis

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: surgical-skills-expert (Australian surgical educator, RACS/MSK focus) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The case implies acute red, hot, swollen first MTP joint plus a diet history (alcohol/red meat) is sufficient to diagnose gout ('If patient has alcohol, red meat -> Gout diagnosis').
An acute hot swollen monoarthritis is septic arthritis until proven otherwise. In Australian practice you must arrange urgent joint aspiration for Gram stain, culture and polarised-light microscopy before attributing it to gout; diet is a risk factor, not a diagnostic criterion. Definitive gout diagnosis = negatively birefringent monosodium urate crystals.
✏️ Serum uric acid is implied to confirm gout.
Serum urate is frequently normal or low during an acute flare and a normal level does not exclude gout (eTG). It is most useful once the acute episode settles. Diagnosis rests on synovial fluid crystal analysis.
✏️ No analgesic/anti-inflammatory management is offered beyond 'Would you like some painkillers?'
Per eTG (Australia), acute gout is treated with an NSAID (e.g. indomethacin or naproxen) OR low-dose colchicine OR a short course of oral prednisolone, chosen by comorbidity/renal function. Use paracetamol as adjunct. This should be surfaced in the model answer.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add an explicit investigations block for acute first-MTP monoarthritis: joint aspiration (MCS + polarised microscopy), FBE/CRP/ESR, serum urate (deferred), UEC/eGFR (guides NSAID/colchicine dosing), and weight-bearing foot X-ray (periarticular erosions/OA/hallux valgus angle).
⭐ Add hallux rigidus vs hallux valgus differentiation cue for the diagnosis/management task: hallux rigidus = dorsal osteophyte with painful/restricted dorsiflexion (grind test positive); hallux valgus = lateral deviation of great toe with medial 'bunion' prominence, often painless ROM. Include first-line conservative management (footwear/orthotics, podiatry referral) and surgical referral thresholds.
⭐ Add missed red flags to the history template: recent trauma/stress fracture, immunosuppression/diabetes (septic arthritis risk), and tophi/prior flares. Tag the case with the correct AMC station framing (history 6 min then interpret provided PEFE findings then give Dx/DDx).
