Traumatic Hip Pain (Proximal Hamstring / Ischial Injury)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med 5 MSK and urology / 2. MSK UL LL (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: musculoskeletal

OSCE – Traumatic Hip Pain (Walking Difficulty After Trauma)

(Exact lecture framework and flow as per transcript)

1. How This Case Appears in the Exam

Stem given

  • 32-year-old lady
  • Presents with walking difficulty

“Apparently the stem is a walking difficulty… very early in your open-ended question… the patient was playing soccer, was tackled, and now she has the pain and everything.”

So although the stem says walking difficulty, it quickly becomes a traumatic hip pain case.

2. Examiner’s Tasks

  • History taking – 6 minutes
  • Confusing physical examination findings given
  • Tell diagnosis and differentials

3. What the Patient Tells You Early

Open-ended answer

“Yeah doctor, I was playing soccer, someone tackled me, and since then I have pain in my hip and I am not able to walk.”

Other key spontaneous history:

  • Heard a popping sound
  • Pain behind the thigh radiating down
  • Not able to bear weight

So this becomes a traumatic lower limb / traumatic hip pain case.

4. The Core Concept of All Traumatic Cases

“In physical examination cases… traumatic case, you’re always looking for ruling out injury to:

  • neurological system
  • musculoskeletal system
  • vascular system”

But the most important thing:

“I will not pass you if you do not ask… asking a detailed description of the injury.”

Why

How the injury happened tells you what injury to expect.

5. History-Taking Structure

Step 1 – Opening

How can I help u today?

“How can I help you?”

or

“Tell me more about your walking difficulty.”

Patient: tackled while playing soccer…… (as above)

“I was playing soccer, someone tackled me and since then I can’t walk.”

Ask: r u in pain?

“Are you in any pain?”

Patient: Yes

Offer painkillers → already given

Address concern → move on

6. Exploring the Complaint

Timing> when did it exactly happened?

“When did it exactly happen?”

Weight bearing – Critical> were u able to bear weight after injury and now?

“Were you able to bear weight after the injury?”

You are checking:

  • Could they bear weight immediately after
  • Can they bear weight now

7. Pain – Full CICORA

Because patient says yes, I have pain

Ask:

  • Where is the pain? Is pain in hip, knee or ankle?

    Hip vs knee vs ankle

    (because it can be ACL, meniscus, Achilles, hip fracture etc)

Patient: my pain is In hip

“The pain is in my hip.”

Ask:

  • Intensity
  • Quality
  • Onset (already known)
  • Radiation

Patient: radiates behind my thigh

“It goes behind my thigh and down my thigh.”

Ask:

  • What makes it better?
  • What makes it worse?

PATTERN OF PAIN> DO U LIMP? R U ABLE TO BEAR WEIGHT?

“Worse when I try to stand or bear weight.”

8. MOST IMPORTANT PART – Can u Describe the Injury In detail for me?

“Can you describe the injury in detail for me?”

Ask specifically:

  • Was it twisting?
  • Was it a direct blow?
  • Did you jump and land awkwardly?

“Someone tackled me from the right side.

I landed on my left side.

I heard a popping sound.”

9. Rule Out Musculoskeletal Injury

Ask:

  • Do you have any swelling in hip joint?
  • Any redness or bruising?
  • Any deformity?
  • Does one side look different from the other?
  • Any difficulty moving the joint?

“This group of questions is ruling out injuries to your musculoskeletal system.”

10. Rule Out Neurological Injury

Ask:

  • Do you have any back pain?
  • Any weakness in the legs?
  • Any numbness or pins and needles in leg?

11. Rule Out Vascular Injury

Ask:

  • Any color change?
  • Any pallor / paleness in the leg?

12. Physical Examination Findings Given

  • Swelling over the left hip
  • Tenderness on the ischial tuberosity
  • Neurology
    • Tone normal
    • Sensory normal
    • Reflexes normal
    • Power reduced because of pain
  • Decreased range of movement on passive movement
  • No leg length discrepancy

13. Interpreting These Findings

Hip and femur fractures usually:

  • Cause leg length discrepancy
  • Short leg in neck of femur fractures
  • Short leg in dislocations

But here:

  • No leg discrepancy
  • Pain is at ischial tuberosity

So:

“We have to come out of the hip joint.”

14. Most Likely Diagnosis

“The best terminology will be… you have a pubic fracture… in the ischial part.”

So:

Provisional diagnosis

Most likely u have a Fracture in the pelvis – which is a complex bone that connects abdomen to your thighs. The Fx is most likely in a part of the bone that we call ischium

15. How to Explain the Diagnosis to the Patient

“Most likely you have a fracture… a fracture in the pelvic bone or the pelvis.”

Explain pelvis

“A complex bone that connects your abdomen to your legs or your thigh.”

Then:

“The fracture is most likely in a part of the bone called the ischium or the ischial tuberosity.”

16. Differentials (Exactly as Taught)

Bony & Joint

  • Femoral neck fracture
  • Femur shaft fracture
  • Acetabular fracture
  • Hip dislocation
  • Sacral fracture

Joint / Cartilage

  • Torn acetabular labrum

Soft tissue

  • Hamstring tear
  • Muscle tears
  • Tendonitis
  • Tendon sprains or strains

17. Final Message of the Case

“Whenever you do have a traumatic case, rule out:

  • nerve injury
  • vascular injury
  • musculoskeletal injury – muscles, tendons and bones.”

And for this case:

“Fracture in the pelvis is the best terminology.”

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: surgical-skills-expert (Australian surgical educator, FRACS, MSK focus) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The case concludes that the 'best terminology' is a pelvic (ischial ramus) fracture, but the presentation - a 32-year-old tackled at soccer, a popping sound, pain behind the thigh, ischial tuberosity tenderness and NO leg-length discrepancy - is far more typical of a proximal HAMSTRING injury (muscle-tendon tear or ischial tuberosity avulsion) than a pubic ramus fracture.
In a young athlete with a sudden eccentric/tackling load, an audible pop and posterior-thigh pain, the leading diagnosis is a proximal hamstring tear or ischial tuberosity avulsion. Isolated pubic/ischial ramus fractures typically occur with high-energy trauma or in osteoporotic elderly patients. Teach the mechanism-based diagnosis (hamstring origin injury) as most likely, with avulsion fracture and ramus fracture as differentials confirmed on imaging.
✏️ The vascular and neurological 'rule-out' screens are incomplete for a limb-trauma station.
Vascular assessment should include distal pulses (dorsalis pedis/posterior tibial), capillary refill and temperature, not just colour/pallor. Neurological assessment should specifically include the sciatic nerve distribution (foot dorsiflexion/plantarflexion, sensation, and screening for foot drop), given the ischial/posterior-thigh location.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add the imaging and management plan: plain pelvis/hip X-ray first-line (excludes ramus/avulsion fracture and femoral neck fracture); MRI is the investigation of choice to grade a proximal hamstring tear/avulsion. Management = RICE, analgesia (paracetamol first-line), crutches with protected weight-bearing, and orthopaedic/sports referral - complete or high-grade proximal avulsions may need surgical repair.
⭐ Reinforce the excellent trauma-safety teaching but make it a formal ABCDE/primary-survey prompt and add distraction-injury screening: in any tackled patient also consider associated hip dislocation and femoral neck fracture (which WOULD shorten/externally rotate the leg) and pelvic-ring injury (mechanically unstable, haemorrhage risk) - escalate/000 if haemodynamically unstable.
⭐ Clarify the internally inconsistent laterality in the transcript ('tackled from the right, landed on left, swelling over the left hip') so learners map the mechanism to the injured side, and interpret 'power reduced because of pain' as an unreliable motor score rather than true weakness.
⭐ Add related-topic links to traumatic and chronic knee cases so the 'rule out neuro/vascular/MSK + describe the mechanism' framework is reinforced across limb-trauma stations.
