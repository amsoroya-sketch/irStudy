Bilateral Knee Osteoarthritis - Management Counselling

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med 5 MSK and urology / other MSK Cases (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: musculoskeletal

Knee Osteoarthritis

Case Stem

Your next patient is a 60 year old man who has presented to you complaining of knee pain for 6 months. His pain is constant and worse at the end of the day. His knee pain is bilateral and worse on the left side. He has no history of injuries.

  • He is a known case of hypertension and is on perindopril.

On PE:

  • You find crepitations on both sides, painful active and passive movements on both sides
  • BMI 32

Task

  • Explain your diagnosis to the patient v
  • Explain your management plan including pharmacological and non pharmacological treatment to the patient.
  • Side effects

Diagnosis

  • Osteoarthritis of both knees

Symptoms of Osteoarthritis (Explained to Patient)

  • Pain
  • Stiffness
  • Swelling
  • Long-term impact on function

Risk Factors Discussed

Modifiable Risk Factors

  • Obesity
  • Previous injury (post-traumatic osteoarthritis)

Other Risk Factors

  • Joint malalignment
    • Genu varum
    • Genu valgum

      → Causes abnormal pressure on the joint
  • Certain occupations:
    • Prolonged standing
    • Heavy lifting
    • Repetitive movements

Investigation – Do We Need an X-ray?

OSCE / Australian Guideline Approach

  • Preferably NO X-ray
  • Physical examination is sufficient to make the diagnosis

Rationale

  • Management will not change even if X-ray shows osteoarthritis
  • Diagnosis of osteoarthritis is mainly clinical
    • History
    • Physical examination
    • Functional assessment
  • Radiological findings:
    • Poorly correlated with symptoms
    • Common in asymptomatic older individuals

Laboratory Investigations

  • No specific blood test for osteoarthritis
  • Inflammatory markers:
    • Normal or minimally raised

Overall Management Approach

Chronic Disease Model of Care

  • Multidisciplinary team management:
    • Physiotherapist
    • Exercise physiologist
    • Psychologist (chronic pain)
    • Occupational therapist
    • Orthopedic surgeon (long-term)

Goals of Management

  • Control pain
  • Reduce symptoms
  • Help patient cope with pain
  • Maintain and optimise physical function
  • Enable daily activities at home
  • Reduce need for care
  • Improve health-related quality of life

Patient Education & Shared Decision-Making

  • Explain the condition
  • Reassure about the nature of the disease
  • Optimise comorbidities
  • Address weight and exercise
  • Emphasise that osteoarthritis is:
    • Long-term
    • Not reversible
    • Treatment aims to control symptoms, not cure

Non-Pharmacological Management

1. Weight Loss

  • BMI is 32 (intentional inclusion in stem)
  • Weight loss:
    • Reduces pain
    • Improves knee function
  • Referrals:
    • Dietician
    • Exercise physiologist

2. Physiotherapy

  • Strengthening exercises
  • Focus on muscles around the knee
  • Stronger muscles = better joint support

3. Physical Aids

  • Walking sticks
  • Walkers
  • Helps maintain:
    • Mobility
    • Daily activities

4. Falls Risk Management

  • Higher risk of falls
  • Referral to Falls Clinic
  • Occupational therapist home assessment:
    • Guardrails on stairs
    • Toilet rails
    • Safer home environment

Pharmacological Management

Step-Ladder / Step-by-Step Approach

  • Do not jump to strongest painkillers
  • Stronger analgesics = more side effects
  • Aim:
    • Reduce pain enough to allow daily activities
    • Not complete pain elimination

Step 1: Topical NSAIDs

  • Examples:
    • Diclofenac
    • Voltaren
  • Advantages:
    • Minimal systemic absorption
    • Fewer side effects
  • Safe even in hypertensive patients

Step 2: Paracetamol

  • Options:
    • Regular paracetamol
    • Modified-release paracetamol (e.g., Panadol Osteo)
  • Purpose:
    • Ongoing pain control

Oral NSAIDs – Avoided in This Patient

  • Reasons:
    • Hypertension
    • On ACE inhibitor (perindopril)
  • Risks:
    • Renal impairment
    • Long-term NSAID use undesirable
  • Therefore:
    • Avoid oral NSAIDs
    • Prefer paracetamol

Step 3: Severe Pain / Specialist Input

  • Consider referral to:
    • Pain clinic
    • Pain specialist
  • Avoid early opioids
  • Opioids:
    • Very limited role
    • Risk of dependence
    • Reduced effectiveness over time

Steroid Injection (For Severe Flare-Ups)

  • Intra-articular steroid injection
  • Used when:
    • Severe pain
    • Flare-ups
    • Poor response to other measures
  • Effect:
    • Temporary pain relief (4–12 weeks)
  • Usually:
    • Single injection
    • Ultrasound-guided

Risks

  • Joint infection
  • Injury to joint structures
    • Ligaments
    • Meniscus

Surgical Management

Indication

  • Severe osteoarthritis
  • Persistent functional impairment
  • Failure of conservative management

Procedure

  • Total knee replacement
  • Knee joint removed
  • Replaced with prosthetic joint

Expected Outcome

  • Some improvement in:
    • Pain
    • Function
  • Not a complete return to normal

Practical Considerations

  • Long public waiting times with Medicare, often close to 10 years
  • While awaiting surgery:
    • Continue exercise
    • Continue weight loss
    • Ongoing conservative management

OSCE Structure – How to Start the Station

Opening

  • Start with open-ended question:
    • “How can I help you today?”

Patient:

“Doctor, I’ve had knee pain for a long time. It’s affecting my daily activities and I want to know what’s going on.”

Explaining the Diagnosis (Patient-Friendly)

Example Dialogue

Doctor:

“You have a condition called osteoarthritis in both of your knees. This is a wear-and-tear off the joint that commonly happens as we get older. The diagnosis is usually clinical and based on the history and physical examination. ”

Doctor (Reasoning):

  • Pain for six months
  • Worse at end of the day
  • Overweight
  • When I examined u, u have Reduced range of movement and Cracking sounds (crepitations) on movements. As the surface of the bones in joint are damaged, this causes pain on walking and in the long run it causes weaker muscles

Causes & Risk Factors Explained to Patient

  • OA does Not have a single cause, But some Risk factors increases the chance:
    • Being overweight
    • Previous injury or trauma to the joint
    • Repeated knee movements
    • Family history of OA

Clinical Features Recap (If Needed)

U may have:

  • Joint pain
  • Stiffness
  • Cracking sounds
  • Swelling / fluid

Management

“ we are not able to reverse and fix the damage done to the joint, but we can control the symptoms and pain, so our goal of management is to:

  1. Reduce the symptoms and pain as much as possible
  2. Helps you maintain the ability to attend your daily activities and work
  3. In non-pharmacological management: weight loss can help decrease the pain and improve the function of knee
  4. I can refer you to dietician and exercise physiologist for exercise plan
  5. I will refer u to physiotherapist who will provide u exercise for strengthing the muscles. Having stronger muscles provide better support to the joint.
  6. We can use physical aid to help you walk
  7. You are at higher risk of fall it is why I am going to refer you to the fall clinic, an occupation therapist will visit your home and check for safe environment at home, and make changes if necessary
  8. For pharmacological management, I will give you topical NSAIDS diclofenac
  9. We will also start painkiller with a stepladder treatment ( step-by-step approach ) because stronger painkillers have more side-effects, so we start with simple painkillers and step up if needed
  10. Aim of giving you painkiller is to reduce the pain, so you can do your daily activity. Please note that this does not mean you will be pain free.
  11. I can start with paracetamol modified release
  12. Avoid NSAIDS because you have high blood pressure, and you are using Perindopril
  13. If needed, we can consider stronger painkillers, and at that time, I will refer you to the pain clinic
  14. If these don’t help, or if we have severe flareup, we can do steroid injection to the joint which helps control the pain for 4 to 12 weeks. This usually happen under the guide of ultrasound.
  15. There are some risk such as infection or injury to the structure of joint
  16. So overall, we will focus on controlling the pain, so you will be able to do your daily function
  17. But if symptoms, worse or significantly impact your life, then I will refer you to orthopaedic surgeon to consider for surgery of total knee replacement
  18. In the surgery, the knee joint is removed and replaced by prosthetic joint & the surgery can provide some improvement in pain and infection

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: surgical-skills-expert (Australian surgical educator, FRACS) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The case states public total-knee-replacement waiting times 'with Medicare, often close to 10 years'. This is factually incorrect and conflates funding bodies.
Elective joint-replacement waiting in the Australian public system is long but not 'close to 10 years' - most patients wait months to a couple of years depending on jurisdiction and urgency category. Medicare (the MBS) funds services; public elective surgery waiting lists are run by state/territory public hospitals, not Medicare. Reword to: 'public waiting lists can be long (often many months to a few years), so continue conservative management while awaiting review'.
✏️ The pharmacological step-ladder lists topical NSAIDs then ongoing paracetamol as core therapy, which overstates the role of paracetamol under current Australian guidance.
Per the RACGP guideline for the management of knee and hip OA, land-based exercise and weight loss are the strongest (core) recommendations; topical NSAIDs are recommended for knee OA; regular paracetamol is NOT recommended for routine background use because the effect size is small (reserve for short-term/as-needed use). Oral NSAIDs (lowest effective dose, shortest duration) are effective but are appropriately avoided here given hypertension and the ACE inhibitor. Duloxetine is a further option for persistent pain.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Name the 'triple whammy' explicitly when justifying NSAID avoidance: the combination of an NSAID + ACE inhibitor (perindopril) + a diuretic markedly increases the risk of acute kidney injury; this strengthens the safety reasoning the case already gestures at and is a common AMC scoring point.
⭐ Emphasise exercise as the single highest-value intervention (strong RACGP recommendation) alongside weight loss - the case includes physiotherapy and weight loss but should foreground structured land-based (and where available, aquatic) exercise as first-line, not merely adjunctive; also correct the OCR typo in the final line ('improvement in pain and infection' should read 'pain and function').
⭐ Add metadata: this is a diagnosis-explanation plus management-counselling station; tag shared decision-making, the clinical (not radiological) basis of OA diagnosis, and the stepwise analgesia rationale as discriminating scoring domains, and cross-link to the hand OA case (case 1) for the shared non-pharmacological pathway.
