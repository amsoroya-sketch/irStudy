Hip Pain (Greater Trochanteric Pain Syndrome)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med 5 MSK and urology / 2. MSK UL LL (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: musculoskeletal

HIP PAIN – OSCE LECTURE NOTES

STEM

Your next patient has presented to your general practice complaining of hip pain.

Task:

  • Take history for 6 minutes
  • Explain your diagnosis and differentials

WHAT DOES “HIP PAIN” MEAN? (MURTAGH CONCEPT)

  • What patients call “hip pain” may come from:
    • Groin
    • Buttock
    • Upper thigh
    • Side of hip
    • Back of hip
  • Therefore hip pain may come from:
    • Spine (lumbar radiculopathy, sacroiliitis)
    • Knee
    • Abdomen
    • Pelvis
    • Pelvic organs
    • Retroperitoneum

FOUR-GROUP EXAM DIFFERENTIAL FRAMEWORK

GROUP 1 – JOINT / HIP STRUCTURE

  • Osteoarthritis
  • Rheumatoid arthritis
  • Psoriatic arthritis
  • Gout
  • Reactive arthritis / IBD
  • Fracture
  • Avascular necrosis
  • Adductor tendonitis
  • Sacroiliitis

GROUP 2 – RED FLAGS

  • Malignancy
  • Metastasis
  • Septic arthritis
  • Osteomyelitis

GROUP 3 – SOFT TISSUE & NEURO

  • Greater trochanteric pain syndrome
  • Lumbar radiculopathy
  • Polymyalgia rheumatica

GROUP 4 – REFERRED & VASCULAR

  • Abdomen
  • KUB (ureteric colic, UTI)
  • Hernia
  • Arterial occlusion
  • Spine disorders

HISTORY-TAKING STRUCTURE

STEP 1 – OPENING

Doctor:

“Can you tell me more about your pain, Jay? I understand your concern. Let me ask you a few questions and I’ll work out what’s happening.”

Doctor:

“Are you in pain now? Would you like a painkiller?”

STEP 2 – PAIN ANALYSIS

Ask:

  • Site
    • Front, back or side of hip?
  • Intensity
    • 1–10 scale
  • Quality
    • Dull, sharp, burning
  • Onset & duration
    Constant or intermittent
    Progression, is this 1st time
  • Radiation
    • To thigh?
    • To back?
  • Aggravating / relieving
  • Affect on work and home

STEP 3 – PAIN PATTERN (LOWER LIMB)

Four pattern questions:

  1. Night pain?
  2. Morning or end of day?
  3. Worse with activity or rest?
  4. Can you bear weight or are you limping?

STEP 4 – GENERAL MSK QUESTIONS

  • Swelling in hip joint?
  • Redness or rashes in the area?
  • Stiffness?
  • Trauma or injury?

STEP 5 – RED FLAGS

Malignancy

  • Weight loss?
  • Appetite?
  • Lumps?
  • Tiredness?
  • Chronic Cough?
  • Previous history of Breast lumps?

Infection

  • Fever?
  • Chills?

STEP 6 – JOINT DIFFERENTIALS

Osteoarthritis

  • Occupation
  • Excessive standing
  • Heavy lifting
  • Excessive Exercise
  • Have you noticed any cracking sound or grating sound when you move your hips ?

Rheumatoid

  • Other joints?
  • Family history?

Psoriatic

  • Rashes on elbows or knees?

Gout

  • Have you noticed eating red meat or drinking alcohol Make the pain worse or triggers the pain?

Reactive / IBD

  • Diarrhea?
  • Blood in stools?
  • Vaginal / penile discharge?
  • Safe sex?

STEP 7 – AVASCULAR NECROSIS

Ask:

  • Steroids?
  • Are you on any fat lowering medication?
  • Blood thinners?
  • Past hip fracture, dislocation or surgery?

STEP 8 – GREATER TROCHANTERIC PAIN SYNDROME

Ask:

  • Exercise?
  • Repetitive movements?
  • Occupation?
  • Pain worse when lying on that side at night?

STEP 9 – NEUROLOGICAL

  • Back pain?
  • Pins and needles?
  • Numbness?
  • Weakness in your legs?

STEP 10 – POLYMYALGIA RHEUMATICA

  • Shoulder pain?
  • Stiffness?
  • Headache?

STEP 11 – REFERRED PAIN

Abdomen & Pelvis

  • Abdominal pain?
  • Vomiting?
  • Periods regular? Heavy? Painful?

KUB

  • Burning or pain on passing urine?
  • Are you passing urine more frequently?
  • Blood in urine?

Peripheral Vascular disease

  • Does the pain start specifically on walking and stop as soon as you rest?
  • Previous history of heart or blood tube disease?

STEP 12 – CLOSURE

  • Smoking?
  • Alcohol?
  • Drugs?
  • Medications
  • Allergies
  • Past history
  • Family history of joint problems

KEY CONDITIONS REVIEW

AVASCULAR NECROSIS

  • Age: 20–50
  • Risks:
    • Steroids
    • Past fracture / dislocation
    • SLE
    • Sickle cell
  • Causes deep groin + hip pain

HIP OSTEOARTHRITIS

  • Worse with activity
  • Better with rest (in severe version, it doesn’t follow this pattern)
  • Pain usually felt in groin
  • Stiffness
  • Limping
  • Crepitus
  • Deformity

GREATER TROCHANTERIC PAIN SYNDROME

  • Gluteus medius tendonitis + trochanteric bursitis
  • Lateral hip pain
  • Usually in people who engage in walking exercises, tennis, sports
  • Worse:
    • Lying on that side
    • Climbing stairs
    • Getting out of car

HISTORY FINDINGS

  • Pain on the outer side of the right hip
  • Pain worse on activity, better with rest
  • Worse on sleeping on that side at night

DIAGNOSIS

Greater trochanteric pain syndrome

EXPLANATION

“You most likely have a condition called greater trochanteric pain syndrome.

This is an inflammation of the muscles in that area and a cushion-like structure called a bursa on the side of your hip.

The reason I made this diagnosis is because your pain is on the outer side and it is worse when you lie on that side at night.”

DIFFERENTIAL STATEMENT

“I also considered infection, but you don’t have fever.

I also considered cancer, but you haven’t lost weight.”

Then list others if time is short.

SPECIAL AMC CASE – ADDUCTOR TENDONITIS

  • Inner thigh / groin pain
  • Pain in the inner thigh on squeezing knees together
  • Site of pain is adductor muscle of thigh

Test:

“Put your fist between your knees and squeeze.”

OSTEOARTHRITIS VERSION

  • Long-term pain
  • Stiffness
  • Crepitus: Cracking sound on walking
  • Restricted movement

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The avascular-necrosis screen asks about 'fat lowering medication' (statins/cholesterol-lowering drugs) as a risk factor. Statins are not an established cause of osteonecrosis of the femoral head.
Recognised AVN risk factors are corticosteroids (the leading cause), excess alcohol, prior hip trauma/dislocation, SLE, sickle cell disease, HIV and dysbaric exposure. Replace/clarify the statin question - it is a distractor, not a risk factor. (Retain the steroid and alcohol questions, which are correct.)
✏️ No management or investigation pathway is provided for the working diagnosis of greater trochanteric pain syndrome (GTPS).
Add Australian first-line management: relative rest and load/activity modification, simple analgesia (paracetamol first-line, short NSAID course if suitable), targeted gluteal/hip abductor physiotherapy, weight optimisation, and a corticosteroid injection if refractory. Imaging (X-ray to exclude OA; ultrasound/MRI) only if diagnosis unclear or not improving.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Reinforce Murtagh's 'true hip vs referred' teaching with an examination cue: true hip/joint pathology (OA, AVN) is felt in the GROIN and reproduced by internal rotation, whereas GTPS gives LATERAL point tenderness over the greater trochanter and pain lying on that side - this distinction is the crux of the case.
⭐ Add the age-based probability framing that the retrieved Murtagh source explicitly gives: in a middle-aged or elderly woman with 'hip pain', consider the under-diagnosed trochanteric bursitis / gluteus medius tendinopathy (GTPS) - directly validates the case diagnosis and could be stated to learners.
⭐ Strengthen red-flag safety-netting: fracture (unable to weight bear after a fall - urgent X-ray), septic arthritis/osteomyelitis (fever + severe pain - urgent referral, potentially 000), and metastatic bone disease (rest/night pain, weight loss) - and note vascular buttock claudication as a serious not-to-be-missed cause.
⭐ Add related-topic cross-link to the traumatic-hip and groin-pain cases in this bundle so learners can contrast atraumatic GTPS with fracture and prosthetic-joint infection presentations.
