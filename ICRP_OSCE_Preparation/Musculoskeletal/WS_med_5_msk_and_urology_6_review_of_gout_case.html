Gout - Diagnosis, Risk Factors and Management (with Steroid-Induced Hyperglycaemia)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med 5 MSK and urology / other MSK Cases (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: musculoskeletal

GOUT

CASE 1: ACUTE KNEE PAIN – GOUT VARIANT

Patient Stem

Your next patient is a 37 year old man who presented to you complaining of pain and swelling in his left knee. The pain started 2 days ago, he has no fever. He drinks 2 beers every night and eats red meat and seafood every day.

He has had 2 episodes of gout in his big toe before. Your colleague sent him for joint aspiration and the results show:

Uric acid crystals seen, negative birefringent, culture negative, leuc +

Task:

  • Explain diagnosis with reasons
  • Explain contributing factors
  • Explain your management plan

DIAGNOSIS DISCUSSION

Examiner Question

  • “What is your diagnosis?”

Diagnosis

  • Gout / Gouty arthritis

Points That May Confuse Candidates

  1. Leukocytes positive
    • Leukocytes can be positive in any inflammation
    • Does not mean infection
    • Culture negative → no infection
  2. Negative birefringence
    • Gout → negative birefringent crystals
    • Pseudogout → positive birefringent crystals
    • Pseudogout not a hot topic
    • Uric acid crystals already confirmed

Explaining Diagnosis to Patient (Task 1)

Framework Used

  • Same as knee pain case

Explanation

  • Condition is called gout / gouty arthritis
  • U have Uric acid crystals deposited in the joint
  • These crystals cause inflammation

Reasons

  • Joint is painful
  • Joint is swollen
  • No fever
  • No infection
  • We have already analyzed Joint fluid and it confirms uric acid crystals

CONTRIBUTING FACTORS / RISK FACTORS (Task 2)

Dietary Factors

  • Red meat
  • Seafood
  • Sugary drinks (fructose-containing)

Alcohol

  • Beer (worst)
  • Spirits (next)

Medications

  • Diuretics (water pills)
  • Especially thiazides used for hypertension

Other Risk Factors

  • Overweight / obesity
  • Trauma to the foot
    • Constant trauma (e.g. braking in taxi drivers)
    • Advice: comfortable shoes
  • Dehydration
    • Not drinking enough water
  • Kidney disease
    • Increases uric acid levels

MANAGEMENT PLAN (Task 3)

Investigations

  • Diagnosis already confirmed by joint aspiration
  • Further tests:
    • Serum uric acid
    • Urea, electrolytes, creatinine (kidney function)

Pharmacological Management

Acute / Immediate Management

Options include:

  • NSAIDs or anti-inflammatory
    • Examples mentioned:
      • Diclofenac (Voltaren)
      • Indomethacin
      • Ibuprofen
  • Steroids
    • Oral prednisolone
    • Intra-articular steroid injection
  • Colchicine

Long-Term Management

  • Start urate-lowering therapy
    • Allopurinol
    • Can be started during acute attack or later

Two Essential Points

  1. Treat-to-target
    • Adjust dose
    • Target uric acid < 0.36
  2. Flare prophylaxis
    • Start colchicine daily
    • Continue for 6 months

Patient Explanation

  • Allopurinol lowers uric acid
  • Reduces frequency of gout attacks
  • Prevents:
    • Joint damage
    • Kidney stones
  • Initial treatment may increase flare risk
  • Hence colchicine is added

Non-Pharmacological Management

  • Avoid contributing factors:
    • Red meat
    • Seafood
    • Sugary drinks
    • Alcohol
  • Stay well hydrated
  • Avoid dehydration
  • Review thiazides if present in stem
  • Control cardiovascular risk factors
    • Gout associated with cardiovascular disease

CASE 2: KNOWN GOUT WITH URINARY SYMPTOMS & HIGH SUGAR

Case Setup

  • Patient is a known case of gout
  • Focus on investigations and complications

Investigations Discussed

  • Serum uric acid
  • Urea, electrolytes, creatinine
  • Urine MCS
  • Fasting blood sugar
  • HbA1c
  • PSA (male >50 with nocturia)
  • Lipid profile

New Examiner Finding

  • Urine: 4+ glucose
  • Random blood sugar: 18

Diabetes Diagnostic Reminder

  • Fasting ≥ 7
  • Random ≥ 11.1
  • Known as “7–11 rule”

INTERPRETING HIGH SUGAR IN THIS CASE

Key Clinical Reasoning

  • Patient recently on prednisolone
  • Prednisolone can transiently raise blood sugar
  • Cannot immediately diagnose diabetes

Management Approach

Step 1: Manage Gout First

  • Change prednisolone
  • Switch to:
    • NSAID
    • Colchicine

Step 2: Long-Term Gout Control

  • Start allopurinol
  • Treat-to-target uric acid < 0.36
  • Lifestyle modification:
    • Exercise
    • Weight loss
    • Avoid red meat, seafood, alcohol

Addressing High Blood Sugar

Explanation to Patient

  • Test needs to be repeated
  • Reason:
    • Steroids affect blood sugar
    • Patient is symptomatic
    • But on a medication that alters glucose

Plan

  • Stop prednisolone
  • Repeat:
    • Fasting blood sugar
    • HbA1c
  • Repeat after a few days to one week

If high again, Diabetes is Confirmed

  • Start metformin (first line)
  • No need to know dose for AMC
  • Follow-up in 3 months with another sugar test
  • Non-pharmacological management:
    • Healthy diet
    • Exercise
    • Weight loss

KEY TEACHING POINT

  • High sugar in this case is context-dependent
  • Examiner wants:
    • Recognition of steroid-induced hyperglycaemia
    • Safe, guideline-based decision-making
  • Manage gout first
  • Reassess sugar after stopping steroids

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The diabetes diagnostic reminder gives only the '7-11 rule' (fasting >=7, random >=11.1 mmol/L) and omits the HbA1c criterion and the requirement for confirmation in an asymptomatic patient.
Australian diagnostic criteria for type 2 diabetes are: fasting plasma glucose >=7.0 mmol/L, random/2-hour OGTT glucose >=11.1 mmol/L, OR HbA1c >=6.5% (>=48 mmol/mol). In an asymptomatic person a single abnormal result must be confirmed on a separate day; a symptomatic patient with one unequivocal value can be diagnosed. This case also correctly recognises steroid-induced (prednisolone) transient hyperglycaemia as a confounder requiring repeat testing after stopping the steroid.
✏️ Allopurinol is presented without initiation/titration safety detail, and colchicine is listed as an acute option without the modern low-dose caveat.
Start allopurinol at a low dose (50-100 mg daily, lower in renal impairment) and titrate to the target serum urate; check renal function first and warn about rare but serious allopurinol hypersensitivity (higher risk with HLA-B*5801, more prevalent in some Asian populations). Use low-dose colchicine (e.g. 0.5 mg two to three times daily) - the old high-dose-to-diarrhoea regimen is abandoned. Febuxostat is an alternative urate-lowering agent if allopurinol is not tolerated.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Clarify the acute-flare pharmacology hierarchy and the diabetes interaction: in a patient who then reveals hyperglycaemia, NSAIDs and colchicine are preferred over oral prednisolone precisely because steroids worsen glucose control - the case reaches this conclusion but should state the target urate (<0.36 mmol/L, or <0.30 mmol/L if tophi/erosions) and continue flare prophylaxis for at least 6 months after reaching target.
⭐ Add first-attack vs recurrent-attack guidance: urate-lowering therapy is clearly indicated here given >=2 prior attacks plus tophaceous risk, and lifestyle counselling should be specific and Australian (limit beer and spirits, reduce red meat/offal and seafood, avoid fructose-sweetened drinks, weight loss, adequate hydration, and review any thiazide - consider switching antihypertensive).
⭐ Add metadata: this is a two-part diagnosis-and-management station combining crystal-arthropathy reasoning with steroid-induced hyperglycaemia; tag the joint-aspiration interpretation (negatively birefringent urate crystals, leucocytes do not equal infection when culture negative) as a key discriminator and cross-link to knee osteoarthritis (case 8) for the acute-monoarthritis differential.
