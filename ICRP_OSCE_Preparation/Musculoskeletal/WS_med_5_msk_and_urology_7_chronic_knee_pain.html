1. CASE INTRODUCTION & EXAM FORMAT

Old vs New Version of Chronic Knee Pain

  • Chronic knee pain used to be one of the old classic cases.
  • After traumatic knee cases were introduced, the line between acute and chronic became clear.

Typical Stem:

45 yrs old man C/O knee pain for 6 months

A 45-year-old man or woman comes with knee pain

You quickly realise it has been there for a long time – around 6 months

Exam Tasks

  • 6 minutes history
  • Physical examination on screen
  • Give diagnosis and differentials

2. CORE CONCEPT – YOU NEED A GOOD LIST OF DIFFERENTIALS

The entire case goes back to:

“You need a good list of differentials.”

3. DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS FRAMEWORK

A. JOINT PROBLEMS

Same arthritis group:

  • Rheumatoid arthritis
  • Psoriatic arthritis
  • Gout
  • Reactive arthritis
  • IBD related arthritis

Still ask red flags

B. RED FLAGS

  • Malignancy / metastasis
  • Infection (even though septic arthritis for 6 months is unlikely, still ask)

C. SOFT TISSUE

  • Patellar tendonitis
  • Bursitis
  • Chondromalacia patella
    • (new name: Patellofemoral syndrome)
  • Ruptured Baker’s cyst

D. NEUROVASCULAR

Vascular

  • Peripheral arterial disease
  • DVT

Neurological

  • Lumbar radiculopathy

E. REFERRED PAIN

  • Hip conditions

F. OTHER

  • Acute rheumatic fever
  • Osteochondritis
  • Osgood–Schlatter disease (only in children 10–15, never adults)
  • Meniscal tear

G. OLD TRAUMATIC INJURIES CAUSING CHRONIC PAIN

  • Old meniscal tears
  • Old ACL / collateral ligament tears → unstable knee → post-traumatic OA

4. IMPORTANT RED FLAG FEATURES IN HISTORY

  • Weight loss
  • Fever
  • Persistent unexplained pain
  • Swelling without trauma → effusion

5. KEY CONDITIONS – FEATURES

A. PATELLOFEMORAL SYNDROME (CHONDROMALACIA)

What is happening

  • Cartilage under the patella is damaged
  • Bone rubs on bone → pain

Symptoms

  • Pain behind or deep to the patella
  • Worse on:
    • Climbing stairs
    • Going downstairs
    • Squatting

Movie Theater Sign

  • Pain after sitting with knee flexed for a long time
  • Worse when getting up
  • Patients like aisle seats to stretch legs

Special Test – Clarke’s Test

How

  • Hold patella
  • Push it downward
  • Ask patient to tighten thigh

Positive

  • Pain when patella rubs

B. PATELLAR TENDONITIS

  • Jumping sports (basketball, volleyball)
  • Tender patellar tendon
  • Worse with activity
  • Better with rest

Exam

  • Press upper pole
  • Palpate below patella → pain

C. PREPATELLAR BURSITIS

  • Housemaid’s knee
  • Kneeling, direct pressure

Findings

  • Swollen
  • Red
  • Tense
  • Fluctuant
  • Movement of knee is normal

D. BAKER’S CYST

  • Joint effusion goes to popliteal fossa
  • Usually painless lump
  • Pain when ruptured
  • Must rule out DVT

E. OSTEOARTHRITIS

Risk factors

  • Obesity
  • Deformities
  • Previous trauma
  • Meniscal tears
  • Old ACL injuries

Features

  • Chronic pain and stiffness
  • Worse with activity
  • Better with rest
  • Crepitation
  • Wasting

Key comparison

  • OA: downstairs more painful
  • Chondromalacia: upstairs more painful

6. HISTORY TAKING FRAMEWORK

Opening

“Tell me more about your pain.”

Address concern

Offer pain relief

Pain Analysis – CICORA

  • Site (front / back / side)
  • Intensity
  • Character
  • Onset
  • Radiation
  • Alleviating / aggravating
  • Function (home & work)

Pattern

  • Night pain
  • Morning vs evening
  • Worse with rest or activity
  • Limping
  • Weight bearing

General MSK Questions

  • Swelling
  • Redness / rash
  • Stiffness
  • Trauma

malignancy and infection questions to be askeed here.


7. KEY QUESTIONS (EXAMINER TRAP)

These must be asked:

  1. Any locking?
  2. Does the knee give way?
  3. Any clicking / cracking / grating?
  4. Is pain worse after sitting for long?
  5. Is pain worse when climbing stairs?

(Points towards chondromalacia)

8. DIFFERENTIAL-SPECIFIC QUESTIONS

Joint

  • Occupation
  • Exercise, jumping
  • RA: other joints, family history, rashes
  • Gout: red meat, alcohol
  • Reactive arthritis / IBD: diarrhea, discharge

Soft Tissue

  • Patellar tendonitis →excessive jumping or exercise
  • Bursitis →any recent kneeling or direct pressure on the knee
  • Baker’s cyst → have u noticed any lump behind knee

Neurovascular

  • Color change
  • Pallor or bluish discoloration
  • Calf swelling
  • Long travel

Neurological

  • Weakness
  • Numbness
  • Pins and needles

Referred Pain

  • Hip pain
  • Ankle pain

Rheumatic Fever

  • Recent sore throat

Wrap-up

  • Smoking
  • Alcohol
  • Drugs
  • Medications
  • Allergies
  • Past medical
  • Family history

9. OLD EXAM CASE – PATELLOFEMORAL SYNDROME

History

  • Hockey player
  • Pain on kneecap
  • Worse on stairs going up and down
  • 6 months

Examination

  • Normal inspection
  • Pain on pressing patella

Diagnosis

Patellofemoral syndromePatpatellofemoal syndromeellofemoral syndrome (Chondromalacia patella)

Explanation to Patient

The cartilage behind your knee cap has been damaged and when u walk, the knee caps rub on bones and causes pain

“The cartilage behind your kneecap has been damaged. When you walk, the kneecap rubs against the bone, which causes pain.”

Why

  • Long duration
  • Worse on stairs
  • Pain on patellar pressure

Differentials

  • Pre-patellar bursitis
  • Patellar tendonitis
  • Osteoarthritis
  • Others

10. NEW VERSION – MENISCAL TEAR

History

  • 45-year-old
  • 6 months pain
  • Worse with walking
  • Better with rest
  • Locking
  • Old injury
  • No swelling

Exam

  • McMurray’s test positive

Diagnosis

Meniscal tear

Explanation

“The meniscus is a cushion inside the knee. After your old injury, it most likely tore. That explains the locking and pain. The special test I did was positive.”

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: surgical-skills-expert (Australian surgical educator, FRACS, MSK focus) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The 'key comparison' states OA is worse going downstairs while chondromalacia (patellofemoral syndrome, PFPS) is worse going upstairs. This dichotomy is unreliable and largely reversed.
PFPS is classically worse DESCENDING stairs and squatting (peak eccentric patellofemoral load), plus prolonged sitting (the 'theatre/movie sign'); both ascending and descending stairs can hurt. Do not teach a clean up-vs-down rule to separate PFPS from OA - use the whole picture (age, crepitus, effusion, locking, imaging) instead.
✏️ The diagnosis text is corrupted by an OCR/typing artefact ('Patellofemoral syndromePatpatellofemoal syndromeellofemoral syndrome').
Clean the diagnosis to read 'Patellofemoral syndrome (chondromalacia patellae)'. Also note Clarke's/patellar-grind test has poor specificity and can provoke pain in normal knees, so a positive test is supportive but not diagnostic.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add first-line management for the two worked diagnoses: PFPS = physiotherapy-led quadriceps/VMO and hip-abductor strengthening, activity/load modification, weight optimisation, paracetamol first-line for analgesia, patellar taping/orthoses if indicated (surgery rarely needed); chronic meniscal tear = physiotherapy first, arthroscopy reserved for true mechanical locking/failed conservative care.
⭐ Add the initial investigation set so the candidate can interpret 'physical examination on screen': weight-bearing knee X-ray (OA joint-space loss, alignment), inflammatory bloods (CRP/ESR, RF, urate) if an arthritis is suspected, and MRI for suspected meniscal/ligamentous pathology - reserving imaging for red-flag or non-improving cases.
⭐ Reinforce chronic red-flag safety-netting that the case lists but does not action: unexplained weight loss/night pain (malignancy/metastasis), fever or an atraumatic hot effusion (septic arthritis - aspirate urgently), and a ruptured Baker's cyst mimicking DVT (examine calf, consider Wells score/ultrasound).
⭐ Correct the minor detail that Osgood-Schlatter is confined to growing adolescents (approx 10-15y) - keep this exclusion, and add referred sources (hip pathology, L3-4 lumbar radiculopathy) as explicit 'must-ask' items since hip disease commonly refers pain to the knee.
