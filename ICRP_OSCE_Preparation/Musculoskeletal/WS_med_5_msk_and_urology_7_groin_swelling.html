Groin Swelling - Inguinal Hernia (History and Differentials)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med 5 MSK and urology / other MSK Cases (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: musculoskeletal

Groin Swelling – OSCE History Taking & Diagnosis (Exact Lecture Framework)

Case Context & Recall

  • This case appeared as a recall in early February (6th Feb).
  • Some recalls mentioned groin pain, others groin swelling.
  • Important clarification:

    👉 The stem is groin swelling, not groin pain.
  • If the stem was groin pain, you would apply the previously discussed groin pain framework.
  • This discussion focuses only on groin swelling, where differentials are different.

Case Stem

  • Patient: 50-year-old man
  • Setting: General practice
  • Chief complaint: Groin swelling
  • Time:
    • 6 minutes for history
    • Explain diagnosis and differentials
  • No physical examination
  • Diagnosis is made purely on history findings

Initial Approach

  • This is considered an easy case
  • Key focus:
    • Clear structure
    • Good list of differentials
  • Hernia is already expected, but you must still rule out other causes

Differentials for Groin Swelling (Conceptual List)

  • Hernia (top of the list)
  • Lymph nodes
    • Lymphadenitis (infective)
    • Lymphadenopathy (malignancy)
  • Skin conditions
    • Boils / furuncles / abscesses
  • Hip-related causes
    • Septic arthritis
    • Effusion
    • Trauma → hematosis
  • Malignancy-related
    • Leukemia
    • Lymphoma
    • Skin cancers (leg, scrotum)
    • SCC, melanoma

History Taking Structure

1. Opening Question

  • Open-ended
  • Example:

    “Can you tell me more about your swelling?”
  • Let the patient explain.
  • If patient says:

    “I’m worried, it’s been there for two months and not getting better”
  • Response:

    “I understand your concern, let me figure out what’s happening and we’ll make the best management plan.”

2. Exploring the Swelling

Timing

  • How long have you had this swelling?

Course

  • Is it:
    • On and off?
    • Constantly there?
  • Is it getting worse or increasing in size?

Alleviating & Aggravating Factors

  • General:

    “Have you noticed anything that makes it better or worse?”

Postural / Positional Effect (Key Question)

  • Specifically ask:
    • Does lying down make it better?
    • Does standing make it worse?

Interpretation

  • Lymph node / skin conditions → no positional change
  • Hernia →
    • Better when lying down
    • Worse when standing or straining

3. Skin & Infection-Related Differentials

Skin

  • Any rashes on skin?
  • Any redness on skin?

Systemic Symptoms

  • Fever?
  • Chills?

Large abscesses can give systemic features.

4. Hernia-Focused Questions

Pain

  • Any pain in the groin?

Red Flags (Incarceration)

  • Abdominal pain?
  • Nausea?
  • Vomiting?
  • Bloating?

Teaching Point

  • Hernias are usually not painful
  • Pain suggests incarceration

5. Hernia Risk Factors (Key Diagnostic Confirmation)

Weight

  • Are you overweight?

Previous Abdominal Surgery

  • Any surgeries on your abdomen?
    • Example: appendectomy

Increased Intra-abdominal Pressure

  • Chronic coughing?
  • Chronic constipation?
  • Straining to pass stools?
  • Any Difficulty passing urine?
  • Heavy lifting?

Standing / Occupation

  • Standing for long periods?
  • What is your occupation now?
  • What was your occupation before?

Example Findings

  • Retired now
  • Previous job involved lifting heavy boxes
  • Chronic constipation
  • Chronic cough
  • BPH → straining to pass urine

6. Hip-Related Differentials

Ask specifically:

  • Any hip pain?
  • Any limping?
  • Can you bear weight?
  • Any trauma or injury?

Purpose:

  • Rule out:
    • Septic arthritis
    • Effusion
    • Hematosis

(Fever/chills already covered)

7. Lymph Node–Related Differentials

Leg Examination (History-Based)

  • Any redness or rashes on the leg?
  • Any suspicious skin lesions?
  • Any suspicious moles?

Scrotal / Genital Causes

  • Any non-healing ulcers on your private parts?
  • Any rashes or redness on penis or private parts?

8. Sexual History (Relevant)

  • Are you sexually active?
  • In a stable relationship?
  • Multiple partners?
  • Do you use condoms?
  • Ever diagnosed with an STI?

Example

  • History of herpes → possible cause of tender lymph nodes

9. Generalized Lymphadenopathy

  • Weight loss?
  • Change in appetite?
  • Other lumps or bumps?
  • Tiredness?
  • Night sweats?

10. Travel History

  • Asked for completion
  • Looking for infections like:
    • Tuberculosis

11. Closure

  • SADMA
  • Past medical history
  • Family history

Exam Findings (Given in the Case)

Patient reports:

  • Swelling on one side
  • Better when lying down
  • Overweight
  • Constipation
  • Chronic cough
  • Previous appendectomy
  • Heavy lifting in previous job
  • Prostate problem → straining to pass urine
  • No:
    • Weight loss
    • Fever
    • Limping
    • Skin rashes

Most Likely Diagnosis

Inguinal Hernia

Explanation to the Patient

Diagnosis Explanation:

“In this case, the abdominal tissue protudes out through the groin and creates a sac that we call hernia.

“Most likely, you have a condition called an inguinal hernia.”

“What happens is that tissue from inside the abdomen protrudes through the groin area and forms a sac, which we call a hernia.”

Reasoning

  1. Risk factors present
    • Straining
    • Heavy lifting
    • Abdominal surgery
  2. Positional feature
    • Gets better when lying down
    • Seen specifically in inguinal hernia

Differentials Explained to Patient

  • I was thinking about Skin problems
    • Boils
    • Furuncles
    • Abscesses
  • Hip problems
    • Septic arthritis
    • Arthritis with effusion
    • Trauma with bleeding
  • Enlarged lymph nodes in your groin
    • Leg infections like cellulitis
    • STIs like herpes, syphilis
    • Skin cancers
      • Melanoma (leg)
      • SCC (scrotum)

Additional Teaching Points from Q&A

Lymph Node Characteristics

  • Can ask:
    • Is it tender?
    • Is it painful to touch?
    • Is it hard or soft?
  • Mobility is difficult for patients to assess

Coughing & Straining

  • Can be included in aggravating factors
  • Postural change is more important

Risk Factors Placement

  • Preferably asked within hernia section
  • Not all at the beginning

Undescended Testis

  • Previous surgery history can cover this
  • Not emphasized further in the case

Final Teaching Summary

  • This case appeared once
  • Confusion exists with groin pain vs swelling
  • Stem matters
  • Using structure + risk factors + positional history leads clearly to inguinal hernia

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The differential list for a groin lump omits femoral hernia and several classic surgical differentials, and contains an OCR/terminology error ('Trauma -> hematosis' should read haematoma).
For a groin swelling the surgical sieve should explicitly include femoral hernia (more common in women and elderly, higher risk of strangulation - a key not-to-be-missed diagnosis), saphena varix, femoral artery aneurysm, hydrocele/varicocele or an undescended/ectopic testis, and a lipoma of the cord, in addition to inguinal hernia and lymphadenopathy. Correct 'hematosis' to 'haematoma'.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add explicit red-flag/incarceration safety-netting and disposition: a groin lump that becomes irreducible, tense, tender, or is associated with abdominal pain, vomiting, distension or absent bowel motions suggests incarceration/strangulation and is a surgical emergency requiring urgent same-day surgical assessment. The case mentions incarceration features but should state the escalation clearly.
⭐ Add management/referral even though the station is history plus explanation: a symptomatic inguinal hernia should be referred for elective surgical repair (open or laparoscopic mesh repair); truly asymptomatic hernias may be observed. Advise the patient to seek emergency care (call 000 or present to ED) if the lump becomes painful and irreducible.
⭐ Add metadata: this is a diagnosis-by-history station (no examination) where the positional history (reduces on lying, worse on standing/straining) and intra-abdominal-pressure risk factors are the discriminating features; tag the sexual-history and lymphadenopathy screen as scored empathy/thoroughness domains.
