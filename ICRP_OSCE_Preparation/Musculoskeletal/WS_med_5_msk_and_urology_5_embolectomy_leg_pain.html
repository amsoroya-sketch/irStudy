Acute Compartment Syndrome After Embolectomy (Reperfusion Injury)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med 5 MSK and urology / other MSK Cases (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: musculoskeletal

Embolectomy Leg Pain Case (Post-procedure Acute Leg Pain)


Your next patient presented to the emergency department with pain his left leg. He is a known case of AF. On examination his leg was pale and pulses were absent. An embolectomy surgery was done 4 hours ago. Now the patient is complaining of severe right leg pain and swelling, pulse is present but he cannot wriggle his toes and paresthesia is present. All passive movements are painful.

CRT normal Temp normal

Task:

Take history from this patient

Explain diagnosis and differentials to the patient

Storyline / Clinical Reasoning

  • Embolus removed from leg artery
  • After surgery:
    • Pain worsening
    • Swelling developing
    • Pulse now present
  • Indicates:
    • Not a new embolus
    • Concern due to:
      • Inability to move toes
      • Pain on passive movement
      • Paresthesia

Diagnosis

Primary Diagnosis

  • Compartment syndrome

Terminology Discussion

  • Term “reperfusion syndrome” may be recalled
  • However:
    • Not well-established terminology
    • References point toward compartment syndrome
  • Non-traumatic compartment syndrome can occur due to:
    • Ischemia
    • Reperfusion injury
    • Revascularization procedures (e.g. embolectomy)

Why Pulses Are Present

  • Pulses present not to rule out compartment syndrome
  • Given intentionally to:
    • Avoid confusion with recurrent emboli

Features Supporting Compartment Syndrome

  • Painful passive movements
  • Restricted active movements
  • Paresthesia
  • Swelling
  • Pulses may still be present

History Taking Structure

General Approach

  • Not a chronic leg pain case
  • Acute pain in special post-procedure context
  • Focus on surgical complications
  • No need to assess hemodynamic stability

Opening Question (Example Dialogue)

“Hi, my name is Amir. Can you tell me more about your pain?”

Expected Patient Response

  • Pain worsening since surgery
  • Leg swelling
  • Anxiety because surgery was expected to help

Addressing Concerns & Analgesia

  • Acknowledge concern
  • Ask about pain control:
    • Offer analgesia
    • Patient already received painkillers
  • Proceed with history

Pain Exploration (SIQORAA Framework)

Site

  • One leg or both legs
  • Unilateral

Intensity

  • Severity score (1–10)

Quality

  • Sharp
  • Burning
  • Dull ache

Onset

  • When did it start exactly?
  • Relation to surgery

Radiation

  • Any spread of pain

Aggravating / Alleviating Factors

  • What makes it better or worse
  • Pain at rest or only on movement

Note: No chronic pain patterns (morning/night/end of day)

Differential-Focused History: Surgical Complications

1. Neurological Injury

  • Pins and needles?
  • Numbness?
  • Weakness in moving the leg?

2. Vascular Injury

  • Any color change?
    • Pallor
  • Do u have any bruises?
  • Do u feel your leg is cold?

3. Musculoskeletal Injury

  • Swelling:
    • One side or both?
    • Up to which level it extend?
    • Getting worse?
  • Any Redness or rashes?
  • Movement:
    • Can you move your toes, ankle, knee?
    • Do u have Pain on movement?

Compartment Syndrome Checklist (Covered by Questions)

  • Pallor ✔
  • Coldness ✔
  • Paresthesia ✔
  • Pain ✔
  • Difficulty with movement ✔

Additional Considerations

Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT)

  • Recent immobilization due to surgery
  • Ask about:
    • Any Previous or family history of clotting disorders
    • Do u have any dilated veins on legs?

No need for long-travel or COST-VMPF history

Atrial Fibrillation History

  • When diagnosed?
  • Current treatment?
  • Compliance with medication?
  • Regular GP follow-ups?
  • Any palpitations or racing heart?

Final Diagnosis Explanation to Patient

How to Explain

  • Name condition first:
    • Compartment syndromewhich is Caused Due to reperfusion after surgery

Explanation (Example Dialogue)

“The clot in your leg was blocking blood flow. Once we removed it, the sudden return of blood increased the pressure inside the muscles. This pressure causes a condition called compartment syndrome.”

Supporting Features Explained

  • Pain on passive movement
  • Inability to move toes
  • Pins and needles / numbness

Differentials (Explained to Patient)

Surgical Complications

  • Nerve injury
  • Blood vessel injury
  • Muscle or tendon injury

Other Differentials

  • Recurrent embolus> another clot in the blood tube
  • Deep vein thrombosis which is another type of clot in blood tube
  • Infection:
    • Cellulitis
    • Sepsis (early but possible)
  • Post-thrombotic syndrome
    • Listed last
    • Less suitable but contextually relevant

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: surgical-skills-expert (Australian surgical educator, FRACS) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The stem describes initial pain in the LEFT leg with an embolectomy performed, then the patient complaining of severe RIGHT leg pain and swelling. The laterality is internally inconsistent (the affected/operated limb should be the same side).
Correct the laterality so the post-operative compartment syndrome affects the same limb that was ischaemic and revascularised (the left leg). A side mismatch confuses the clinical reasoning that pain in the reperfused limb signals reperfusion-related compartment syndrome.
✏️ The case ends at explaining the diagnosis and differentials and does not state the definitive management, which is a critical omission for a limb-threatening surgical emergency.
Acute compartment syndrome is a surgical emergency: management is urgent escalation to the vascular/orthopaedic surgical team for immediate emergency fasciotomy (aim within ~6 hours of onset), not delayed. Adjuncts include removing constricting dressings, keeping the limb at heart level (not elevated), high-flow oxygen, IV analgesia and IV fluids, plus checking creatine kinase, potassium and renal function to detect reperfusion-associated rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalaemia and acute kidney injury. Diagnosis is clinical; intracompartmental pressure measurement should never delay fasciotomy when the diagnosis is evident.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add the 'earliest and most sensitive sign' teaching explicitly: pain out of proportion and pain on passive stretch of the compartment muscles are the earliest features, while pulselessness and paralysis are late/ominous signs - the case correctly notes pulses may be present but should state why waiting for pulselessness is dangerous.
⭐ Add reperfusion physiology and post-embolectomy monitoring: after revascularisation of an acutely ischaemic limb, reperfusion causes tissue oedema and raised compartment pressures; patients need serial neurovascular observations, and a low threshold for prophylactic/therapeutic fasciotomy. Add cardiovascular optimisation of the underlying atrial fibrillation with anticoagulation to prevent further embolism.
⭐ Add metadata: reclassify this as a time-critical emergency-scenario station and cross-link to acute limb ischaemia (the '6 Ps') so learners connect the pre-operative ischaemia and the post-operative compartment syndrome as a continuum.
