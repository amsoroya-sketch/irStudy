Rotator Cuff Tear - Explaining Ultrasound Results

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med 5 MSK and urology / other MSK Cases (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: musculoskeletal

Shoulder – Rotator Cuff Injury

Your next patient has come to your general practice to discuss his ultrasound results. The physiotherapist had referred him for a shoulder ultrasound and the results show:

  • Complete tear of the subscapularis
  • Partial tear in the supraspinatus and infraspinatus
  • Teres minor normal
  • No bursitis seen

Task

  • Explain USD to the patient
  • Take further history

Diagnosis: rotator cuff injury

  • Candidate will have extra time
  • Explanation should be long and detailed
  • Aim:
    • Build understanding
    • Maintain rapport
    • Use time effectively

3. Starting the Station – Opening & Rapport

3.1 Opening the Discussion

Example Dialogue

  • “Hi, my name is Amir, I’ll be your doctor today. I have your results with me, but just before we start, can you tell me what has happened so far?”

3.2 Patient’s Likely Opening Statement

  • Long-standing shoulder problem
  • Seen physiotherapist
  • Completed ~2 months of physio
  • No improvement
  • Sent for ultrasound
  • Now attending for results

Reasoning

  • Creates better rapport
  • Better flow than immediately explaining results

4. Explaining the Ultrasound Results

4.1 Building Basic Understanding of the Shoulder

  • The Shoulder joint is a ball and socket joint
  • Head of the arm bone acts like a ball that Attaches into the shoulder joint
  • This type of joint is:
    • Structurally weak
    • Requires muscles around it to remain stable

4.2 Introducing the Rotator Cuff

  • There are four muscles around the shoulder which are Responsible for:
    • Movement of the shoulder
    • Stability and integrity of the joint
  • These muscles are called the rotator cuff muscles

Names of the four muscles

  • Supraspinatus
  • Infraspinatus
  • Teres minor
  • Subscapularis

4.3 Explaining the Ultrasound Findings (One by One)

  • “In your scan, I can see that u have full tear in the subscapularis muscle which means the Tear has gone through the entire thickness of the muscle. U have Partial tear in Supraspinatus & Infraspinatus muscle which means Only part of the muscle is torn
  • Teres minor muscle is Normal
    • Not torn

4.4 Explaining Full vs Partial Tears

  • Full tear
    • Muscle torn completely
  • Partial tear
    • Muscle torn partially
  • Ultrasound reports commonly use these terms
  • Explanation should match exact report wording

5. Transition to History Taking

Example Dialogue

  • “Is it okay if I ask you a few more questions?”
  • Patient agrees
  • This marks the transition to second task

6. Further History Taking

6.1 Opening Question (If Not Already Done)

  • If not done at the beginning, must do it now

Example

  • “Can you tell me a little bit more about your shoulder problem?”
  • “What has been going on so far?”

7. Pain Assessment

7.1 Characteristics of Pain (Full SIQORAA)

  • Site – front, side, or back
  • Intensity
  • Quality
  • Onset & duration – on/off, continuous
    Progression – getting worse
  • Radiation
  • Alleviating / Aggravating factors
  • Affecting life

7.2 Pattern & Functional Impact

  • Pain at night
  • Worse in:
    • Morning
    • End of the day
  • Worse with:
    • Activity
    • Exercise
    • Rest
  • Most important question:
    • “How is this affecting your life?”

8. Shoulder-Specific Questions

8.1 Stiffness & Movement

  • Any stiffness u have?
  • Any Difficulty or restriction in movement
  • Key emphasis:
    • Any Difficulty with overhead activities

8.2 Instability

  • “Have you noticed any instability in the shoulder?”
  • If no:
    • “Does your shoulder give away or pop out?”
  • “Giving away” = instability

9. Occupation & Sports

9.1 Occupation

  • Explore job role
  • “Do you perform any overhead activities at job”
  • Example:
    • Reaching cupboards
    • Lifting overhead

9.2 Sports

  • Ignore non-overhead sports (e.g. running)
  • Focus on sports involving overhead movement

10. Previous Injury or Trauma

  • Ask about:
    • Previous shoulder injuries
    • Trauma
  • In this case:
    • Usually no
  • If yes:
    • Describe incident
    • What happened after
    • Scans done
    • Treatment received

11. Chronic Pain Assessment

  • Ask about mood
  • Ask:
    • “How are you coping with the pain?”
  • Explore:
    • Support at home
    • Financial situation
    • Relationships at home

12. Why Candidates Kept Failing This Case

  • Despite full rotator cuff history, candidates failed repeatedly
  • Diagnosis was clear
  • Issue was case design, not candidate logic
  • Examiner expected ruling out other differentials

Additional Mandatory Component – Ruling Out Differentials

(This was the key to passing the case)

13.1 Polymyalgia Rheumatica

  • Especially in older patient
  • Ask about:
    • Hip pain
    • Tiredness
    • Stiffness
    • Headaches
    • Blurring of vision

13.2 Cervical Radiculopathy (C5)

  • Can present as shoulder pain
  • Ask about:
    • Neck pain
    • Weakness in arm movement
    • Numbness
    • Pins and needles

13.3 Osteoarthritis

  • Consider:
    • Post-traumatic osteoarthritis
    • Joint instability due to multiple muscle tears
  • Ask about:
    • Cracking sounds
    • Popping sounds
    • Have u noticed any Locking of the shoulder when u move it?

13.4 Rheumatoid Arthritis

  • Ask about:
    • Other joint pains
    • Generalized body pains
    • Family history of joint disease

13.5 Red Flags – Cardiac Cause

  • Especially with left shoulder pain
  • Ask about:
    • Chest pain
    • Shortness of breath

14. Final Teaching Point

  • This approach is not logically standard
  • Required only because of the way the case is designed
  • Extremely annoying, but necessary to pass

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: surgical-skills-expert (Australian surgical educator, FRACS) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The case describes the shoulder as 'a ball and socket joint' and attributes stability solely to the four rotator-cuff muscles. This is anatomically incomplete for patient explanation and reasoning.
The glenohumeral joint is a shallow ball-and-socket joint whose stability depends on both dynamic stabilisers (the four rotator-cuff muscles: supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis) and static stabilisers (the glenoid labrum, joint capsule and glenohumeral ligaments). Mentioning the labrum/capsule keeps the explanation accurate and consistent with the instability case (case 3).

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add an explicit management pathway (the station tests explanation + history but candidates should be ready to outline it): a symptomatic full-thickness subscapularis tear that has failed ~2 months of physiotherapy warrants referral to an orthopaedic/shoulder surgeon for consideration of surgical repair, whereas partial-thickness supraspinatus/infraspinatus tears are usually managed with continued structured physiotherapy, activity modification, simple analgesia (paracetamol +/- short-course topical NSAID) and a subacromial corticosteroid injection if impingement pain persists. Acute large traumatic cuff tears should be referred early.
⭐ Formalise the 'ruling out differentials' teaching into a clear red-flag checklist rather than framing it as an examiner quirk: polymyalgia rheumatica (older patient, girdle stiffness), C5 cervical radiculopathy (neck pain, dermatomal numbness, weakness), referred cardiac pain (left shoulder, exertional chest pain/dyspnoea) and Pancoast tumour are genuine not-to-be-missed causes of shoulder pain.
⭐ Add metadata: this is a counselling/explaining-results station with a second history-taking task; tag health-literacy communication (plain-language explanation of full vs partial tear matching the exact report wording) as a discriminating scoring domain.
