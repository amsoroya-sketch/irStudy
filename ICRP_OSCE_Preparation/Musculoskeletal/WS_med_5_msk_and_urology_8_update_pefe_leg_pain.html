Leg Pain - Structured Physical Examination from Examiner (PEFE)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med 5 MSK and urology / 3. Ankle, heel, foot (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: musculoskeletal

PEFE – LEG PAIN

WHY WE NEED A STRICT PEFE STRUCTURE

  • When asking Physical Examination from Examiner, you must ask specific questions.
  • If you don’t:
    • Examiner keeps asking:

      “What are you looking for?”
    • This wastes time and causes stress.
  • Time management is critical:
    • If you spend 4 minutes on PEFE and don’t reach differentials,

      → you fail the case.
  • You must be clever:
    • Use a nice structure
    • Know the game
    • Use the rules of the game against themselves
    • Design a clever structure

PEFE STRUCTURE – LEG PAIN

1. GENERAL APPEARANCE

Ask:

  • Is the patient in pain or distress?
  • Is the patient alert or drowsy?

Why alert or drowsy?

Because cellulitis can give sepsis.

2. VITAL SIGNS

Temperature is a must

But as a habit, always ask:

  • Blood pressure
  • Pulse rate
  • Respiratory rate
  • Temperature

Why blood pressure and pulse?

  • Hypertension is a risk factor for PAD
  • Many cases do have hypertension

Always ask four vital signs as a habit.

3. LOWER LIMB – INSPECTION

You must ask two main things.

A. Look for Cellulitis

Ask:

  • Do I see any rash or redness?

If examiner says yes → ask:

  • Do I see any discharge?

(Cellulitis cases usually have discharge)

Is there any tenderness?

B. Look for Peripheral Vascular Disease

Ask:

  • Do I see any ulcers?
  • Do I see any hyperpigmentation?
  • Do I see pallor?

Pallor is the most specific for PAD

Hyperpigmentation is also asked because we think about arterial and venous disease

4. PALPATION

A. Temperature

Ask two separate things:

  1. Do I have a local rise of temperature?

    → for cellulitis
  2. Do I have cold peripheries?

    → for peripheral arterial disease

B. Tenderness

Ask:

  • Is there any tenderness in the ankle, leg, knee or hip?

C. Capillary Refill & Pulses

Ask:

  • Capillary refill time

Then pulses – you must be specific:

“I want to check the

– Dorsalis pedis

– Posterior tibial

– Popliteal

– Femoral artery”

In PAD:

  • Pulses are absent
  • Capillary refill may be delayed

5. MOVEMENT

Ask:

  • Is movement tender?
  • What is the range of movement of the joints?

This is done even if it is cellulitis or PAD

6. GAIT & BEARING WEIGHT (Optional but important)

You may ask in general appearance:

  • Can the patient bear weight?
  • Does he have an antalgic gait?

Bearing weight is more important.

7. SPECIAL TESTS (MOST IMPORTANT PART)

A. Berger’s Test

Ask:

“I would like to do Berger’s test.”

Examiner gives positive or negative.

If positive → go to the next step to check bruits

B. Bruits

Ask:

  • Femoral bruit
  • Carotid bruit
  • Aortic or renal artery bruit

AMC often gives:

  • Positive femoral artery bruit

→ This confirms arterial disease

C. Neurological Examination (for New Case)

For neurogenic claudication

Ask:

“I would like to do a neurological examination of the lower limb.”

Focus on:

  • Motor
  • Sensory

You are looking for:

  • Neurological deficit

You do NOT need full tone, power, reflexes, coordination.

Because of time management.

D. Straight Leg Raise (SLR)

Used for:

  • Lumbar radiculopathy causing leg pain

8. OFFICE TESTS

If:

  • Patient is diabetic
  • Or you find sensory loss

→ Ask for:

  • Blood sugar level

If someone asks:

  • ECG for PAD → allowed but excessive
  • Ankle Brachial Index → unavailable

    (AMC considers it an investigation, not office test)

FINAL DIAGNOSTIC LOGIC

Cellulitis

  • Rash
  • Painful
  • Discharge
  • Febrile

PAD + Cellulitis (Mixed Case)

  • Absent pulses
  • Delayed capillary refill
  • Positive Berger’s test
  • Rash with discharge
  • Tenderness

Neurogenic Claudication

  • Pulses normal
  • Berger’s test normal
  • No rash
  • Neurological deficit
    • Sensory loss
    • Motor weakness

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: surgical-skills-expert (Australian surgical educator, RACS/MSK focus) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ The special test is called 'Berger's test' throughout.
The correct eponym is Buerger's test (leg elevation to ~45 degrees producing pallor, then dependency producing rubor, indicating peripheral arterial insufficiency). 'Berger' risks confusion with Berger disease (IgA nephropathy). Correct the spelling in the PEFE script.
✏️ The case states Ankle Brachial Index is 'unavailable' because 'AMC considers it an investigation, not an office test'.
ABI measured with a sphygmomanometer and a handheld Doppler is a validated bedside/office test routinely performed in Australian general practice and is appropriate to request at the examination/office-test stage for suspected PAD. It is reasonable to ask for it as a bedside test; frame formal arterial duplex as the follow-on investigation.
✏️ Office tests for a diabetic/neuropathy limb are limited to 'blood sugar level'.
Add bedside neurovascular office tests: 10 g monofilament and 128 Hz tuning-fork vibration sense (peripheral neuropathy), capillary refill and Doppler pedal signals, and urinalysis; blood glucose/HbA1c and BSL are appropriate but should sit alongside these standard bedside diabetic-foot assessments.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add explicit interpretation thresholds to the PEFE so the trainee closes the loop: ABI < 0.9 = PAD, < 0.4 = critical ischaemia, > 1.3 = calcified/non-compressible vessels; delayed capillary refill (> 2 sec) and absent dorsalis pedis/posterior tibial pulses support PAD.
⭐ Add a safety/triage layer to 'general appearance and vital signs': explicitly screen for sepsis (SIRS/fever + tachycardia + hypotension) in the cellulitis pathway and for critical limb ischaemia, so the structured exam drives an escalation decision, not just a diagnosis.
⭐ Reframe 'use the rules of the game against themselves' into professional, patient-centred language for a college marking rubric (RACGP/AMC communication domain) - efficient, hypothesis-driven examination requests rather than 'gaming' the examiner.
