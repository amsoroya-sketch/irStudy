Neck Pain in a Breast Cancer Survivor - Suspected Spinal Metastasis (Version 2)

Source: Dr. Amir workshop — Med 5 MSK and urology / 1. MSK Spine (Aug 2026 drop) · Specialty: musculoskeletal

OSCE – Neck Pain

Case Stem

48 year old lady comes to you complaining of neck pain. She is also complaining of tingling and numbness in her hand. She is a known case of breast cancer who was treated with bilateral mastectomy with axillary clearance and radiotherapy. She is currently on tamoxifen.

Task:

  • Take history
  • Explain your diagnosis and differentials to the patient
  • Request further investigations
  • Discuss prognosis with patient

First Thinking Step – How to Approach the Stem

“With just seeing the stem, if you’re a good doctor, what is the first thing that you’ll think about?”

  • Answer: Metastasis
  • The case is designed this way
  • Patient has breast cancer → first concern is metastasis

Core Concept of the Case

This is still:

Cervical radiculopathy

But the cause is most likely:

Metastasis to the spine

Radiculopathy can be caused by:

  • Disc prolapse
  • Tumor / metastasis pressing on nerve root

This case =

Cervical radiculopathy secondary to spinal metastasis

Differentials in This Case

Standard Neck Pain Differentials

  • Spine causes
  • Red flags
  • Arthritis
  • Referred pain

Context-Specific Differentials (Breast Cancer)

Add because of the stem:

  • Radiation-induced neuropathy
  • Surgical nerve trauma
  • Chemotherapy-induced neuropathy
  • Lymphedema (post axillary clearance)

History Taking Framework

1. Opening

Doctor:

“Can you tell me more about your pain?”

“How can I help you today?”

Patient example:

“I’ve had pain at the back of my neck for a few days. It’s getting worse. I’m worried.”

Acknowledge concern.

Offer pain relief if needed.

2. Pain Exploration – SIQORAA

Ask exactly as before:

  • S – Site
  • I – Intensity
  • Q – Quality (dull, sharp)
  • O – Onset
  • R – Radiation
  • A – Aggravating / Alleviating

Patient may say:

“The pain radiates to my arm.”

Ask about:

  • Worse with coughing or sneezing
  • Effect on life

Key Question (Do not forget)

  • Pattern of pain
    • Night pain
    • Worse in morning
    • Worse with activity or rest

3. Breast Cancer History (Use the Clue)

Because the stem highlights cancer, start here.

Ask:

  1. When were you diagnosed?
  2. Treatment adherence
    • Are you taking tamoxifen regularly? (tamoxifen usually have lots of side-effects, and people usually gives up.)
  3. Follow-up
    • Are you having regular GP / specialist visits?
    • When was your last scan done?
  4. Metastasis symptoms
    • Weight loss
    • Loss of appetite
    • Lumps or bumps (especially neck)
    • Tiredness
    • Cough (lung)
    • Headache / early morning vomiting (brain)

4. Axillary Clearance Complications

Ask about:

  • Lymphedema
    • Any swelling in arms
    • Skin changes in arm?

Differential questions

5. Radiculopathy Check

Because numbness is present:

“Have you noticed any weakness in moving your arms?”

6. Mechanical Neck Pain

Still rule out:

  • Occupation
  • Repetitive neck movement
  • Excessive exercise

7. Arthritis

Ask about:

  • OA
  • RA
  • Reactive arthritis

8. Referred Pain

Ask about:

  • Chest pain
  • Shortness of breath
  • Headache
  • Fever or chills
  • Shoulder pain

9. Cervical Myelopathy

Ask:

  • Leg weakness and numbness
  • Walking difficulty
  • Loss of bladder or bowel control

Structure Summary

Same neck pain structure

→ Reordered because breast cancer is a major clue

Diagnosis Explanation

Even if there is:

  • No weight loss
  • No appetite loss
  • No lumps

You still conclude:

Diagnosis

Cervical radiculopathy

Cause:

Most likely due to metastasis from breast cancer to the spine

How to Explain to Patient

“You most likely have a condition called cervical radiculopathy, which means there is pressure on the nerves coming out of your neck.”

“In your case, I am most concerned that this pressure is being caused by a spread of your breast cancer to the spine.”

If unsure:

“I am mostly concerned that this may be due to a spread of your breast cancer.”

Differentials (As Stated)

Top ones:

  • Metastasis
  • Chemotherapy-induced neuropathy
  • Radiotherapy-induced neuropathy
  • Lymphedema

Other neck pain differentials:

  • Other malignancies
  • Infections
  • Cervical myelopathy
  • Osteoarthritis
  • Rheumatoid arthritis
  • Referred pain from head (tension headache, brain metastasis)
  • Ischemic heart disease
  • Shoulder problems

Investigations

The Two That Decide Pass or Fail

You must say:

  1. MRI
  2. PET scan / Bone scan

These are to look for metastasis

Other Investigations (Can Mention)

  • Cervical spine X-ray
  • CA 15-3, Tumor markers
  • ESR
  • CRP
  • Rheumatoid factor

Why Each Test

  • MRI
    • Soft tissue
    • Nerve roots
    • Foraminal narrowing
    • Radiculopathy
  • PET / Bone scan
    • Looks for cancer spread in body
  • X-ray
    • Looks at bony spine
    • Compression fractures
    • Bone lesions
  • Tumor markers
    • Breast cancer
  • ESR / CRP
    • Inflammatory arthritis

Prognosis

Because diagnosis is not confirmed, keep both options open.

If it is Metastasis

“If this is a spread of the cancer to the spine, the outcome is not so good.”

  • Treatment would focus on:
    • Symptom control
    • Chemotherapy
    • Palliative care

If it is Disc-related Radiculopathy

“This has a good prognosis.”

  • Painkillers
  • Physiotherapy
  • Regular follow-up

Why Cancer Is a Red Flag

If a patient has:

  • Neck pain or back pain
  • With a past history of cancer

You must first think:

Metastasis

You cannot assume it is musculoskeletal without imaging.

History of cancer + neck pain = think metastasis first

Investigate → confirm → then decide prognosis

🔬 Expert Review & Enhancements

Reviewed by: surgical-skills-expert (Australian surgical educator, RACS/FRACS, MSK focus) · fact-checked against the irStudy medical textbook RAG index.

✏️ Corrections

✏️ In a breast-cancer patient with new neck pain plus arm neurology, the case frames the work-up as routine 'MRI and PET/bone scan to look for metastasis', missing the emergency.
New neurological symptoms with suspected spinal metastasis raise metastatic spinal cord/nerve-root compression (MSCC) - an oncological EMERGENCY. Prioritise URGENT whole-spine MRI (same day, within 24 h), commence high-dose dexamethasone (e.g. 16 mg daily with PPI cover) if compression is suspected, and make an urgent referral to radiation oncology and spinal surgery. Do NOT wait for an outpatient PET or bone scan before excluding cord compression.
✏️ Prognosis is stated as 'if this is a spread of the cancer to the spine, the outcome is not so good' with treatment reduced to 'symptom control, chemotherapy, palliative care'.
This is overly pessimistic and premature. Bone-predominant metastatic breast cancer frequently follows a prolonged course (often years) with good disease control. Management centres on switching/optimising systemic endocrine therapy (e.g. moving from tamoxifen to an aromatase inhibitor +/- a CDK4/6 inhibitor if progressing), bone-modifying agents (denosumab or IV zoledronic acid, PBS-listed) with calcium/vitamin D, and palliative radiotherapy to symptomatic lesions - not blanket palliation. Avoid quoting a poor prognosis before histological confirmation and staging.
✏️ 'Chemotherapy-induced neuropathy' is listed as a top differential, but the stem records only mastectomy, axillary clearance, radiotherapy and tamoxifen - no chemotherapy.
Confirm a chemotherapy history before invoking chemotherapy-induced neuropathy; chemo-induced peripheral neuropathy is also typically symmetrical glove-and-stocking, not unilateral radicular arm pain. The more fitting treatment-related differential here is radiation-induced brachial plexopathy following axillary radiotherapy - name it specifically.
✏️ CA 15-3 tumour markers are listed among diagnostic investigations.
CA 15-3 is used for monitoring known breast-cancer disease activity/response, not for diagnosing metastasis; it lacks the sensitivity/specificity to confirm or exclude bony spread. Present it as a monitoring adjunct, not a diagnostic test.

⭐ Suggested Enhancements

⭐ Add an explicit MSCC safety pathway (urgent whole-spine MRI within 24 h, dexamethasone, urgent radiation-oncology/spinal-surgery referral, catheter/bladder assessment) - this is the single most important upgrade given a cancer patient with neck pain and limb neurology.
⭐ Add a physical-examination block (this version has none): dermatomal sensory testing, myotomal power, upper- and lower-limb reflexes, UMN signs for myelopathy (Hoffmann, Babinski, clonus) and gait/Romberg - the case jumps from history to investigations without an examination.
⭐ Add radiation-induced brachial plexopathy as a named differential (relevant after axillary radiotherapy) alongside surgical nerve trauma and lymphoedema, and give bedside discriminators from a C6/C7 root lesion.
⭐ Structure the 'discuss prognosis' task around a breaking-bad-news framework (SPIKES): check understanding, warning shot, avoid committing to prognosis before tissue diagnosis/staging, and arrange multidisciplinary follow-up - the current script commits to prognosis too early.
⭐ Add carpal tunnel syndrome as a benign differential for the hand tingling (Phalen/Tinel) so the candidate does not anchor solely on metastasis, and tag this as the 'high-stakes' randomised counterpart to Version 1 (benign radiculopathy).
