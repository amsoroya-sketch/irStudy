CASE 1: CHILDHOOD ECZEMA (Atopic Dermatitis) ⭐⭐⭐

Frequency: HIGH-YIELD - 15-20% of AMC exams
Duration: 8 minutes
Difficulty: Foundation
Setting: General Practice, Young Medical Centre, NSW

CANDIDATE INSTRUCTIONS

Patient: Emma Chen, 6-year-old girl brought in by mother

Task: You have 8 minutes to:

  1. Take a focused history of Emma's skin complaint from her mother
  2. Examine the affected areas (simulated - examiner will describe findings)
  3. Discuss your provisional diagnosis and initial management plan with the mother

Assessment Criteria: Systematic history-taking (SKIN framework), Appropriate examination approach (5 Ps), Differential diagnosis, Management plan (Australian context), Communication with parent

PATIENT SCRIPT (Mother - Mrs. Chen, 35F)

Opening Statement:

"I'm worried about Emma's skin. She's had dry, itchy patches on her arms and behind her knees for about 6 months now. It's getting worse, and she's scratching all night - can't sleep properly."

If asked about SITE:

  • Started on the creases of her elbows about 6 months ago
  • Then appeared behind her knees
  • Now also on her wrists and neck
  • Face occasionally gets red and dry

If asked about KIND/Character:

  • Red patches, very dry and scaly
  • Sometimes it weeps a little bit (yellowish fluid)
  • Scratch marks all over (she can't stop scratching)
  • Skin looks thickened in some areas (chronic scratching)

If asked about ITCH:

  • VERY itchy - "She's constantly scratching"
  • Much worse at night (wakes up 3-4 times scratching)
  • Better during day when distracted at school
  • Sometimes scratches until it bleeds

If asked about Atopic History:

  • Has had sneezing/runny nose episodes (hay fever) since age 3
  • Gets wheezy sometimes with exercise (mild asthma - uses Ventolin occasionally)
  • Atopic triad present: Eczema + Asthma + Hay fever

Family History:

  • Mother has eczema (had it as a child, still gets flares on hands)
  • Father has hay fever
  • Older brother (age 10) has asthma

EXAMINATION FINDINGS (Examiner Describes)

Distribution:

Flexural pattern (classic atopic eczema):

  • Bilateral antecubital fossae (elbow creases)
  • Bilateral popliteal fossae (behind knees)
  • Wrists (flexor surfaces)
  • Neck (posterior)

Morphology:

  • Acute changes: Erythematous patches, small vesicles (some weeping), excoriations (scratch marks)
  • Chronic changes: Lichenification (thickened, leathery skin) in antecubital fossae
  • Dry, scaly skin in affected areas
  • Some areas with honey-colored crusting (suggests secondary Staph aureus infection)

SAMPLE GOOD ANSWER

Differential Diagnosis:

"Based on Emma's history and examination, my top diagnosis is atopic dermatitis (eczema). The key features supporting this are:

  1. Flexural distribution (elbows, knees, wrists, neck) - classic for atopic eczema
  2. Very itchy rash, worse at night
  3. Chronic relapsing nature (6 months)
  4. Personal history of atopy (asthma, hay fever)
  5. Strong family history (mother has eczema, father hay fever, brother asthma)
  6. Age of onset (early childhood)

The honey-colored crusting suggests secondary Staph aureus infection (impetiginization), which is common in eczema."

Management Plan:

"Mrs. Chen, Emma has atopic eczema, which is a very common skin condition in children. It's caused by a problem with the skin barrier - her skin doesn't hold moisture well. With proper treatment, we can control it well.

1. Moisturizers (MOST IMPORTANT):

  • Recommend QV Cream or Cetaphil (PBS-subsidized)
  • Apply 3-4 times daily - the more the better
  • Apply within 3 minutes after bath ("soak and seal")
  • Use 250-500g per week

2. Topical Corticosteroids:

  • Hydrocortisone 1% (mild) for face and neck - apply once daily for 5-7 days
  • Betamethasone valerate 0.05% (Betnovate - moderate) for body - apply once daily for 5-7 days
  • PBS-subsidized (~$7 per script)
  • Apply steroid first, wait 30 minutes, then moisturizer

3. Infection Treatment:

  • Honey crusting = infection
  • Flucloxacillin syrup 125mg QID for 5 days (PBS)

4. Help with Sleep:

  • Promethazine (Phenergan) syrup 5mg at bedtime
  • Sedating antihistamine - helps sleep and reduces scratching

5. Trigger Avoidance:

  • Fragrance-free laundry detergent
  • Lukewarm baths (not hot)
  • Avoid wool clothing (cotton best)
  • Keep fingernails short

Follow-up: GP review in 2 weeks. If not better, dermatology referral.

Red flags: Return if fever, painful blisters (herpes infection), or worsening despite treatment."

CASE 2: PSORIASIS - NEW DIAGNOSIS ⭐⭐⭐

Frequency: HIGH-YIELD - 15-20% of AMC exams
Duration: 8 minutes
Difficulty: Intermediate
Key Focus: Extensor distribution, nail changes, psoriatic arthritis screening

CANDIDATE INSTRUCTIONS

Patient: David Williams, 28-year-old male

Task: Take focused history, examine affected areas, formulate diagnosis and management plan

Key Assessment: Recognition of psoriasis (morphology, distribution), Psoriatic arthritis screening, Appropriate PBS medications

PATIENT SCRIPT (David Williams, 28M)

Opening Statement:

"I've had these red, scaly patches on my elbows and knees for about 3 months. Moisturizer isn't helping. It's embarrassing - I play footy and the other guys keep asking about it."

Key History Points:

  • Distribution: Elbows (outer), knees (outer), lower back, scalp
  • Morphology: Red raised patches, silvery-white scales, well-defined edges
  • Itch: Not very itchy (3/10)
  • Triggers: New high-stress job 4 months ago, increased alcohol (10-15 drinks/week)
  • Joint pain: Fingers stiff in morning (30 minutes), right ankle sore after footy
  • Family history: Father has psoriasis (diagnosed in 30s)

EXAMINATION FINDINGS

Distribution:

  • EXTENSOR surfaces (classic psoriasis): Bilateral elbows, bilateral knees
  • Lower back (sacral area)
  • Scalp (posterior)

Morphology:

  • Well-demarcated erythematous plaques (2-5cm diameter)
  • Silvery-white scale on surface
  • Plaques are raised, firm, slightly warm

Nails (IMPORTANT - 50% psoriasis patients):

  • Pitting (small depressions) on fingernails - both hands
  • Onycholysis (nail separation) - right index finger

Joints (Psoriatic Arthritis Screening):

  • PIPs (2nd, 3rd fingers bilaterally): Mild swelling, tender to palpation
  • Right ankle: Mild swelling
  • Early psoriatic arthritis present

SAMPLE GOOD ANSWER

Diagnosis:

"David, you have psoriasis with early psoriatic arthritis.

Key Features:

  1. Extensor surfaces (elbows, knees) - classic for psoriasis
  2. Well-demarcated plaques with silvery-white scale
  3. Nail changes (pitting, onycholysis)
  4. Family history (father has psoriasis)
  5. Triggers (stress, alcohol)
  6. Joint involvement (PIPs swollen, morning stiffness) → psoriatic arthritis

Management Plan:

1. Topical Therapies (First-line):

  • Daivobet ointment (calcipotriol/betamethasone combination) - PBS (~$7)
  • Apply once daily to patches
  • Very effective for plaque psoriasis

2. Scalp Psoriasis:

  • Bettamousse (betamethasone foam) - PBS
  • Apply 2-3 times per week

3. Lifestyle Changes:

  • Reduce alcohol: Trigger - try <10 standard drinks/week
  • Stress management: Major trigger
  • Sun exposure: 10-15 minutes daily (helps psoriasis)
  • NOT tanning beds (skin cancer risk)

4. Psoriatic Arthritis:

URGENT rheumatology referral - joint pain is psoriatic arthritis (1 in 4 develop this)

  • Early treatment prevents joint damage
  • May need methotrexate (treats both skin and joints)

5. When to Consider Stronger Treatment:

  • If creams don't work after 6-8 weeks
  • If psoriasis >10% body surface area
  • Options: Phototherapy (UV light), Methotrexate, Biologics (PBS-subsidized if severe)

Prognosis: Chronic condition, comes and goes (flares and remissions). Cannot cure, but can control well. Many patients improve with treatment + lifestyle changes.

CASE 3: CELLULITIS vs DVT (Acute Red Leg) ⭐⭐⭐

Critical Differential: Differentiating cellulitis from DVT is high-stakes clinical decision

Key Features: Portal of entry (tinea pedis), fever/chills (cellulitis), Wells score (DVT risk assessment)

Management: IV flucloxacillin + benzylpenicillin, treat tinea pedis to prevent recurrence

CASE 15: MENINGOCOCCAL RASH (EMERGENCY) ⭐⭐⭐

⚠️ EMERGENCY STATION - DON'T MISS

Scenario: 4-year-old with fever and non-blanching purpuric rash (48 hours)

Critical Actions:

  • Perform blanching test → Non-blanching = EMERGENCY
  • IM/IV Benzylpenicillin STAT (1.2g or 50mg/kg in children)
  • Call 000 - urgent transfer to hospital
  • Notifiable disease - inform public health
  • Contact prophylaxis - rifampicin for household

Don't wait for investigations - Give antibiotics before transfer
