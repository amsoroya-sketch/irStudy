## CASE 1: CHILDHOOD ECZEMA (Atopic Dermatitis) ⭐⭐⭐

**Frequency**: HIGH-YIELD - Appears in 15-20% of AMC Clinical exams
**Duration**: 8 minutes
**Difficulty**: Foundation

---

### CANDIDATE INSTRUCTIONS (Posted outside station)

**Clinical Setting**: General Practice, Young Medical Centre, NSW

**Patient**: Emma Chen, 6-year-old girl brought in by mother

**Task**: You have 8 minutes to:
1. Take a focused history of Emma's skin complaint from her mother
2. Examine the affected areas (simulated - examiner will describe findings)
3. Discuss your provisional diagnosis and initial management plan with the mother

You will be assessed on:
- Systematic history-taking (SKIN framework)
- Appropriate examination approach (5 Ps)
- Differential diagnosis
- Management plan (Australian context)
- Communication with parent

---

### PATIENT SCRIPT (Mother - Mrs. Chen, 35F)

**Opening Statement**:
"I'm worried about Emma's skin. She's had dry, itchy patches on her arms and behind her knees for about 6 months now. It's getting worse, and she's scratching all night - can't sleep properly."

**If asked about SITE**:
- Started on the creases of her elbows about 6 months ago
- Then appeared behind her knees
- Now also on her wrists and neck
- Face occasionally gets red and dry

**If asked about KIND/Character**:
- Red patches, very dry and scaly
- Sometimes it weeps a little bit (yellowish fluid)
- Scratch marks all over (she can't stop scratching)
- Skin looks thickened in some areas (chronic scratching)

**If asked about ITCH**:
- VERY itchy - "She's constantly scratching"
- Much worse at night (wakes up 3-4 times scratching)
- Better during day when distracted at school
- Sometimes scratches until it bleeds

**If asked about NEW exposures/triggers**:
- No new medications
- Recently started using a new laundry detergent (2 months ago) - mother wonders if related
- No new pets, foods, or soaps
- Started kindergarten 6 months ago (around when eczema began)

**If asked about Associated Symptoms**:
- No fever, weight loss, or other symptoms
- Has had sneezing/runny nose episodes (hay fever) since age 3
- Gets wheezy sometimes with exercise (mild asthma - uses Ventolin occasionally)

**If asked about Past Medical History**:
- Born at term, normal delivery
- **Atopic history**: Mild asthma (diagnosed age 4), hay fever (seasonal)
- No previous skin problems as a baby
- Immunizations up to date

**If asked about Medications**:
- Salbutamol (Ventolin) inhaler - uses occasionally for wheeze
- Recently tried "some cream from the chemist" (aqueous cream) - didn't help
- No allergies to medications

**If asked about Family History**:
- Mother has eczema (had it as a child, still gets flares on hands)
- Father has hay fever
- Older brother (age 10) has asthma

**If asked about Social History**:
- Lives with both parents and older brother
- Attends kindergarten 5 days/week
- Mother works part-time as teacher, father is engineer
- Family has 2 cats (had them for years, before eczema started)

**If asked about Impact**:
- Missing sleep (wakes scratching)
- Embarrassed at school (kids ask about her red skin)
- Mother has tried moisturizers but Emma resists (says it stings)

**Questions Mother Might Ask**:
1. "Is this eczema? Will she grow out of it?"
2. "I've heard steroid creams are dangerous - will they make her skin thin?"
3. "Could it be the cats causing this?"
4. "What can I do to help her sleep - she's so tired from scratching all night?"

### EXAMINATION FINDINGS (Examiner Describes)

**When candidate says "I'd like to examine Emma's skin now":**

**General Appearance**:
- 6-year-old girl, appears stated age
- Alert, interactive, scratching arms during consultation
- No acute distress

**Distribution**:
- **Flexural pattern** (classic atopic eczema):
  - Bilateral antecubital fossae (elbow creases)
  - Bilateral popliteal fossae (behind knees)
  - Wrists (flexor surfaces)
  - Neck (posterior)
- Face: Mild periorbital dryness

**Morphology**:
- **Acute changes**: Erythematous patches, small vesicles (some weeping), excoriations (scratch marks)
- **Chronic changes**: Lichenification (thickened, leathery skin) in antecubital fossae (chronic scratching)
- Dry, scaly skin in affected areas
- Some areas with **honey-colored crusting** (suggests secondary Staph aureus infection)

**Palpation**:
- Skin feels warm (inflammation)
- Rough, scaly texture
- No tenderness to touch

**Other Systems**:
- Nails: Short (mother keeps them trimmed to reduce damage from scratching)
- No lymphadenopathy
- Chest: Clear breath sounds, no wheeze at rest

### SAMPLE GOOD ANSWER

**Differential Diagnosis**:
"Based on Emma's history and examination, my top diagnosis is **atopic dermatitis (eczema)**. The key features supporting this are:
1. Flexural distribution (elbows, knees, wrists, neck) - classic for atopic eczema
2. Very itchy rash, worse at night
3. Chronic relapsing nature (6 months)
4. Personal history of atopy (asthma, hay fever)
5. Strong family history (mother has eczema, father hay fever, brother asthma)
6. Age of onset (early childhood - 60% start before age 1, most by age 5) (Murtagh's General Practice, 8th ed, p.2279)

The honey-colored crusting suggests **secondary Staph aureus infection** (impetiginization), which is common in eczema.

Differentials to consider:
- **Contact dermatitis**: Possible (new laundry detergent), but distribution is more generalized than expected for contact
- **Scabies**: Very itchy, but distribution wrong (scabies is interdigital, wrists, not flexures)
- **Psoriasis**: Wrong distribution (psoriasis is extensor surfaces - elbows/knees, not flexures)

I'd reassure Mrs. Chen this is not due to poor hygiene or the cats (had cats before eczema started)."

**Management Plan**:
"Mrs. Chen, Emma has **atopic eczema**, which is a very common skin condition in children. It's caused by a problem with the skin barrier - her skin doesn't hold moisture well, which makes it dry and itchy. The good news is, with proper treatment, we can control it well, and many children improve as they get older.

**1. Moisturizers (most important):**
I'll recommend **QV Cream** or **Cetaphil** (available from pharmacy, partially subsidized). Apply this **3-4 times daily** - the more the better. The key is to apply within 3 minutes after bath (called 'soak and seal'). Use lots - 250-500g per week for a child. The aqueous cream you tried can actually irritate eczema, so avoid that.

**2. Steroid creams:**
I know you're concerned about steroids, but when used correctly, they're very safe and essential for controlling eczema flares. I'll prescribe two strengths:
- **Hydrocortisone 1%** (mild) for her face and neck - apply once daily for 5-7 days (Murtagh's General Practice, 8th ed, p.2918)
- **Betamethasone valerate 0.05%** (Betnovate - moderate) for her body (arms, legs) - apply once daily for 5-7 days (Murtagh's General Practice, 8th ed, p.1761)

These are PBS-subsidized (about $7 per script). Apply the steroid cream FIRST to red areas, wait 30 minutes, then apply moisturizer all over. As the redness improves, reduce to every second day, then twice weekly. This prevents skin thinning.

**3. Infection treatment:**
The honey-colored crusting suggests infection. I'll prescribe **Flucloxacillin syrup** 125mg four times daily for 5 days (PBS-subsidized). This will clear the infection. (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)

**4. Help with sleep:**
For the night-time scratching, I'll prescribe **Promethazine (Phenergan) syrup** 5mg at bedtime. This is a sedating antihistamine that will help her sleep and reduce scratching. Use only on bad nights (not every night long-term).

**5. Trigger avoidance:**
- Change to fragrance-free laundry detergent (Omo Sensitive, Cold Power Sensitive)
- Lukewarm baths (not hot - hot water strips skin oils)
- Pat dry (don't rub), apply moisturizer immediately
- Avoid wool clothing (cotton is best)
- Keep fingernails short
- The cats are unlikely the cause since you had them before eczema started

**6. Follow-up:**
Please see me or another GP in 2 weeks to see how she's responding. If it's not better, I'll refer her to a dermatologist.

**Red flags - please return if:**
- Fever develops (infection spreading)
- Painful blisters appear (possible herpes infection - needs urgent treatment)
- Worsening despite treatment

Do you have any questions?"

### SAMPLE GOOD ANSWER

**Differential Diagnosis**:
"Based on David's history and examination, my diagnosis is **chronic plaque psoriasis** with **early psoriatic arthritis**.

Key features:
1. **Distribution**: Extensor surfaces (elbows, knees) - classic for psoriasis
2. **Morphology**: Well-demarcated erythematous plaques with silvery-white scale
3. **Nail changes**: Pitting, onycholysis (50% psoriasis patients) (Murtagh's General Practice, 8th ed, p.3137)
4. **Koebner phenomenon**: New lesion at trauma site
5. **Family history**: Father has psoriasis (30% if one parent affected) (Murtagh's General Practice, 8th ed, p.2937)
6. **Triggers**: Recent stress (new job), increased alcohol

The **joint involvement** (PIP swelling, morning stiffness) suggests **psoriatic arthritis** (10-30% of psoriasis patients develop this). This is important to identify early because treatment can prevent permanent joint damage." (Murtagh's General Practice, 8th ed, p.2938)

**Management Plan**:
"David, you have **psoriasis**, which is a chronic skin condition caused by your immune system being overactive. The good news is it's **not contagious** - you can't spread it to others, so don't worry about the guys at footy. Yes, it is hereditary - your dad's psoriasis increases your risk, and it usually appears in your 20s-30s.

**Will it spread?**
It can vary - some people have just a few patches, others more widespread. We can control it with treatment, but it's a chronic condition that tends to come and go (flares and remissions). Stress and alcohol are big triggers, so managing those will help.

**Treatment plan:**

**1. Topical creams (first-line):** (Murtagh's General Practice, 8th ed, p.711)
I'll prescribe **Daivobet ointment** (combination of steroid + vitamin D cream) - PBS-subsidized (about $7). Apply once daily to the patches. This is very effective for plaque psoriasis.

For your **scalp**, I'll prescribe **Bettamousse** (betamethasone foam) - easier to apply to scalp than cream. Use 2-3 times per week.

Your **nails** are harder to treat with creams - may need stronger treatment if they bother you.

**2. Lifestyle changes (important):**
- **Reduce alcohol**: Alcohol makes psoriasis worse. Try to cut down to <10 standard drinks per week.
- **Stress management**: Your job stress is a trigger. Consider stress-reduction strategies (exercise, mindfulness, etc.)
- **Sun exposure**: YES, sun helps psoriasis (UV light suppresses immune system). 10-15 minutes daily sun (but sunscreen on unaffected areas - we don't want skin cancer). **Don't use tanning beds** - they're dangerous (skin cancer risk).

**3. Joint problem:**
The finger and ankle pain you mentioned is likely **psoriatic arthritis** - about 1 in 4 people with psoriasis develop this. I'll refer you to a **rheumatologist** (joint specialist) for assessment. Early treatment is important to prevent joint damage. They may start you on **methotrexate** (tablet once weekly) which treats both the skin and joints.

**4. When to consider stronger treatment:**
If the creams don't work after 6-8 weeks, or if your psoriasis covers >10% of your body, we can try: (Murtagh's General Practice, 8th ed, p.2918)
- **Phototherapy** (UV light treatment at hospital)
- **Systemic medications** (methotrexate, acitretin)
- **Biologics** (injectable medications - very effective, PBS-subsidized if severe)

**Follow-up:**
- See me in 6 weeks to assess response
- I'll book you rheumatology appointment (may be 2-3 month wait in public system)

**Red flags** - return if:
- Joints become very painful or swollen
- Psoriasis spreads rapidly despite treatment

Any questions?"

## CASE 3: CELLULITIS vs DVT (Acute Red Leg) ⭐⭐⭐

**Frequency**: HIGH-YIELD - Appears in 20-25% of AMC Clinical exams
**Duration**: 8 minutes
**Difficulty**: Advanced (critical differential)

---

### CANDIDATE INSTRUCTIONS

**Clinical Setting**: Emergency Department, Young District Hospital, NSW

**Patient**: Margaret Thompson, 68-year-old female

**Task**: You have 8 minutes to:
1. Take a focused history of Margaret's leg problem
2. Examine the affected leg (simulated - examiner will describe findings)
3. Formulate differential diagnosis (CRITICAL: cellulitis vs DVT)
4. Outline initial management and investigations

You will be assessed on:
- Differentiating cellulitis from DVT (high-stakes clinical decision)
- Systematic examination
- Appropriate investigations (D-dimer, Doppler ultrasound)
- Management (empirical antibiotics vs anticoagulation)

---

### PATIENT SCRIPT (Margaret Thompson, 68F)

**Opening Statement**:
"My right leg has been red, swollen, and painful for the last 2 days. It's getting worse - now I can barely walk on it. I'm worried it might be a blood clot."

**If asked about SITE**:
- **Right leg** only (left leg normal)

- Now spreading up towards the knee
- **Foot also swollen**

**If asked about KIND/Character**:
- Red, hot, swollen
- Skin is tight and shiny
- Very painful to walk
- No rash or blisters

**If asked about Timeline**:
- Started 2 days ago (sudden onset)
- Woke up and noticed right leg was red and swollen
- **Getting worse** - redness spreading up leg, more painful
- No previous episodes

**If asked about NEW exposures/triggers**:
- **Portal of entry (CRITICAL for cellulitis)**:
  - Has had "athlete's foot" (tinea pedis) between toes for years
  - Right foot - between 4th and 5th toes is cracked and weepy
  - Didn't think much of it ("just dry skin")
- No recent injuries, insect bites, or trauma to leg
- No recent surgery

**If asked about DVT Risk Factors**:
- **Recent immobility**: NO - active, walks daily
- **Recent long flights**: NO - hasn't travelled in years
- **Recent surgery**: NO
- **Previous DVT/PE**: NO
- **Cancer**: NO
- **Hormone therapy**: NO (postmenopausal, no HRT)

**If asked about Associated Symptoms**:
- **Fever**: Yes, felt hot and shivery last night (didn't take temperature)
- **Chills**: Yes, had rigors (shaking) last night
- No chest pain, no shortness of breath (rules out PE)
- No cough, no other symptoms

**If asked about Past Medical History**:
- **Type 2 diabetes** (diagnosed 10 years ago) - on metformin
- **Hypertension** (on perindopril)
- **Obesity** (BMI ~35)
- No previous blood clots
- No heart disease

**If asked about Medications**:
- Metformin 1000mg BD (diabetes) (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)
- Perindopril 5mg daily (blood pressure) (Therapeutic Guidelines, 2024)
- **No anticoagulants**
- No known allergies

**If asked about Social History**:
- Lives with husband (both retired)
- Independent with ADLs
- Walks to shops daily (until leg problem started)
- Non-smoker
- No alcohol

**If asked about Impact**:
- Can't walk properly (too painful)
- Worried about blood clot (friend had DVT last year)
- Concerned about losing leg ("I've heard diabetes can cause that")

**Questions Patient Might Ask**:
1. "Is this a blood clot? Do I need blood thinners?"
2. "Could I lose my leg with diabetes?"
3. "My friend had a blood clot and had to go on warfarin - will I need that?"
4. "How long will I be in hospital?"

---

### EXAMINATION FINDINGS (Examiner Describes)

**Vital Signs** (CRITICAL for cellulitis vs DVT):
- **Temperature**: 38.2°C (fever - supports cellulitis)
- BP: 145/85 mmHg
- HR: 92 bpm
- RR: 16/min
- SpO2: 97% on room air (Murtagh's General Practice, 8th ed, p.2402)

**General Appearance**:
- 68-year-old woman, appears uncomfortable
- Limping on right leg

**RIGHT LEG** (affected):
- **Erythema** (redness): From mid-shin extending up to below knee
- **Poorly demarcated border** (vs well-demarcated in erysipelas)
- **Swelling**: Entire lower leg swollen (foot to knee)
- **Warmth**: Warm to touch (compare to left leg - much warmer)
- **Tenderness**: Tender to palpation over shin area (maximum tenderness)
- **No calf tenderness** on squeezing (Homan's sign negative - but unreliable anyway)
- **No pitting oedema** in calf (swelling is inflammatory, not fluid)
- **Skin intact**: No breaks, ulcers, or visible wounds on leg
