# The AMC Clinical Examination: OSCE Structure, Marking, and the Thinking-Out-Loud Imperative

## Clinical Presentation

The AMC Clinical Examination uses the Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) format as its primary assessment tool for international medical graduates and Australian medical graduates seeking registration. Understanding the architecture of this examination is not merely administrative knowledge — it is foundational clinical strategy. Candidates who misunderstand how the OSCE is designed, marked, and scored consistently underperform regardless of their underlying clinical knowledge.

The OSCE is built around discrete clinical stations, each with a precisely defined predominant assessment area: history taking, physical examination, diagnostic formulation, counselling, or management planning. Every station is constructed from a specific clinical scenario — a 24-year-old woman with two months of headache, for example — but the examinable content is not the diagnosis itself. It is the structured, logical, demonstrable clinical process that leads to that diagnosis, and the quality with which the candidate communicates their reasoning aloud. This distinction — between knowing a diagnosis and demonstrating clinical competence — is the central conceptual challenge of the AMC Clinical Examination.

This station type appears across virtually every clinical domain. Whether the presenting complaint is headache, chest pain, abdominal pain, or a communication task such as explaining a new diagnosis, the underlying architecture remains consistent: a defined task, a specific mark sheet, and an examiner who is trained to assess the quality and logic of what is delivered — not what remains unspoken inside the candidate's mind.

---

## Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

Every AMC Clinical Examination station is engineered backwards from a mark sheet. The examiner does not score impressions or holistic feelings; they tick predetermined boxes that correspond to specific, observable candidate behaviours. Understanding this structure changes how a candidate prepares.

**Station Design Architecture:**
Each station begins with a topic selection (e.g., migraine, acute coronary syndrome, type 2 diabetes counselling), followed by a decision about the predominant assessment area. The case writer then constructs:
1. A candidate stem — the brief clinical scenario provided to the candidate outside the station door
2. A role player script — detailed instructions for the simulated patient, including their emotional state, the information they will volunteer or withhold, and a scripted concern (e.g., "My brother had a brain tumour and I am worried about the same thing")
3. A mark sheet — a structured list of specific, observable behaviours that the examiner scores in real time

**The Mark Sheet Structure:**
The mark sheet contains key points — non-negotiable clinical behaviours that must be demonstrated. In a headache history-taking station, these might include: systematic pain characterisation, screening for red flags of space-occupying lesion, enquiry into criteria for specific diagnoses such as migraine, and appropriate differential diagnosis formulation. The examiner scores each item on a numerical scale (typically one to seven, where four and above represents a pass), and the aggregate score determines the candidate's outcome for that station.

**Implication for Candidates:**
The candidate's task is not to arrive at the correct diagnosis — it is to visibly demonstrate the clinical reasoning process that a competent Australian medical practitioner would employ. This requires thinking out loud: verbalising differentials, explaining what has been excluded and why, and making the internal reasoning process externally visible. The examiner cannot access what remains unspoken; only what is delivered can be scored.

## Systematic Approach

### The Thinking-Out-Loud Principle

The single most important technical skill in the AMC Clinical Examination is the deliberate externalisation of clinical reasoning — communicating the thought process to the examiner and, in communication stations, to the simulated patient. This is not natural clinical behaviour. In daily clinical practice, a clinician silently considers and eliminates diagnoses, then presents a conclusion. In the OSCE environment, this internal process must be made audible and visible.

**What thinking out loud looks like in a headache history-taking station:**

Rather than asking about fever and silently noting its absence, the candidate states: *"I'm going to ask you some questions to help me rule out some serious causes of headache first. Do you have any fever or neck stiffness? I ask because I want to make sure there is no sign of meningitis or infection around the brain."* After receiving a negative answer: *"That's reassuring — it makes an infectious cause less likely."*

When concluding the history before presenting the differential: *"Based on what you've told me, I was concerned initially about some serious conditions — particularly a space-occupying lesion such as a brain tumour, given the two-month duration. However, you have no red flags: no morning vomiting, no visual changes, no focal neurological symptoms, no progressive worsening, no fever, and no meningism. I was also considering meningitis and sinusitis, which I've been able to largely exclude. The pattern you describe — unilateral, pulsating pain with nausea, photophobia, and a positive family history — is most consistent with migraine."*

This performance is deliberately more explicit than real-world clinical communication. It must be, because the mark sheet rewards visible reasoning, not correct conclusions alone.

**Key principles:**
- Verbalise differentials as they are being considered and excluded, not only at the end
- Name red flags as they are screened, and explain the clinical significance of each
- Signal transitions between sections of the history: *"I've finished asking about the pain itself — now I'd like to ask about some associated symptoms"*
- Narrate the diagnostic reasoning process as a coherent, sequential argument

### The Structured Clinical History — Using the Case of Headache

A well-constructed OSCE history follows a predictable architecture that can be adapted to any presenting complaint. The following framework uses headache as the model case.

**Opening and Agenda-Setting:**
Introduce yourself, confirm the patient's identity, and establish rapport. State the task briefly: *"I understand you've been having headaches for the past two months. I'd like to ask you some detailed questions to understand what's been happening — is that alright?"* Offer a brief agenda statement: *"I'll start by asking about the headaches themselves, then I'll ask some questions about your general health and other symptoms."*

**Systematic Pain Characterisation (SOCRATES):**
- **Site:** Unilateral or bilateral; frontal, temporal, occipital, or diffuse
- **Onset:** Sudden (thunderclap — critical red flag), gradual, or progressive
- **Character:** Throbbing/pulsating (migraine), pressure/band-like (tension), stabbing/lancinating (trigeminal neuralgia)
- **Radiation:** Neck, jaw, face, eye
- **Associated symptoms:** Nausea, vomiting, photophobia, phonophobia, aura (visual scotoma, fortification spectra, unilateral paraesthesia), lacrimation, rhinorrhoea, ptosis, conjunctival injection
- **Timing:** Duration of individual episodes, frequency, time of day, relationship to menstrual cycle, waking from sleep
- **Exacerbating and relieving factors:** Postural change, cough, exertion, bright light, noise, movement, rest, analgesia
- **Severity:** Numerical scale; functional impact — does the patient need to lie in a darkened room?

**Red Flag Screening (Space-Occupying Lesion and Other Must-Not-Miss Diagnoses):**
This section must be performed systematically and audibly. The candidate should explicitly state the purpose:

*"I now need to ask you some important questions to make sure there's nothing serious causing these headaches."*

Enquire about:
- Progressive worsening over weeks to months
- Morning headache worst on waking or worsened by Valsalva (raised intracranial pressure)
- Vomiting without nausea, particularly in the morning
- Visual changes: blurring, diplopia, visual field loss
- Focal neurological symptoms: limb weakness, speech difficulty, altered coordination, cognitive change
- Seizure activity
- Fever, neck stiffness, photophobia with systemic illness (meningitis/encephalitis)
- Thunderclap onset reaching maximum intensity within seconds (subarachnoid haemorrhage)
- Age over 50 with new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication (giant cell arteritis)
- History of malignancy, HIV, or immunosuppression (secondary headache from metastasis or opportunistic infection)
- Recent head trauma
- Unexplained weight loss

**Migraine-Specific Criteria:**
After excluding red flags, systematically establish the positive diagnostic criteria for migraine:
- Unilateral location (though bilateral does not exclude migraine)
- Pulsating quality
- Moderate to severe intensity impairing normal activity
- Worsening with physical activity
- Associated nausea or vomiting
- Photophobia and phonophobia
- Aura: typical visual, sensory, or speech symptoms lasting 20–60 minutes, fully reversible, preceding or accompanying headache
- Episode duration: 4–72 hours untreated
- Family history (first-degree relative with migraine)
- Triggers: hormonal fluctuation, oral contraceptive pill, stress, sleep disruption, dietary factors (caffeine withdrawal, alcohol, specific foods), dehydration, bright light, strong odour

**Relevant Medical, Social, and Medication History:**
- Current medications, particularly the combined oral contraceptive pill (important for both diagnosis and management — COC use in migraine with aura carries increased stroke risk and requires specific management decisions)
- Analgesic overuse (medication overuse headache if using simple analgesics more than 15 days per month or triptans more than 10 days per month)
- Caffeine intake and pattern
- Occupational and psychosocial stressors
- Sleep quality and quantity
- Alcohol and substance use
- Previous investigations (neuroimaging, etc.)
- Previous episodes and prior diagnoses

### The Counselling Station — Explaining a Diagnosis and Management Plan

When the predominant assessment area is counselling rather than history taking, the station architecture shifts. The stem provides the clinical context (diagnosis established, investigations completed), and the candidate's task is to explain, educate, and formulate a shared management plan.

**Using the same migraine scenario as a counselling station:**

The stem might read: *"A 24-year-old woman presents with a six-month history of headaches with nausea and photophobia. Her mother has migraine. She is on the combined oral contraceptive pill. MRI brain and neurological examination are normal. You have diagnosed migraine. Explain the diagnosis to the patient and counsel her regarding her management plan."*

**Counselling Framework — ICE-Centred Approach:**

**Opening and Establishing Prior Understanding:**
Begin by assessing what the patient already knows and believes: *"Before I explain what I've found, I'd like to understand what you've been thinking might be causing these headaches. Have you had any thoughts about it?"* This elicits the Ideas component of the ICE framework and prevents the common error of providing information that conflicts with unstated patient beliefs without addressing them.

**Eliciting Concerns:**
Directly and explicitly enquire about concerns: *"Is there anything specific you've been worried this might be?"* In the context of a patient whose brother had a brain tumour, this question unlocks the central concern that will dominate the consultation unless addressed early. The candidate who bypasses this and proceeds directly to the migraine explanation will fail to address the most clinically significant element of the case — the patient's unspoken fear.

**Acknowledging and Responding to Concerns:**
Once the concern is voiced — *"I was worried I might have a brain tumour like my brother"* — the response must be empathic before it is informative: *"That's a completely understandable concern, especially given what your family has been through. I can see why that would be on your mind."* Only then should the reassurance and explanation follow, with explicit reference to the investigations that have excluded the feared diagnosis: *"The good news is that the MRI of your brain has come back completely normal — there is no sign of a tumour or any other structural problem. The normal neurological examination supports this reassurance."*

**Eliciting Expectations:**
*"What were you hoping we would be able to do for you today?"* This addresses the Expectations component and allows the candidate to align the consultation's content with what the patient actually needs. A patient who expected a referral to a neurologist requires a different conversation to one who expected medication and reassurance.

**Explaining the Diagnosis in Plain Language:**
Avoid medical jargon. Explain migraine as a neurological condition involving abnormal brain activity that causes the characteristic pattern of symptoms. Use an analogy if helpful: *"Think of it as a kind of electrical storm in part of the brain — it causes the blood vessels to behave differently, which is why the headache tends to throb and why you feel nauseous and sensitive to light."* Check understanding after each segment: *"Does that make sense so far?"*

**Explain the Role of the Combined Oral Contraceptive Pill:**
This is a high-yield mark sheet item in this specific scenario. The COC pill is a recognised migraine trigger and, critically, migraine with aura in combination with COC use is associated with a significantly increased risk of ischaemic stroke. The candidate must address this clearly: *"I also need to discuss the contraceptive pill you're on. Oestrogen-containing pills can sometimes trigger or worsen migraines. More importantly, for women who experience migraines — particularly with aura — the combined pill slightly increases the risk of stroke. We need to discuss switching to a progestogen-only pill or an alternative form of contraception. I would also like to clarify whether you experience any warning symptoms before your headaches, as this affects this advice."*

### Differential Diagnosis Formulation — Thinking Aloud at the Station's End

At the conclusion of a history-taking station, the candidate is typically asked to provide a diagnosis and differential diagnosis list. This segment must reflect a structured, prioritised, clinically reasoned list — not a random enumeration of headache causes.

**Presenting the differential in OSCE style:**

*"Based on the history, the most likely diagnosis is migraine without aura. I say this because she meets multiple diagnostic criteria: the headaches are unilateral, pulsating, moderately severe, associated with nausea and photophobia, lasting 4–72 hours untreated, and she has a positive family history and a clear hormonal trigger related to her menstrual cycle.*

*My differential diagnoses, in order of likelihood, are: tension-type headache — possible, though the quality and severity are more consistent with migraine; medication overuse headache — I would need to clarify her analgesic use pattern; and cervicogenic headache. I have excluded the following serious conditions because she has no red flags: there is no progressive course, no focal neurology, no morning predominance, no fever or meningism, no thunderclap onset, and the MRI is normal, making space-occupying lesion, subarachnoid haemorrhage, and intracranial hypertension very unlikely."*

## Red Flags — Must Not Miss

- **Thunderclap onset (maximal intensity within seconds to minutes)** — Subarachnoid haemorrhage until proven otherwise; requires immediate CT brain and, if negative, lumbar puncture for xanthochromia; do not discharge without investigation
- **Progressive worsening over weeks** — Space-occupying lesion (primary brain tumour, metastasis, abscess, subdural haematoma); requires urgent neuroimaging
- **Morning predominance with vomiting** — Raised intracranial pressure; supine position increases ICP overnight; associated with space-occupying lesion or idiopathic intracranial hypertension
- **Focal neurological deficit** — Motor weakness, speech disturbance, diplopia, visual field loss, ataxia — indicates structural pathology; requires urgent MRI
- **Fever with neck stiffness and photophobia** — Bacterial meningitis or viral encephalitis; medical emergency requiring immediate blood cultures, LP if safe, and empirical antibiotics and aciclovir
- **New headache in patient over 50** — Giant cell arteritis in differential; check ESR, CRP, temporal artery tenderness; risk of irreversible visual loss if untreated
- **Headache in immunocompromised patient** — Opportunistic infection (cryptococcal meningitis, toxoplasmosis, CMV encephalitis); requires LP and specific investigation
- **Worsening with Valsalva (cough, sneeze, straining)** — Raised ICP or posterior fossa lesion; Chiari malformation should also be considered
- **Combination of COC pill and migraine with aura** — Significantly elevated stroke risk; COC should be ceased and alternative contraception prescribed
- **Headache in pregnancy** — Pre-eclampsia, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, and posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome must be excluded; BP and urinalysis mandatory
- **New headache in patient with known malignancy** — Brain metastasis until proven otherwise; requires urgent neuroimaging

## Investigations

**Bedside:**
- Blood pressure measurement (hypertensive headache, pre-eclampsia)
- Urinalysis (pre-eclampsia, infection)
- Blood glucose (hypoglycaemia)
- Fundoscopy (papilloedema indicating raised intracranial pressure)
- Full neurological examination including cranial nerves, cerebellar testing, and visual fields

**Bloods:**
- Full blood count (anaemia, infection, thrombocytosis)
- ESR and CRP (giant cell arteritis; ESR typically >50 mm/hr, often >100 mm/hr)
- Coagulation studies and thrombophilia screen (cerebral venous sinus thrombosis)
- U&E, LFT, glucose (metabolic causes, medication monitoring)
- Pregnancy test (all women of reproductive age with new or changed headache pattern)
- Serology if indicated (HIV, inflammatory screen)

**Imaging:**
- CT brain without contrast (acute setting: exclude haemorrhage, herniation; appropriate first-line in emergency)
- MRI brain with and without gadolinium (superior for posterior fossa, white matter lesions, venous thrombosis, small tumours; preferred when space-occupying lesion or demyelination is suspected)
- MR venography (cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, particularly in young women on COC)
- CT angiography (suspected aneurysm or arteriovenous malformation)
- Lumbar puncture (after imaging excludes raised ICP with mass effect): CSF opening pressure, cells, protein, glucose, culture, xanthochromia

**Specialist:**
- Neurology referral: atypical features, refractory migraine, first presentation with neurological deficit, abnormal neuroimaging
- Ophthalmology: suspected giant cell arteritis (risk of arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy), visual symptoms
- Temporal artery biopsy: gold standard for giant cell arteritis (do not delay steroids pending biopsy if clinical suspicion is high)

## Management

**Acute Migraine Treatment (Stepwise):**

*Step 1 — Mild to moderate attacks:*
- Simple analgesia: paracetamol 1 g oral or ibuprofen 400–600 mg at headache onset
- Anti-emetics: metoclopramide 10 mg or prochlorperazine (also have intrinsic analgesic effect in migraine)
- Rest in a quiet, darkened environment

*Step 2 — Moderate to severe attacks or failure of step 1:*
- Triptans: sumatriptan 50–100 mg oral (first-line; can also be given as nasal spray 20 mg or subcutaneous 6 mg for rapid onset or prominent nausea/vomiting); can be combined with NSAIDs
- Zolmitriptan, rizatriptan, and naratriptan are alternatives with varying pharmacokinetics
- Triptans should be taken at headache onset (not during aura phase)
- Contraindicated in: ischaemic heart disease, uncontrolled hypertension, previous stroke or TIA, hemiplegic migraine, basilar migraine, pregnancy (relative contraindication)
- **Critical management point:** COC pill must be reviewed and likely ceased given risk of cerebral venous sinus thrombosis and ischaemic stroke, particularly if migraine with aura is confirmed

**Preventive Therapy — Consider when:**
- More than 4 migraine days per month significantly impacting quality of life
- Acute treatments are ineffective, contraindicated, or overused
- Specific subtypes: hemiplegic migraine, migraine with brainstem aura

*First-line preventive options:*
- Beta-blockers: propranolol 40–120 mg daily or metoprolol (avoid in asthma, depression)
- Topiramate 25–100 mg daily (teratogenic — requires contraceptive counselling in women of reproductive age)
- Amitriptyline 10–75 mg nocte (particularly useful if comorbid tension-type headache or sleep disturbance)
- Sodium valproate (avoid in women of childbearing age — teratogenicity and reproductive harms)

*Second-line / specialist-initiated:*
- CGRP monoclonal antibodies (erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab) — funded under the PBS for chronic migraine refractory to two preventive agents
- Botulinum toxin type A — for chronic migraine (≥15 headache days/month, of which ≥8 are migraine)

**Non-Pharmacological Management:**
- Trigger identification and avoidance diary
- Regular sleep schedule, adequate hydration, regular meals
- Stress management: cognitive behavioural therapy, mindfulness, biofeedback
- Avoidance of caffeine excess and abrupt withdrawal
- Aerobic exercise — evidence-based preventive effect

## Communication and Patient Education

**Explaining the Diagnosis:**
Begin with a clear, jargon-free statement: *"You have a condition called migraine. Migraine is a neurological condition — it's a condition of the brain and nervous system — that causes the type of severe headache you've been experiencing, along with the nausea and sensitivity to light."* Emphasise that migraine is a recognised, treatable medical condition, not a manifestation of stress or anxiety (a common and distressing misconception).

**Addressing the Brain Tumour Concern:**
This is the pivot point of any consultation where such a concern has been expressed. The candidate must address this explicitly, empathically, and with the reassurance grounded in clinical evidence: *"I understand you were worried this might be a brain tumour, especially given your brother's experience. I want to reassure you that the MRI scan of your brain is completely normal — there is no tumour, no swelling, and no structural problem at all. Your neurological examination is also normal. I am confident this is migraine."*

**The Combined Oral Contraceptive Pill Discussion:**
This conversation requires sensitivity and shared decision making. The candidate should:
1. Explain the association between oestrogen-containing contraception and migraine
2. Explain the stroke risk if migraine with aura is present
3. Discuss alternatives: progestogen-only pill, hormonal IUD (Mirena), barrier methods, long-acting reversible contraception
4. Clarify aura status before making a definitive recommendation
5. Involve the patient in the decision: *"I'd like us to think about this together and find something that works for you"*

**Medication Counselling:**
- Explain how and when to take acute medications (at headache onset, not during aura)
- Warn about medication overuse headache — *"If you need to take pain relief more than two days a week regularly, the medication itself can start to cause more frequent headaches — it's a recognised side effect called medication overuse headache"*
- Explain the role and rationale of preventive therapy if indicated
- Provide written information: Migraine Australia resources, general practitioner review plan

**Lifestyle Counselling:**
- Encourage keeping a headache diary to identify personal triggers
- Discuss the impact of the oral contraceptive pill, sleep irregularity, meal-skipping, and dehydration
- Reassure that migraine is manageable and that most patients achieve significant improvement with appropriate treatment

## AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

- **Do not confuse knowing the diagnosis with demonstrating clinical competence.** The examiner's mark sheet rewards the process, not the conclusion. Arriving at the correct diagnosis through a disorganised or incomplete history will not attract passing marks.

- **Think out loud, always.** Verbalise every differential as it enters your mind, explain what you are excluding and why, and narrate transitions between sections of the history. The examiner can only score what is externally visible.

- **Screen for red flags systematically and audibly in every headache case.** Name each red flag as you ask about it: *"I'm asking about morning vomiting because I want to exclude raised pressure in the brain."* Do not silently tick them off.

- **Always elicit ICE before providing information.** The candidate who launches into an explanation of migraine without first asking what the patient thinks and fears will miss the central concern of the consultation — and miss the corresponding mark sheet items.

- **Address the COC pill in every migraine counselling station.** This is a high-yield, frequently examined point. Failure to address the stroke risk association in migraine with aura is a significant clinical error and a common reason for failing this station type.

- **Structure your differential diagnosis as a reasoned argument, not a list.** State the most likely diagnosis first, explain the supporting evidence, then name your differentials in descending probability, and explain what has been excluded and on what clinical basis.

- **Do not abbreviate the red flag screen because you are confident of the diagnosis.** The examiner's mark sheet specifically checks whether red flags were explored. A candidate who correctly diagnoses migraine but omits the space-occupying lesion screen will lose key marks.

- **Use signposting language to demonstrate organisation.** *"I've now finished asking about the pain itself — I'd like to move on to ask about some associated symptoms"* demonstrates structured thinking and allows the examiner to follow and score your sequence accurately.

- **In counselling stations, acknowledge emotion before providing information.** If the simulated patient expresses distress, fear, or frustration, respond empathically before continuing with clinical content. Failing to acknowledge the emotional register of the consultation is a common and costly error.

- **Never memorise the case — practise the clinical structure.** The mark sheet for a headache station is identical whether the final diagnosis is migraine, tension-type headache, or cluster headache, because it assesses the approach to headache, not the diagnosis alone. Practising a logical, complete headache structure prepares the candidate for any headache presentation.

- **Manage the combined oral contraceptive pill with nuance.** Do not simply state *"you need to stop the pill"* without exploring aura status, explaining the rationale clearly, and offering alternative contraceptive options. Shared decision making is itself a mark sheet item.

- **Demonstrate awareness of the functional and psychosocial impact.** Ask about the impact of headaches on work, study, relationships, and daily activities. This signals patient-centred practice and is a specific item on communication station mark sheets.

## Safety Net

**Immediate return to the Emergency Department if any of the following occur:**

- Sudden onset of the worst headache of the patient's life — this may represent subarachnoid haemorrhage and requires emergency evaluation regardless of prior headache history
- Headache accompanied by fever, neck stiffness, or a new non-blanching rash — possible bacterial meningitis; call 000 immediately
- New weakness of the face, arm, or leg; difficulty speaking; sudden visual loss; unsteadiness — possible stroke or TIA; call 000 immediately
- Rapid worsening of headache frequency or severity over days to weeks
- Headache after a head injury, particularly with confusion, drowsiness, or vomiting
- First-ever seizure

**Planned Follow-Up:**

- General practitioner review in 4–6 weeks to assess response to acute treatment strategy and to review headache diary
- If preventive therapy is commenced, review at 8–12 weeks for efficacy and side effects (preventive agents typically require 6–12 weeks for full effect)
- Neurology referral if: diagnosis remains uncertain, attacks are refractory to two adequate preventive agents, new neurological symptoms develop, or imaging reveals an incidental finding requiring expert interpretation
- Contraceptive review with GP or family planning service within 4 weeks to transition from combined oral contraceptive pill to a safe alternative

**Patient Resources:**

- Migraine Australia (migraine.org.au) — reliable patient-facing information about migraine triggers, treatment, and diary tools
- NPS MedicineWise (nps.org.au) — medication information in plain English
- Written summary of the consultation, diagnosis, and management plan provided to the patient at the conclusion of the consultation
