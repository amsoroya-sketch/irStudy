Haematemesis in a 72-Year-Old Woman — AMC Clinical Examination History Taking Station

Clinical Presentation

A 72-year-old woman presents to the Emergency Department accompanied by her partner after vomiting blood. This is an acute, potentially life-threatening presentation requiring the candidate to demonstrate both clinical urgency and systematic history-taking skill within a six-minute structured encounter. The haematemesis station tests the candidate's ability to efficiently characterise the bleeding, generate a prioritised differential diagnosis through targeted questioning, identify red flag features, and communicate with appropriate empathy in a high-stakes emergency setting.

This presentation is clinically important because haematemesis in an elderly patient carries a broad differential ranging from benign conditions such as Mallory-Weiss tears to immediately life-threatening pathology including upper gastrointestinal malignancy and oesophageal varices secondary to chronic liver disease. The candidate must demonstrate the capacity to hold multiple diagnostic possibilities simultaneously whilst following a logical and humane clinical structure. In this particular case, the convergence of red flag features — unintentional weight loss, anorexia, fatigue, and chronic NSAID use — with haematemesis in a 72-year-old points strongly toward gastric malignancy on a background of peptic ulcer disease, making systematic and thorough history-taking essential for arriving at the correct diagnosis.

The AMC Clinical Examination places significant weighting on history-taking competency in presentations such as this one, where the diagnosis cannot be established by physical examination alone and where the candidate's structured questioning directly determines whether life-threatening pathology is identified and appropriately escalated. Candidates must be fluent in a dual-component approach: characterising the vomiting itself and separately characterising the nature, quantity, and source of the blood.

Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

The examiner in this station assesses the candidate's ability to open professionally, demonstrate patient-centred communication, conduct a systematic and differential-driven history, screen comprehensively for red flags, explore the patient's Ideas, Concerns, and Expectations (ICE), and close the consultation with an appropriate summary and safety net.

Opening and Haemodynamic Assessment: Before beginning the history, the candidate must confirm haemodynamic stability by requesting or acknowledging vital signs — blood pressure, pulse rate, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation. This demonstrates clinical awareness of the life-threatening potential of the presentation without diverting into examination. The opening question should be open-ended, inviting the patient to describe the event in her own words, followed by a brief empathic acknowledgement of the frightening nature of the experience.

Structured History: The history proceeds through characterisation of the complaint (the vomit and the blood separately), systematic differential-directed questioning, red flag screening, and relevant past medical and social history. Each differential is explored with a distinct cluster of questions rather than a generic symptom list.

ICE Elicitation: The candidate should actively explore the patient's own understanding of what has happened, her concerns about what it may mean, and what she expects from the consultation. In this scenario, the patient has presented in a state of acute anxiety and the examiner expects the candidate to address this explicitly.

Red Flag Screen and Closing: The red flag screen must include constitutional symptoms (weight loss, anorexia, fatigue) and evidence of haematological instability (dizziness, presyncope). The candidate should close with a clear summary of findings, a preliminary explanation pitched to the patient's level, and a clear plan for next steps.

Socrates Flow

Systematic Approach

Step 1 — Opening and Safety Confirmation

Before initiating the history, the candidate should request confirmation of haemodynamic stability. This is not a physical examination step; it is a clinical safety check appropriate to the emergency department context.

Appropriate opening approach:

"Before I begin, could you please confirm the patient's blood pressure, pulse rate, and oxygen saturation?"

Once stability is confirmed, the candidate sits at eye level, introduces by name and role, and opens with an empathic, open-ended invitation:

"I understand this has been a very frightening experience for you and your partner. Can you tell me in your own words what happened today?"

Following the patient's initial statement — typically a brief account of sudden vomiting of blood and subsequent alarm — the candidate should validate her emotional response and signpost the consultation:

"That must have been very alarming to see, and you did exactly the right thing coming in. I am going to ask you some questions to help me understand exactly what has happened so we can make sure you receive the best possible care. Is that alright?"

Step 2 — Characterising the Haematemesis (Two-Component Approach)

The complaint of vomiting blood contains two distinct clinical components: the vomiting event itself and the character of the blood. Both must be explored separately and systematically.

Component A — The Vomiting

  • Timing: When exactly did the episode occur? Was it today, this morning, within the last few hours?
  • Frequency: Is this the first time you have vomited blood, or has this happened before? How many times did you vomit in total?
  • Clinical rationale: A single episode in the context of chronic NSAID use and constitutional symptoms is more consistent with malignancy or peptic ulcer disease; recurrent, forceful vomiting episodes raise Mallory-Weiss syndrome.
  • Preceding vomiting pattern: Had you been vomiting repeatedly before the blood appeared?
  • Clinical rationale: Mallory-Weiss tears characteristically follow multiple episodes of forceful retching or vomiting, causing mucosal tears at the gastro-oesophageal junction.

Component B — The Blood

  • Colour and character: Was the blood bright red and fresh, or was it dark and altered — like coffee grounds?
  • Clinical rationale: Bright red blood suggests active or recent proximal upper GI bleeding; coffee-ground material indicates digested blood from a slower or more distal source, such as a gastric or duodenal ulcer.
  • Presence of clots: Were there blood clots in the vomit? Blood clots suggest significant volume bleeding.
  • Quantity: Was it a small amount — just streaks or a few drops — or was it a large amount, perhaps filling a cup or more?
  • Clinical rationale: Volume estimation guides haemodynamic risk and urgency of endoscopy.

Component C — Source Clarification

In any presentation of blood from the mouth, the candidate must systematically exclude alternative anatomical sources:

  • Epistaxis: "Have you had any nose bleeds recently, or around the time of this episode?"
  • Clinical rationale: Posterior epistaxis can drain into the pharynx and be swallowed, subsequently appearing as haematemesis or melaena.
  • Haemoptysis: "Did you cough up any of the blood, or was it purely from vomiting?"
  • Clinical rationale: Haemoptysis — frothy, bright red, coughed-up blood — must be distinguished from haematemesis as it directs evaluation toward a respiratory source.

Component D — Systemic Stability Symptoms (Red Flag Symptoms)

  • "Have you felt dizzy or lightheaded since this happened?"
  • "Have you felt any palpitations or a racing heart?"
  • "Have you felt unusually weak or faint?"

Clinical rationale: These questions screen for haemodynamic compromise from significant blood loss — anaemia, hypovolaemia, or haemorrhagic shock.

Step 3 — Differential-Directed Questioning

Having characterised the complaint, the candidate moves through a structured differential diagnosis framework, directing specific questions at each diagnostic group.

3A — Malignancy Cluster (Gastric Cancer and Oesophageal Cancer)

These represent the Must-Not-Miss diagnoses in a 72-year-old presenting with haematemesis and constitutional symptoms.

Constitutional red flag screen:
- "Have you noticed any unintentional weight loss recently? If so, how many kilograms, and over what period of time?"
- "Has your appetite changed? Have you been eating less than usual?"
- "Have you been feeling more tired or fatigued than usual?"
- "Have you noticed any lumps anywhere on your body?"

Gastric cancer-specific questions:
- "Have you been having any pain or discomfort in your upper abdomen?" — if yes, explore fully with SOCRATES
- "Have you noticed any dark or tarry stools?" — melaena suggests upper GI bleeding and raises concern for malignancy or peptic ulcer

Oesophageal cancer-specific questions:
- "Have you had any difficulty or pain with swallowing — either solids, liquids, or both?"
- "Has food ever felt like it is sticking on the way down?"
- "Have you noticed a change in your voice — any hoarseness?"

Clinical note: The absence of dysphagia in this patient makes oesophageal cancer less likely than gastric cancer, though it remains on the differential. Both should be explicitly considered and explicitly addressed in the candidate's summary.

3B — Peptic Ulcer Disease and Severe Oesophagitis / Gastro-Oesophageal Reflux Disease (GORD)

  • "Have you had any burning pain in your chest or upper abdomen — a heartburn sensation?"
  • "Have you noticed any acid or sour taste coming up into your mouth?"
  • "Have you noticed increased saliva production or a feeling of water brash?"
  • "Have you had any abdominal pain? Can you tell me more about where it is and what it feels like?"

Risk factor screening:
- "Do you take any regular medications — in particular, any anti-inflammatory tablets such as ibuprofen, naproxen, or aspirin, even at low dose?" — Chronic NSAID use is a major risk factor for peptic ulcer disease and upper GI bleeding
- "Do you drink alcohol? How many standard drinks per day or per week?"
- "Do you smoke? How many cigarettes per day, and for how long?"
- "Do you consume a lot of spicy food or caffeine?"
- "Have you had a Helicobacter pylori infection in the past, or been treated for an ulcer previously?"

3C — Oesophageal Varices (Complication of Chronic Liver Disease / Portal Hypertension)

This differential requires a targeted liver disease and portal hypertension screen. Oesophageal varices represent a life-threatening emergency; their exclusion must be systematic.

  • "Have you noticed any yellowish discolouration of your skin or the whites of your eyes?" — jaundice
  • "Has your urine been unusually dark — like tea or cola coloured?" — conjugated bilirubinuria
  • "Have your stools been unusually pale or clay coloured?" — acholic stools
  • "Has your abdomen been swelling recently?" — ascites
  • "Have you had any confusion, difficulty concentrating, or noticed changes in your sleep pattern?" — hepatic encephalopathy
  • "Have you noticed prominent veins on your abdomen?"
  • "How much alcohol do you drink on a daily basis?" — the most important single risk factor for cirrhotic portal hypertension

Clinical note: The absence of liver disease features makes varices less likely in this patient; however, this must be actively excluded rather than assumed.

3D — Mallory-Weiss Syndrome

This diagnosis is essentially excluded by careful characterisation of the vomiting history. Mallory-Weiss tears occur at the gastro-oesophageal junction following repeated, forceful retching or vomiting, creating longitudinal mucosal tears. The key discriminating question is:

  • "Before you vomited the blood, had you been vomiting repeatedly or retching hard for some time — or did the blood appear the very first time you vomited?"

Clinical note: A single episode of haematemesis without preceding forceful vomiting makes Mallory-Weiss syndrome significantly less likely. This should be stated explicitly when presenting differentials to the examiner.

3E — Bleeding Disorders

While typically a lower-priority differential in this age group with this presentation, bleeding disorders must be considered, particularly given the candidate's duty to screen for anticoagulant use.

Symptom screen:
- "Have you noticed that you bruise more easily than usual?"
- "Have you had any recurrent or unusually heavy nose bleeds?"
- "Have you noticed prolonged bleeding from small cuts?"

Medication review — specific inquiry:
- "Are you taking any blood-thinning medication — for example, warfarin, rivaroxaban, apixaban, or clopidogrel?"
- Clinical rationale: Even if a general medication history has been taken, anticoagulant and antiplatelet therapy must be specifically and explicitly asked about in any upper GI bleeding presentation, as these agents substantially increase bleeding risk and alter management.

Family history:
- "Is there any family history of bleeding disorders?"

Step 4 — Past Medical, Family, and Social History

Past medical history:
- Previous episodes of haematemesis or melaena
- Known peptic ulcer disease, gastritis, or Barrett's oesophagus
- Prior upper GI endoscopy findings
- Known liver disease, hepatitis B or C
- Inflammatory bowel disease
- Previous abdominal surgery or gastric surgery

Family history:
- Gastric cancer, colorectal cancer, or other gastrointestinal malignancy
- Bleeding disorders

Social history:
- Smoking history (pack-years)
- Alcohol use (standard drinks per day; duration)
- Dietary habits
- Functional status and activities of daily living
- Living circumstances — who is at home, level of support
- Occupation (current or past)

Step 5 — ICE — Ideas, Concerns, and Expectations

ICE exploration should be woven naturally into the consultation rather than reserved for a single formal block. The following questions are appropriate for this station:

  • Ideas: "When you saw the blood this morning, did you have any thoughts about what might be causing it?"
  • Concerns: "Is there anything in particular that you or your partner are worried about?" — At this point, many patients will voice concern about cancer; this must be acknowledged with empathy and honesty.
  • Expectations: "What were you hoping we would be able to do for you today?"

Clinical communication note: In elderly patients presenting to the Emergency Department with acute, frightening symptoms, the examiner expects the candidate to demonstrate genuine empathy, not formulaic ICE elicitation. Addressing the patient's and partner's distress is a communication competency criterion in this station.

Redflags Panel
Clinically Enriched

Red Flags — Must Not Miss

  • Unintentional weight loss — Loss of more than 5% body weight over three to six months without deliberate effort is a cardinal red flag for underlying malignancy, particularly gastric or oesophageal cancer. Quantify by asking number of kilograms and time period. Mandatory referral for upper GI endoscopy.

  • Anorexia (loss of appetite) — Progressive or significant reduction in appetite in association with haematemesis substantially raises the probability of gastrointestinal malignancy. Must be documented with onset and severity.

  • Unexplained fatigue — Chronic fatigue or increasing tiredness in an elderly patient with haematemesis may reflect chronic occult blood loss with iron deficiency anaemia on a background of malignancy. Requires full blood examination.

  • Melaena (dark, tarry stools) — Black, tarry, foul-smelling stools indicate digested blood from an upper gastrointestinal source and confirm significant bleeding proximal to the caecum. Always ask specifically; patients may not volunteer this without direct questioning.

  • Dysphagia — Progressive difficulty swallowing, particularly when initially affecting solids, is a red flag for oesophageal or gastric cardia malignancy. Its presence markedly elevates urgency for endoscopic assessment.

  • Haemodynamic instability — Dizziness, presyncope, palpitations, or near-syncope in association with haematemesis indicates significant blood loss with cardiovascular compromise. Requires immediate resuscitation, not merely history-taking.

  • Large volume or bright red haematemesis — Substantial haematemesis, particularly with fresh red blood or clots, suggests brisk active upper GI haemorrhage and requires urgent endoscopy and resuscitation.

  • Age over 60 with upper GI symptoms — Any new onset upper gastrointestinal symptoms — dyspepsia, haematemesis, melaena — in a patient over 60 years of age requires urgent investigation to exclude malignancy, irrespective of the apparent aetiology.

  • Long-term NSAID or aspirin use — Chronic NSAID or aspirin use substantially increases the risk of gastric and duodenal ulceration, bleeding, and malignant transformation. This must be specifically and explicitly asked about, not merely inferred from a general medication history.

  • Significant alcohol use — Daily or heavy alcohol intake raises risk for oesophageal varices secondary to cirrhosis and portal hypertension, alcoholic gastritis, and Mallory-Weiss syndrome. Quantification is essential.

Investigations

Bedside:
- Vital signs — blood pressure (including postural drop), heart rate, oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, temperature
- Twelve-lead ECG (in elderly patient with haemodynamic compromise)
- Urinalysis
- Point-of-care blood glucose

Bloods:
- Full blood examination — haemoglobin, haematocrit, platelet count, mean corpuscular volume (iron deficiency pattern)
- Coagulation studies — INR, APTT (detect coagulopathy or monitor anticoagulant therapy)
- Urea and electrolytes, creatinine — elevated serum urea:creatinine ratio suggests upper GI blood digestion and absorption
- Liver function tests — bilirubin, albumin, transaminases, GGT, ALP
- C-reactive protein, ESR
- Group and screen / crossmatch (if significant bleeding)
- Iron studies, serum ferritin
- Tumour markers — serum CEA, CA 19-9 (in the context of suspected malignancy, used adjunctively, not diagnostically)
- Helicobacter pylori serology or stool antigen (if peptic ulcer disease suspected)

Imaging:
- Erect chest radiograph — pneumoperitoneum (perforation), mediastinal widening (oesophageal pathology)
- CT abdomen and pelvis with contrast — staging of suspected malignancy; assessment of hepatic architecture, portal hypertension features
- CT angiography — if active significant haemorrhage and endoscopy is not immediately available or has failed

Specialist:
- Urgent upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (oesophagogastroduodenoscopy, OGD) — the definitive investigation for haematemesis; allows direct visualisation, biopsy, and therapeutic intervention (e.g., injection, clipping, banding of varices)
- Histopathological biopsy of any gastric or oesophageal lesion
- Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) — locoregional staging of malignancy once identified
- Hepatology or gastroenterology referral as appropriate

Management

Acute Management (Emergency Department)

  1. Resuscitation first — Ensure haemodynamic stability. Establish large-bore intravenous access (two peripheral cannulae). Commence intravenous fluid resuscitation if hypotensive or tachycardic. Crossmatch and transfuse packed red blood cells if haemoglobin is critically low or patient is haemodynamically compromised.

  2. Nil by mouth — Maintain NBM status in anticipation of urgent endoscopy.

  3. Proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy — Commence high-dose intravenous PPI (e.g., esomeprazole 80 mg IV bolus followed by infusion at 8 mg/hour) empirically pending endoscopy in cases of suspected peptic ulcer disease or oesophagitis. This reduces re-bleeding risk and promotes clot stability.

  4. Cease offending medications — Cease NSAIDs, aspirin (unless prescribed for secondary cardiovascular prevention — weigh benefits and risks), and anticoagulants where clinically appropriate after specialist review.

  5. Urgent endoscopy — Arrange urgent upper GI endoscopy within 24 hours (or immediately if haemodynamically unstable). This is the cornerstone of both diagnosis and treatment.

  6. Gastroenterology / upper GI surgery referral — Notify gastroenterology or upper GI surgery immediately for all significant haematemesis cases.

Suspected Malignancy Management

  1. Formal upper GI endoscopy with biopsy — Multiple biopsies from any suspicious gastric or oesophageal lesion. Minimum of eight biopsies from a gastric ulcer to achieve adequate diagnostic sensitivity.

  2. CT staging — CT chest, abdomen, and pelvis with contrast for TNM staging once histological diagnosis is confirmed.

  3. Multidisciplinary team (MDT) review — All confirmed or suspected GI malignancies should be discussed at the upper GI MDT meeting (gastroenterology, upper GI surgery, medical oncology, radiation oncology, dietetics, social work).

  4. Eradication of H. pylori — If H. pylori positive, standard triple therapy (PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin for seven to fourteen days) or quadruple therapy where local resistance patterns indicate. Confirm eradication six weeks post-treatment with urea breath test.

  5. Ongoing NSAID management — Patients requiring ongoing NSAID therapy for chronic musculoskeletal conditions should receive the lowest effective dose with concurrent PPI gastroprotection, or transition to a COX-2 selective agent with specialist review.

Communication and Patient Education

Explaining the Findings

When communicating the results of the history to the patient, the candidate must balance honesty and clarity with sensitivity to patient distress. The following approach is appropriate for this station:

"Margaret, I have been asking you these questions because there are several conditions that can cause blood in the vomit, and I wanted to make sure I understood your situation fully. During our conversation, I found some features that I want to be honest with you about — they are what we call red flag features, meaning they are findings that require further investigation. You have been losing weight without trying to, your appetite has not been good, and you have been feeling very tired. Together with the blood in your vomit, these features raise the concern of a possible cancer in the stomach — which we call gastric cancer. I want to be clear that I cannot diagnose this from our conversation alone, and we will need a camera test — a gastroscopy — to look inside your stomach properly. However, I also want you to know that another common cause is an ulcer in the stomach, which can also cause bleeding and which can be effectively treated. We will investigate this thoroughly and you will have the right team caring for you."

Key Communication Principles

  • Use plain, accessible language — avoid jargon such as "haematemesis" or "oesophagogastroduodenoscopy" when speaking directly with the patient
  • Acknowledge the patient's and partner's distress explicitly and early in the consultation
  • Provide a clear next-steps plan — investigations first, diagnosis second
  • Avoid definitive diagnostic statements before investigations are complete; use language such as "raise the concern of" or "we need to investigate further to confirm"
  • Check understanding using the teach-back method: "I have shared a lot of information — can you tell me in your own words what you understand about the next steps?"
  • Provide written information about upper GI endoscopy, including preparation, procedural steps, and recovery

AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

  • Always confirm haemodynamic stability before commencing the history — In any haematemesis station, the first clinical action must be to acknowledge or request vital signs. Failure to do so signals a lack of clinical urgency to the examiner, regardless of how thorough the history is.

  • Treat haematemesis as two separate problems: the vomiting and the blood — Do not conflate these. Characterise the vomiting pattern (frequency, preceding events, relationship to retching) and the blood (colour, character, clots, quantity) separately and systematically.

  • Always clarify the source of blood from the mouth — Ask specifically about epistaxis and haemoptysis. This demonstrates clinical reasoning and prevents diagnostic error; a posterior nose bleed presenting as haematemesis is a classic clinical trap.

  • Constitutional red flags are mandatory and non-negotiable — Weight loss, anorexia, and fatigue must be asked about in every upper GI bleeding station. In a 72-year-old patient, failure to elicit these red flags is a critical omission that may result in failure regardless of other history components.

  • Ask about NSAIDs specifically, not just "medications" — Many patients do not consider over-the-counter ibuprofen or aspirin to be "medications." Ask directly: "Do you take any anti-inflammatory tablets, even ones you can buy without a prescription, like ibuprofen or Nurofen?"

  • Ask about anticoagulants specifically, not just "blood thinners" — Direct anticoagulant agent names (apixaban, rivaroxaban, warfarin) should be used in the question. Many patients do not know the class of their medication but recognise the name.

  • Do not ask about irrelevant general symptoms — Constipation, diarrhoea, and bloating add minimal diagnostic value in a pure haematemesis presentation. Use the limited time to ask targeted, differential-directed questions. The examiner rewards specificity.

  • Mallory-Weiss syndrome is excluded — not assumed — Actively exclude Mallory-Weiss by asking about the pattern of vomiting preceding the blood. A single episode of haematemesis without forceful retching makes it unlikely; state this explicitly in your differential.

  • In a 72-year-old with constitutional red flags, gastric cancer is the top differential — Rank your differentials clearly and defend your ranking. The combination of age, weight loss, anorexia, fatigue, haematemesis, and chronic NSAID use makes gastric cancer the leading diagnosis until proven otherwise by endoscopy and histopathology.

  • ICE is a clinical competency criterion, not a formality — Patients presenting with haematemesis are frightened. Demonstrating genuine empathy, addressing the patient's concerns, and checking understanding of the plan are assessed communication skills, not optional courtesies.

  • Present your two-to-three top differentials with clear clinical reasoning — When closing the consultation, briefly summarise why your top diagnosis is most likely given the specific findings elicited, and acknowledge what investigations are required to confirm or refute it. Vague or incomplete differential presentations are heavily penalised.

  • Do not neglect the liver disease screen in any haematemesis station — Even in the absence of obvious alcohol history, a brief screen for jaundice, dark urine, pale stools, and abdominal swelling must be completed. Oesophageal varices represent a catastrophic, life-threatening diagnosis that must be systematically excluded.

Safety Net

Instructions for the patient on discharge or interim management:

  • "I want to make sure you know exactly what to watch for at home — though I expect we will be completing further tests today before you leave."

Return immediately to the Emergency Department if:
- Further vomiting of blood occurs — any amount, fresh or altered
- Dark or tarry stools are noticed
- Dizziness, lightheadedness, or fainting occurs
- Rapid or irregular heartbeat is felt
- Severe abdominal pain develops
- Increasing weakness or profound fatigue

Follow-up plan:
- Urgent upper GI endoscopy — within 24 hours of presentation if haemodynamically stable; sooner if instability develops
- Gastroenterology outpatient review within two weeks if endoscopy is diagnostic and stable
- General Practitioner review within 48–72 hours following hospital discharge for medication review and ongoing care coordination
- Cease all NSAIDs and notify treating GP of cessation
- Alcohol cessation counselling and referral to alcohol and drug services if significant alcohol use identified
- Written discharge instructions in plain English documenting red flag symptoms, follow-up appointments, and medication changes
- If malignancy is confirmed on endoscopy and biopsy, expedited referral to the upper GI MDT meeting, with appointment within two weeks in accordance with Australian cancer care guidelines
