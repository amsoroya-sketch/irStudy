# Right Upper Quadrant Abdominal Pain — Biliary Colic, Cholecystitis, and Cholangitis

## Clinical Presentation

A 52-year-old woman presenting with right upper quadrant (RUQ) abdominal pain represents one of the highest-yield and most diagnostically demanding stations in the AMC Clinical Examination. The clinical challenge lies not merely in recognising gallbladder pathology, but in precisely distinguishing between three related yet clinically distinct conditions: biliary colic, acute cholecystitis, and acute cholangitis. Failure rates for this station are disproportionately high because candidates conflate these diagnoses, applying the wrong label to a case whose distinguishing features are deliberately embedded in the history and examination findings.

The station demands a structured, systematic approach to an upper abdominal pain presentation, with particular attention to a core diagnostic triad — pain, fever, and jaundice — that, when present together, constitutes a medical emergency requiring urgent intervention. The examiner assesses whether the candidate can generate an appropriately broad differential for RUQ pain, acquire the targeted history that separates biliary pathology from its mimics, perform a complete and properly sequenced abdominal examination including the critical Murphy's sign, and communicate a precise diagnosis with supporting reasoning to both the examiner and the patient.

The station may be configured as a combined history and physical examination task, or as a physical examination and investigation task where history has already been taken. In either format, the ability to arrive at a single working diagnosis — biliary colic, cholecystitis, or cholangitis — with a clear rationale, differentiates the passing candidate from the one who hedges or misdiagnoses.

---

## Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

This station tests the candidate's ability to integrate history, physical examination, and clinical reasoning in a focused but comprehensive assessment of RUQ abdominal pain. The examiner evaluates three domains: the breadth and systematic quality of the history; the completeness and technique of the physical examination, including Murphy's sign; and the clarity and accuracy of the diagnostic formulation communicated to the patient.

**For history-taking tasks:** The candidate should open with an empathic, patient-centred introduction, acknowledge the presenting complaint, offer analgesia if the patient is in active pain, and then systematically explore the pain using a structured assessment tool (SOCRATES). The candidate must screen for jaundice, fever, and red flags for malignancy, and must identify the distinguishing features between biliary colic (episodic, meal-related, self-resolving) and inflammatory biliary conditions (persistent pain, systemic features, tenderness).

**For physical examination tasks:** The candidate should approach the patient professionally, orient themselves with a brief statement of intent, begin with general inspection (prioritising jaundice and level of consciousness), obtain all four vital signs with explicit emphasis on temperature, and perform a complete abdominal examination from inspection through to percussion and auscultation, culminating in Murphy's sign. A bedside urinalysis (urine dipstick) is a mandatory office-based test in this station.

**For combined history, examination, and management tasks:** The candidate must demonstrate integration of all findings into a coherent clinical narrative, explain the diagnosis using accessible language, articulate a structured differential, and outline acute management — including hospital admission, analgesia, IV fluids, IV antibiotics, and surgical review with planned cholecystectomy.

## Systematic Approach

### History Taking — SOCRATES and Targeted Biliary Screen

**Opening and establishing rapport**

Begin with a professional introduction, confirm the patient's identity and comfort, and ask an open-ended opening question: *"Can you tell me what's been happening for you?"* Allow the patient to describe the pain in their own words before proceeding to directed questioning. If the patient is in active pain at the time of the interview, acknowledge this directly and offer analgesia before proceeding.

**Pain characterisation (SOCRATES)**

- **Site:** Confirm RUQ location. Pain arising here immediately elevates gallbladder, liver, and duodenal pathology to the top of the differential.
- **Onset:** Was the onset sudden or gradual? Biliary colic typically begins acutely and may follow a meal. Cholecystitis is often preceded by episodes of biliary colic. Establish whether this is the first episode or a recurrent presentation.
- **Character:** Biliary colic and cholecystitis both produce a deep, visceral, aching pain, sometimes described as cramping. Constant, unrelenting pain suggests cholecystitis or cholangitis rather than simple biliary colic.
- **Radiation:** Classic gallbladder pain radiates to the right shoulder tip (via diaphragmatic irritation and the phrenic nerve, C3–C5) or to the right infrascapular region. This radiation is highly specific for biliary origin.
- **Associations:** Nausea and vomiting are common across all biliary conditions. Ask specifically about fever and chills — their presence raises the likelihood of cholecystitis or cholangitis considerably. Ask about pain on breathing, which may indicate diaphragmatic irritation from gallbladder inflammation.
- **Time course:** Biliary colic characteristically lasts two to six hours and resolves spontaneously. Cholecystitis produces persistent, unrelenting pain. Cholangitis produces progressive pain with systemic deterioration.
- **Exacerbating and relieving factors:** Pain precipitated by large, fatty meals is strongly suggestive of gallbladder origin. In biliary colic, there are typically no reliable relieving factors beyond time.
- **Severity:** Obtain a numerical pain score (0–10) to gauge severity and to monitor response to treatment.

**Systematic general gastrointestinal screen**

Following SOCRATES, screen for associated gastrointestinal symptoms: nausea, vomiting, bloating, change in bowel habit, constipation, and diarrhoea.

**Targeted biliary and hepatic questioning — the three pivotal domains**

*Domain 1 — Fever and systemic infection:*
Ask explicitly about fever, rigidity, chills, and sweating. This question must be asked at least once during the history. The presence of fever distinguishes cholecystitis (fever present in many, but not all, cases) from biliary colic (afebrile), and the combination of high fever with pain and jaundice constitutes the Charcot triad of cholangitis, demanding urgent clinical action.

*Domain 2 — Jaundice and hepatitis screen:*
Ask about yellowish discolouration of the skin or eyes, dark urine (bilirubinuria), pale stools (acholic stools), and pruritus. These features indicate biliary obstruction at the level of the common bile duct. While jaundice can occur in approximately 15% of cholecystitis cases due to adjacent hepatic inflammation, its combination with fever and RUQ pain is the defining triad of cholangitis until proven otherwise. Additionally enquire about travel history (hepatitis A exposure), alcohol consumption, intravenous drug use, sexual history, and blood transfusions to complete the hepatitis risk factor assessment.

*Domain 3 — Previous biliary episodes and gallstone risk:*
Ask specifically: *"Have you ever had episodes of similar pain after eating large or fatty meals?"* Previous self-resolving episodes of meal-related RUQ pain strongly suggest pre-existing gallstones. The female sex, age over 40, and fatty meal precipitation constitute classic epidemiological risk factors for cholelithiasis.

**Red flag screen for malignancy**

In any patient with hepatobiliary pathology, screen for features of underlying malignancy: unintentional weight loss, anorexia, early satiety, palpable masses or lumps, and rectal bleeding. A family history of bowel cancer or hepatobiliary malignancy should be elicited. Cancer of the gallbladder, biliary ducts (cholangiocarcinoma), hepatocellular carcinoma, or pancreatic head cancer can all produce obstructive jaundice with RUQ pain and requires early consideration, particularly in older patients or those with persistent symptoms.

**Pancreatic screen**

Given that the epigastric region is contiguous with the RUQ, brief pancreatic questioning is appropriate: alcohol intake (the most important risk factor for pancreatitis), recent excessive alcohol consumption, and any history of abdominal trauma.

**Upper gastrointestinal screen**

Screen for gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) and peptic ulcer disease, as both can present with upper abdominal discomfort that mimics biliary pain: acid-brash taste, heartburn, water brash (sudden hypersalivation), and exacerbation with spicy food or caffeine.

**Cardiac and respiratory screen**

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and basal pneumonia are important upper abdominal mimics that must be excluded. Ask about chest pain, chest tightness, dyspnoea, palpitations, and cough. ACS in particular must be considered as a must-not-miss diagnosis in any patient over 40 with upper abdominal pain.

**Medication, surgical, and social history**

Enquire about medications (NSAIDs, steroids, and many other drugs can cause hepatic and gastric injury), previous abdominal surgeries (biliary strictures may complicate bile duct surgery, creating a nidus for cholangitis), and alcohol and tobacco use.

### Physical Examination — Sequence and Technique

**General appearance and vital signs**

Approach the patient with professional demeanour. The single most important initial observation is **jaundice** — examine the sclerae and skin under adequate lighting. Jaundice is the pivotal sign that elevates the differential toward obstructive biliary pathology, hepatitis, or malignancy, and its presence alongside fever and pain establishes the Charcot triad of cholangitis as the working diagnosis.

After assessing for jaundice, note: level of consciousness (alert, drowsy, or confused — the latter two indicating potential Reynold's pentad in severe cholangitis), hydration status, and pallor.

**Vital signs — all four must be obtained, with explicit emphasis on temperature:**
- Temperature — mandatory; its presence or absence is the single most important vital sign for differentiating biliary colic and cholecystitis from cholangitis
- Blood pressure
- Heart rate
- Respiratory rate
- Oxygen saturation

A temperature of 38.5°C or above in the context of RUQ pain mandates urgent consideration of cholecystitis or cholangitis.

**Abdominal inspection**

With the patient supine, adequately exposed from xiphisternum to symphysis pubis, inspect systematically:
- Abdominal movement with respiration — voluntary guarding or splinting suggests peritoneal irritation; in severe cholecystitis the patient may limit right-sided inspiratory movement
- Symmetry and contour — distension may suggest bowel obstruction; a visible right-sided mass may represent an empyema of the gallbladder
- Scars — previous cholecystectomy scar (right subcostal / Kocher's incision, or laparoscopic port scars) is clinically significant; prior biliary surgery predisposes to stricture formation and cholangitis
- Cutaneous stigmata of chronic liver disease — spider naevi, caput medusae, palmar erythema — although these are signs of chronic rather than acute hepatobiliary pathology, their presence shapes the differential

**Superficial and deep palpation**

Begin with superficial palpation systematically across all nine abdominal regions, starting away from the area of pain. Note any involuntary guarding, rigidity (board-like, suggesting peritonitis), or hyperaesthesia.

Proceed to deep palpation:
- Assess for localised tenderness in the RUQ — this is expected in cholecystitis and cholangitis
- Assess for rigidity, guarding, and rebound tenderness (peritonism) — their presence indicates severity and possible perforation; document their presence or absence explicitly in all cases regardless of the suspected diagnosis
- Palpate for hepatomegaly — position fingers parallel to the right costal margin, advancing upward from the right iliac fossa on expiration; note the liver edge, texture (smooth vs. irregular/nodular), and tenderness
- Palpate for splenomegaly — begin in the right iliac fossa advancing toward the left costal margin
- Palpate for a palpable gallbladder — a distended, palpable gallbladder (Courvoisier's sign) is classically associated with malignant biliary obstruction (pancreatic head cancer) rather than stones, as chronic stone disease fibroses and shrinks the gallbladder

**Percussion**

Percuss for hepatic dullness and splenic dullness. Assess for ascites (shifting dullness and fluid thrill) if there is clinical suspicion of chronic liver disease. Tympanism over a visibly distended abdomen suggests bowel obstruction or ileus.

**Auscultation**

Listen for bowel sounds in all four quadrants. Absent bowel sounds suggest ileus or peritonitis. Active, high-pitched tinkling sounds suggest bowel obstruction. Normal bowel sounds do not exclude significant intra-abdominal pathology.

**Inguinal region and digital rectal examination**

A complete abdominal examination includes inspection of the inguinal regions for herniae and examination of the external genitalia where clinically relevant. A digital rectal examination (DRE) must be performed as part of any complete acute abdominal pain assessment — the only contraindications are the absence of digits or the absence of an anus. Rectal examination may yield blood on the glove or identify a rectal mass, both of which carry diagnostic significance.

### Murphy's Sign — Technique, Interpretation, and Clinical Significance

Murphy's sign is the defining special test for acute cholecystitis and is a mandatory component of the physical examination in any patient with RUQ pain.

**Technique:**
1. Ask the patient to breathe out fully. At full expiration, the diaphragm ascends and the liver and gallbladder rise superiorly within the abdomen, away from the examining hand.
2. Place two to three fingers deep beneath the right costal margin at the mid-clavicular line (the anatomical surface marking of the gallbladder fundus).
3. Maintain firm, constant, deep pressure at this point.
4. Ask the patient to breathe in slowly and deeply. During inspiration, the diaphragm descends, carrying the liver and gallbladder inferiorly toward the examiner's fingers.
5. A **positive Murphy's sign** is elicited when the descending inflamed gallbladder contacts the examiner's fingers, producing a sudden arrest of inspiration due to pain — the patient gasps and involuntarily stops breathing in.

**Interpretation:**
- A positive Murphy's sign is highly suggestive of acute cholecystitis, indicating peritoneal inflammation surrounding the gallbladder.
- A positive Murphy's sign in the presence of jaundice and fever constitutes Charcot's triad, indicating cholangitis rather than isolated cholecystitis.
- A negative Murphy's sign does not exclude cholecystitis, particularly in elderly or immunocompromised patients who may have blunted inflammatory responses.
- Simple deep palpation tenderness in the RUQ without the inspiratory arrest criterion does **not** constitute a positive Murphy's sign.

**Systematic respiratory and cardiovascular examination**

In any acute abdominal pain case, a brief respiratory examination must be performed to exclude basal pneumonia or pleuritis (dullness, reduced breath sounds, or crackles at the right base may indicate right lower lobe consolidation presenting as RUQ pain). A focused cardiovascular examination (auscultation of heart sounds for S1, S2, and murmurs) is mandatory to exclude referred cardiac pathology.

**Office-based test — urine dipstick**

Urine dipstick is a mandatory bedside test. In the context of RUQ pain with possible biliary obstruction, the most critical finding is **bilirubinuria** (bilirubin detected on dipstick), which indicates conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia and points toward obstructive jaundice from biliary pathology or hepatocellular disease. Urobilinogen may also be elevated in haemolytic jaundice. The urine dipstick is a simple, immediately available, high-yield office test that must be included in every hepatobiliary assessment.

### The Diagnostic Triad — Differentiating the Three Biliary Conditions

The three biliary conditions share a common anatomical origin but differ critically in their clinical features, severity, and urgency of management. The following framework enables precise clinical differentiation:

**Biliary Colic**

- Stone present within the gallbladder but not obstructing the cystic or common bile duct
- Pain: episodic, self-resolving (typically within 2–6 hours), postprandial (precipitated by fatty meals), located in RUQ or epigastrium, radiates to right shoulder tip or right infrascapular region
- Nausea may accompany pain; vomiting is less common
- **No fever**
- **No jaundice**
- **Examination: normal** — no tenderness, no Murphy's sign, no peritonism
- The absence of examination findings in the context of a classic history is itself diagnostically significant

**Acute Cholecystitis**

- Stone obstructing the cystic duct causing gallbladder outflow obstruction, gallbladder distension, and mural inflammation
- Pain: constant, persistent, severe, does not spontaneously resolve; located in RUQ; radiation to shoulder or back; may be worse on inspiration
- Nausea and vomiting are prominent
- Fever: present in many cases (though not universally — fever is supportive, not mandatory, for the diagnosis)
- Jaundice: may be present in approximately 15% of cases due to adjacent hepatic inflammation or Mirizzi syndrome — but jaundice combined with high fever is cholangitis until proven otherwise
- **Examination: RUQ tenderness, positive Murphy's sign**; localised peritonism (guarding, rebound) may be present; palpable gallbladder occasionally detected
- Diagnosis confirmed by ultrasound

**Acute Cholangitis**

- Bacterial infection within the biliary ductal system (intrahepatic or extrahepatic bile ducts) most commonly secondary to choledocholithiasis (common bile duct stone)
- Other causes: malignant obstruction (pancreatic head cancer, cholangiocarcinoma), post-surgical stricture, autoimmune stricture (primary sclerosing cholangitis)
- **Charcot's Triad (the diagnostic sine qua non):**
  1. **Fever** (often high-grade, with rigors)
  2. **Jaundice**
  3. **Right upper quadrant pain**
- The presence of all three features mandates a diagnosis of cholangitis until proven otherwise
- Reynold's Pentad (severe/septic cholangitis): Charcot's Triad + altered consciousness + hypotension — indicates septic shock and demands immediate resuscitation and emergency intervention
- Murphy's sign may be positive, but jaundice distinguishes cholangitis from cholecystitis
- This is a **life-threatening condition** requiring urgent hospital admission, IV antibiotics, IV fluids, and urgent biliary decompression (ERCP or percutaneous drainage)

## Differential Diagnosis

| Category | Diagnosis | Key Distinguishing Features |
|----------|-----------|----------------------------|
| Common | Biliary colic | Episodic RUQ pain, postprandial, self-resolving, normal examination, no fever, no jaundice |
| Common | Acute cholecystitis | Constant RUQ pain, positive Murphy's sign, fever ± jaundice, ultrasound confirms |
| Common | Acute cholangitis | Charcot's triad: fever + jaundice + RUQ pain; URGENT management required |
| Common | Peptic ulcer disease | Epigastric pain, relationship to food (relieved or worsened), acid brash, history of NSAIDs |
| Common | Gastro-oesophageal reflux | Heartburn, acid regurgitation, worse lying flat and postprandially, no fever |
| Important | Acute pancreatitis | Epigastric pain radiating to back, worse on lying flat, relieved leaning forward, elevated lipase |
| Important | Acute hepatitis | Prodromal illness, jaundice, RUQ discomfort, elevated transaminases, hepatitis serology positive |
| Important | Right basal pneumonia | Respiratory symptoms, reduced breath sounds, fever, dullness at right base, pleuritic component |
| Important | Pancreatic head cancer | Painless progressive jaundice, weight loss, Courvoisier's sign (palpable gallbladder), elderly |
| Important | Hepatocellular carcinoma / liver metastases | Weight loss, anorexia, hepatomegaly (irregular), abnormal LFTs, imaging |
| Must Not Miss | Acute coronary syndrome | Chest pressure/tightness, diaphoresis, radiation to jaw or left arm, ECG changes, troponin rise |
| Must Not Miss | Cholangiocarcinoma | Obstructive jaundice, weight loss, biliary stricture on imaging, particularly in PSC patients |
| Must Not Miss | Ascending cholangitis with sepsis | Reynold's pentad — confusion + hypotension added to Charcot's triad, life-threatening emergency |

## Red Flags — Must Not Miss

- **Fever + Jaundice + RUQ Pain (Charcot's Triad)** — diagnostic of cholangitis; life-threatening biliary sepsis; requires immediate hospital admission, IV antibiotics, IV fluid resuscitation, and urgent biliary decompression (ERCP or percutaneous cholecystostomy); do not delay management awaiting imaging confirmation

- **Altered consciousness or hypotension in addition to Charcot's Triad (Reynold's Pentad)** — indicates septic shock secondary to cholangitis; immediate resuscitation, ICU-level care, and emergency biliary decompression required; mortality is high without immediate intervention

- **Involuntary guarding, board-like rigidity, or generalised rebound tenderness** — suggests perforation of the gallbladder with generalised peritonitis; surgical emergency requiring immediate surgical consultation

- **Progressive jaundice without fever in a patient over 50** — raises strong suspicion for malignant biliary obstruction (pancreatic head cancer, cholangiocarcinoma); Courvoisier's sign (palpable non-tender gallbladder) further supports this; requires urgent imaging (CT abdomen/pelvis) and oncology/surgical referral

- **Unintentional weight loss, anorexia, or palpable abdominal mass** — red flags for underlying hepatobiliary or gastrointestinal malignancy; urgent investigation pathway including CT scanning and tumour markers

- **Bilirubinuria on urine dipstick** — indicates conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia from biliary obstruction or hepatocellular damage; must not be attributed to simple biliary colic; warrants urgent LFTs, bilirubin fractionation, and imaging

- **Pain that is constant, worsening, and unresponsive to analgesia** — differentiates cholecystitis/cholangitis from biliary colic; suggests evolving inflammatory or obstructive pathology requiring inpatient assessment

- **New-onset upper abdominal pain with diaphoresis, radiation to the jaw or left arm, or known cardiovascular risk factors** — ACS must be excluded with ECG and troponin before attributing pain to biliary pathology

## Investigations

**Bedside:**
- Urine dipstick — test for bilirubin (conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia), urobilinogen, blood, and leucocytes; bilirubinuria indicates obstructive jaundice
- ECG — to exclude acute coronary syndrome in any patient with upper abdominal pain and cardiovascular risk factors
- Blood glucose level (BGL)

**Bloods:**
- Liver function tests (LFTs): bilirubin (total and direct), ALT, AST, ALP, GGT, albumin — ALP and GGT elevation with raised bilirubin suggests biliary obstruction; transaminase elevation suggests hepatocellular injury
- Full blood count (FBC): leucocytosis indicates acute inflammation or infection; thrombocytopenia may indicate sepsis or chronic liver disease
- C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR): raised in cholecystitis and cholangitis, correlates with severity
- Urea, electrolytes, and creatinine (UEC): assess hydration status and renal function, particularly in sepsis
- Serum lipase (or amylase): exclude acute pancreatitis
- Blood cultures: mandatory in fever with suspected cholangitis; guide antibiotic therapy
- Coagulation studies (INR/APTT): assess hepatic synthetic function; INR elevation indicates impaired hepatic function
- Group and hold: in any patient requiring urgent surgical or endoscopic intervention

**Imaging:**
- **Abdominal ultrasound** — first-line imaging of choice for biliary pathology; non-invasive, widely available, no radiation; identifies gallstones, gallbladder wall thickening (>3 mm), pericholecystic fluid (features of cholecystitis), common bile duct dilatation (>6 mm — suggests choledocholithiasis or distal obstruction), and hepatic lesions; a sonographic Murphy's sign may be elicited by the ultrasonographer
- **CT abdomen and pelvis with IV contrast** — indicated when diagnosis is uncertain, when complications are suspected (perforation, abscess, gangrenous cholecystitis), or when malignancy requires assessment; superior to ultrasound for visualising the common bile duct, pancreas, and surrounding structures
- **MRCP (magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography)** — non-invasive, high-resolution imaging of the biliary tree; preferred over diagnostic ERCP when choledocholithiasis is suspected but endoscopic intervention is not yet planned

**Specialist and procedural:**
- **ERCP (endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography)** — both diagnostic and therapeutic; the definitive intervention for choledocholithiasis with cholangitis; allows sphincterotomy, stone extraction, and biliary stenting; not a primary outpatient investigation
- **Hepatitis serology** (hepatitis A IgM, hepatitis B surface antigen, anti-HBc, hepatitis C antibody): when hepatitis is in the differential based on history and LFT pattern
- **Tumour markers** (CEA, CA 19-9, AFP): adjunct to imaging when hepatobiliary malignancy is suspected; not diagnostic in isolation

## Management

### Biliary Colic

**Acute:**
- Adequate analgesia — NSAIDs (e.g., diclofenac) are highly effective for biliary colic due to their prostaglandin inhibition reducing biliary pressure; opioid analgesia if NSAIDs are contraindicated
- Antiemetics if nausea or vomiting present (e.g., metoclopramide or ondansetron)
- Oral hydration if tolerated
- Discharge with dietary advice (low-fat diet reduces frequency of episodes) pending outpatient workup

**Ongoing:**
- Abdominal ultrasound confirming cholelithiasis
- Surgical outpatient referral for elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy — the definitive treatment for symptomatic cholelithiasis
- Patient education: avoid large fatty meals, seek urgent review if pain becomes constant, fever develops, or jaundice appears

### Acute Cholecystitis

**Acute (inpatient management):**
- Hospital admission — mandatory; this is not a condition to be managed in the community setting during an acute episode
- Nil by mouth or clear fluids only (bowel rest)
- IV fluid resuscitation — assess hydration status and replace deficits
- Adequate analgesia — IV or IM opioid analgesia (e.g., morphine or fentanyl); NSAIDs appropriate if renal function normal and no contraindications
- IV antibiotics — covering Gram-negative enteric organisms; common regimens include piperacillin-tazobactam, or co-amoxiclav with metronidazole; guided by local antibiogram and severity
- Antiemetics
- Monitor temperature, vital signs, urine output, and clinical response
- Repeat bloods at 24–48 hours to monitor inflammatory markers and LFTs

**Surgical management:**
- Surgical team review is mandatory in acute cholecystitis
- Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is the definitive treatment — removal of the gallbladder with its contained stones
- Timing: current evidence and Australian surgical practice supports early laparoscopic cholecystectomy within 72 hours of symptom onset when feasible, as this reduces recurrence, complications, and overall length of hospital stay compared to interval cholecystectomy
- In mild cases or when the patient is high surgical risk, conservative management with antibiotics and interval cholecystectomy at 4–6 weeks may be appropriate
- In frail or high-risk patients unable to tolerate surgery, percutaneous cholecystostomy (radiologically guided drainage of the gallbladder) provides temporising management

### Acute Cholangitis

**This is a medical emergency. Immediate resuscitation and treatment are required.**

- Immediate hospital admission to a high-dependency or ICU environment if haemodynamically unstable
- IV fluid resuscitation — aggressive if signs of sepsis or shock
- Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics covering Gram-negative enterobacteriaceae, anaerobes, and enterococci — piperacillin-tazobactam or meropenem in severe cases; blood cultures must be collected before antibiotic commencement
- Adequate analgesia
- Monitoring: vital signs every 15–30 minutes; urine output; lactate; repeat blood cultures if initial cultures negative and patient deteriorating
- Urgent biliary decompression — the cornerstone of cholangitis management; ERCP with sphincterotomy and stone extraction is the preferred modality; surgical common bile duct exploration or percutaneous transhepatic cholangiography (PTC) with drainage are alternatives if ERCP is not available or technically unsuccessful
- Gastroenterology or hepatobiliary surgery team involvement from the outset
- Interval laparoscopic cholecystectomy once the acute episode has resolved and the patient is fit for surgery

## Communication and Patient Education

**Explaining biliary colic:**
Introduce the gallbladder as a small sac behind the liver that stores bile, which helps the body digest fatty foods. Explain that small stones have formed inside the gallbladder, and that when a fatty meal triggers the gallbladder to contract, the stones can temporarily block the outlet, causing the pain. Reassure the patient that the pain resolves when the stone shifts back. Advise that a low-fat diet will reduce the frequency of episodes. Explain that the definitive treatment is a keyhole operation to remove the gallbladder, which the surgeon will discuss at the outpatient appointment.

**Explaining acute cholecystitis:**
Explain that one of the stones has become lodged in the tube exiting the gallbladder, causing the gallbladder to become inflamed and swollen. This is why the pain is constant and severe, rather than coming and going. Explain the need for hospital admission, intravenous antibiotics, intravenous fluids, and strong pain relief. Explain that the surgical team will assess the patient and plan to remove the gallbladder — most likely with a keyhole operation (laparoscopy) through two or three small incisions, which is a very common and safe procedure. Clarify that the surgery may be performed during this admission or planned for the near future depending on the surgeon's assessment.

**Explaining cholangitis:**
Explain that there is a serious infection in the bile ducts, most likely caused by a stone that has moved from the gallbladder into the bile ducts and is causing a blockage. Emphasise that this is a significant condition requiring urgent treatment including antibiotics through a drip and a procedure to clear the blocked duct. Use clear, non-alarming language while being honest about the seriousness of the condition. Inform the patient and family about the procedures involved and obtain consent as clinically appropriate.

**Questions to anticipate from the patient:**
- *"Will I need an operation?"* — Yes, likely; cholecystectomy is the definitive treatment and will be planned by the surgical team.
- *"Can I go home today?"* — Not with cholecystitis or cholangitis; a period of inpatient treatment is necessary.
- *"What caused the stones?"* — Multiple factors including diet, genetics, age, and female sex contribute; not always avoidable.
- *"Do I need to change my diet?"* — A low-fat diet reduces gallbladder contractions and may limit symptom frequency until surgery; after cholecystectomy most dietary restrictions can be relaxed.

## AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

- **Temperature is the single most important vital sign in this station.** Ask for it explicitly by name, every time, without exception. A candidate who elicits pain and jaundice but fails to ask for temperature cannot reliably distinguish cholecystitis from cholangitis, and this failure constitutes a critical examination error.

- **Jaundice is the single most important general appearance finding.** Before asking about pallor, dehydration, or any other general finding, ask the examiner: *"Is the patient jaundiced?"* Jaundice in combination with fever and pain is cholangitis until proven otherwise.

- **Murphy's sign must be performed and explained correctly.** Many candidates describe generalised RUQ tenderness as a positive Murphy's sign. This is incorrect. Murphy's sign requires inspiratory arrest — the patient must stop breathing in due to pain when the descending inflamed gallbladder contacts the examining hand. The correct technique must be demonstrated or described step-by-step if asked.

- **Never diagnose cholangitis without all three components of Charcot's triad.** Pain alone, or pain with fever but no jaundice, is cholecystitis. Pain with jaundice but no fever may be biliary obstruction without infection. Only the triad of all three — fever, jaundice, and RUQ pain — establishes cholangitis as the working diagnosis.

- **Never diagnose cholecystitis in a patient who has only had episodic, self-resolving, meal-related pain with a normal examination.** This is biliary colic. The examination findings — specifically the presence or absence of tenderness and Murphy's sign — are the critical differentiating factor, not the history alone.

- **Urine dipstick (urinalysis) is a mandatory office-based test.** Specify that you are specifically looking for bilirubin on the dipstick, which indicates conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia from biliary obstruction or hepatocellular disease.

- **For investigations, lead with your two most important tests.** In cholecystitis or suspected biliary pathology, these are: (1) LFTs and (2) abdominal ultrasound. Do not lead with a generic list of bloods. Demonstrate that you understand which investigations are most diagnostically informative for this specific presentation.

- **When explaining the diagnosis to the patient, always provide the reasons for your diagnosis.** Do not simply name the condition — explain the specific features that led you to that diagnosis (e.g., *"The positive Murphy's sign on examination indicates gallbladder inflammation"* or *"The combination of your fever, yellow skin, and pain in the same location tells me there is an infection in the bile ducts"*). Examiners specifically assess the quality of diagnostic reasoning communicated to the patient.

- **In stations beginning with physical examination (no history), still structure your examination from general appearance to abdomen to special tests.** Do not go directly to Murphy's sign without first establishing general appearance, vital signs, and completing systematic abdominal inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation. However, allocate extra time to examination technique and findings articulation, as this is the primary assessed domain in examination-first stations.

- **Include a DRE as part of any complete acute abdominal pain examination.** There is no clinical justification for omitting it in an acute abdominal pain case. The AMC expects a complete examination approach; selective omission of routine components suggests incomplete clinical training.

- **Do not conflate the causes and the condition.** When asked about the cause of cholecystitis or cholangitis, the answer is most commonly cholelithiasis (gallstones). However, cholangitis may also be caused by malignancy or stricture — demonstrate awareness of this, particularly in patients without a clear stone history.

- **In the management of cholecystitis, include all four pillars explicitly:** admission, analgesia, IV fluids, IV antibiotics, and surgical referral for cholecystectomy. Omitting any of these in a structured management response constitutes an incomplete answer. The final treatment is surgical removal of the gallbladder — do not end the management discussion at antibiotics.

## Safety Net

**For biliary colic managed in the community:**
Instruct the patient to return to the emergency department immediately if any of the following develop:
- Pain that becomes constant and does not resolve within six hours
- Development of fever or rigors (shaking chills)
- Yellowing of the skin or eyes, or darkening of urine
- Severe vomiting preventing oral intake
- New right shoulder or back pain in conjunction with abdominal pain that is not resolving

Advise adherence to a low-fat diet until the outpatient surgical appointment. Provide written information about gallstones and cholecystitis in plain English (available through the Royal Australasian College of Surgeons and major Australian hospital systems). Ensure a clear pathway to the outpatient surgical clinic with a defined timeline (typically within four to six weeks for uncomplicated biliary colic).

**For discharged post-cholecystitis patients (following inpatient treatment):**
Advise return to the emergency department if fever returns, pain recurs, jaundice develops, or the patient is unable to tolerate oral intake. Reinforce the importance of attending the planned surgical follow-up appointment for interval cholecystectomy. Provide contact details for the surgical outpatient clinic.

**For cholangitis:**
This condition requires inpatient management and should not be managed in the community. The safety net discussion occurs at the time of discharge following definitive treatment — at this point, patients should be counselled that recurrence is possible if the underlying cause (stones, stricture, or malignancy) has not been definitively addressed, and that any return of fever, jaundice, or abdominal pain warrants immediate emergency department review.
