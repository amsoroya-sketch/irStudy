# Requesting Physical Examination Findings from the Examiner — AMC Clinical Examination Framework

## Clinical Presentation

In the AMC Clinical Examination, the physical examination component of a structured station requires candidates to actively elicit clinical findings from the examiner in a systematic, specific, and time-efficient manner. Rather than performing hands-on examination of a real patient, the candidate directs the examiner through a structured verbal request process — asking for each specific finding individually and in logical sequence. This format is distinct from other clinical examination settings and demands a disciplined, pre-planned framework.

The ability to request physical examination findings effectively is particularly critical in stations presenting with acute or subacute abdominal pain — a high-frequency scenario in the AMC Clinical Examination. These stations may involve presentations such as right lower quadrant pain (appendicitis, ectopic pregnancy, ovarian pathology), upper abdominal pain (hepatobiliary disease, peptic ulcer, lower lobe pneumonia), or diffuse abdominal pain (peritonitis, bowel obstruction). The candidate must demonstrate that they understand not only the sequence of examination but also the clinical rationale for each step.

The AMC marking framework for physical examination specifically assesses choice, organisation, and sequence of the examination requested. Examiners are instructed not to volunteer findings unless the candidate asks for each component with sufficient specificity. A vague request — such as "I would like to examine the abdomen" — will prompt the examiner to ask, "What are you looking for?" This response wastes time, disrupts the examination flow, and costs marks. Mastery of this component requires candidates to practise directing a complete, logically sequenced physical examination within approximately two minutes, using a pre-prepared structural framework tailored to the presenting complaint.

---

## Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

The AMC examiners scoring the physical examination component evaluate candidates against three core criteria:

**1. Choice:** Is the examination requested relevant to the presenting complaint? Does the candidate identify the correct key system as the primary focus, and do they appropriately incorporate relevant secondary systems?

**2. Organisation and Sequence:** Is the examination logically ordered — from general observation to vital signs, through the key system in full, to relevant secondary systems, and finally to relevant point-of-care (office) tests? Is there a coherent clinical structure that reflects sound medical reasoning?

**3. Completeness:** Does the candidate ask for each specific element within the key examination system? Omitting components of the primary system — for example, failing to ask about percussion tenderness or rectal examination in a patient presenting with abdominal pain — will result in significant mark deductions.

The formal AMC mark sheet specifies that candidates must ask for each specific component of the examination, and that findings should not be offered unless specifically requested. This places the full burden of direction on the candidate. A well-prepared candidate approaches this component with a pre-constructed framework they can deliver fluently regardless of examination pressure. The framework must be adapted at the case level to reflect the presenting complaint, the differential diagnoses under consideration, and the specific clinical context (upper versus lower abdominal pain, male versus female patient, acute versus chronic presentation).

Within the context of a diagnostic formulation station, the physical examination request component is typically allocated approximately two minutes. This time constraint demands efficient sequencing: the candidate must complete the full key system examination and relevant secondary systems, then move to office tests, all within this window.

## Systematic Approach

### Step 1 — General Appearance

General appearance is the appropriate starting point for any physical examination sequence. It signals to the examiner that the candidate follows a logical, head-to-toe approach and does not overlook important clinical information observable from the end of the bed.

In the context of abdominal pain, the following general appearance findings carry the greatest clinical significance and should be requested:

- **Level of consciousness:** Is the patient alert and oriented, or drowsy? Depressed conscious state in the context of abdominal pain suggests haemodynamic compromise, septic shock, or a surgical emergency.
- **Hydration status:** Does the patient appear dehydrated — dry mucous membranes, sunken eyes, reduced skin turgor? Dehydration has significant implications for acute management, particularly in the context of vomiting, diarrhoea, or third-space fluid losses.
- **Pallor:** Does the patient appear pale? Pallor raises concern for significant haemorrhage — notably intraperitoneal bleeding from ruptured ectopic pregnancy or ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm.

Additional general appearance features (jaundice, lymphadenopathy, oedema, cyanosis) should be incorporated selectively when directly relevant to the differential diagnosis. For example, jaundice is relevant in right upper quadrant or epigastric pain but would be an irrelevant finding to emphasise in a right lower quadrant pain presentation. Candidates should resist the temptation to recite a lengthy list of general appearance features, as this consumes time and signals poor clinical prioritisation.

---

### Step 2 — Vital Signs

Vital signs should be requested as a standard component of every physical examination sequence, regardless of the presenting complaint. The clinical rationale is that vital sign abnormalities frequently redefine or expand the differential diagnosis.

The standard vital sign set comprises:
- Blood pressure (including postural blood pressure measurement)
- Pulse rate and character
- Respiratory rate
- Temperature
- Oxygen saturation

If haemodynamic assessment was already performed at the beginning of the station, the candidate should note that they have already obtained blood pressure, pulse rate, respiratory rate, and temperature, and direct the examiner specifically to additional vital sign information not yet obtained — most importantly:

- **Postural blood pressure:** A postural drop of ≥20 mmHg systolic or ≥10 mmHg diastolic on standing indicates clinically significant volume depletion, and may be the only vital sign abnormality in early haemorrhagic or dehydrated states.
- **Oxygen saturation:** Relevant in any patient with upper abdominal pain where lower lobe pneumonia is a differential consideration.

Requesting vital signs at this stage also provides the opportunity to detect early sepsis (tachycardia, fever, hypotension), ruptured viscus (tachycardia, hypotension), or an incidental finding that changes the clinical picture entirely (e.g., pyrexia of 39.5°C in a patient presenting with leg pain raises cellulitis alongside peripheral arterial disease).

---

### Step 3 — Key System Examination (Abdominal Examination)

For any presentation of acute or subacute abdominal pain, the abdominal examination is the key system and must be performed in its entirety. Every element must be requested from the examiner with specificity. Incomplete abdominal examination — for example, performing palpation and auscultation but omitting percussion, inguinal examination, or rectal examination — will attract significant mark deductions.

The complete abdominal examination proceeds as follows:

#### Inspection
Direct the examiner with specific, answerable questions rather than broad requests:
- "On inspection, is the abdomen moving symmetrically with respiration?" — Restricted abdominal movement suggests peritoneal irritation.
- "Do I see any Cullen's sign or Grey Turner's sign?" — Periumbilical or flank bruising indicating retroperitoneal haemorrhage (pancreatitis, AAA rupture).
- "Is there abdominal distension?" — Suggests bowel obstruction, ascites, or organomegaly.
- Visible peristalsis or surgical scars may also be noted if clinically relevant.

#### Superficial and Deep Palpation
This is the most clinically rich component of abdominal examination and must include:

- **Tenderness:** "On palpation, do I have any tenderness?" — Follow with: "Where is the tenderness localised?"
- **Guarding:** "Is there guarding?" — Voluntary guarding indicates pain avoidance; involuntary guarding suggests peritoneal irritation.
- **Rigidity:** "Is there rigidity?" — Board-like rigidity is a classical sign of generalised peritonitis.
- **Rebound tenderness:** "Is there rebound tenderness?" — A key sign of peritoneal inflammation; positive in appendicitis (Blumberg's sign), pelvic inflammatory disease, and perforated viscus.
- **Masses:** "Do I feel any masses?" — Relevant across a broad differential including malignancy, inflammatory mass, or ectopic pregnancy.

#### Percussion
- "On percussion, is there percussion tenderness?" — Percussion tenderness parallels rebound tenderness as a marker of peritoneal irritation, with slightly less discomfort to the patient.
- Note: shifting dullness is relevant if ascites is suspected; this should be incorporated if the differential includes hepatic or ovarian pathology.

#### Auscultation
- "On auscultation, are bowel sounds present?" — Absent bowel sounds indicate ileus or late obstruction; high-pitched, tinkling sounds suggest early mechanical obstruction; normal bowel sounds do not exclude pathology.

#### Inguinal Area Examination
- "I would like to examine the inguinal region. Are there any lumps or swellings?" — Assesses for inguinal hernia (incarcerated or obstructed), lymphadenopathy, or femoral hernia, all of which may present with lower abdominal or groin pain.

#### Rectal Examination
- "I would like to perform a rectal examination." — Every patient presenting with abdominal pain requires a digital rectal examination unless anatomically impossible. This is a non-negotiable component of the abdominal examination in the AMC Clinical Examination context. Rectal examination may reveal:
  - Faecal impaction
  - Rectal mass
  - Tenderness (anterior tenderness in appendicitis or pelvic pathology)
  - Blood on the examining finger (gastrointestinal bleeding, colorectal malignancy)

### Step 4 — Relevant Secondary System Examinations

Following completion of the key system examination, the candidate must consider which secondary systems are clinically relevant to the specific presentation. The choice of secondary systems must be defensible based on the differential diagnosis generated during history taking.

#### Lower Abdominal Pain — Pelvic Examination (Female Patients)

Before proceeding, the candidate must obtain consent and request the presence of a chaperone:
- "I would like to perform a pelvic examination with my patient's consent and with a chaperone present."

**Inspection:**
- "On inspection of the external genitalia, do I see any redness or rashes?" — Relevant for vulvovaginitis, Bartholin's abscess, herpes simplex.
- "Are there any lumps or swellings?" — Bartholin's cyst/abscess, vulval lesions, prolapse.
- "Is there any bleeding or discharge visible?" — Relevant for ectopic pregnancy, cervical pathology, PID.

**Speculum Examination:**
- "On speculum examination, tell me about the cervix — is there any bleeding or discharge?" — Cervical excitation discharge is characteristic of cervicitis; bleeding from the os raises concern for ectopic pregnancy, cervical malignancy, or threatened miscarriage.
- "Tell me about the vaginal walls — any abnormality?" — Assesses vaginal inflammation, lacerations, foreign body.

**Bimanual Examination:**
- "On bimanual examination, is there cervical motion tenderness?" — A cardinal sign of pelvic inflammatory disease; also present in ectopic pregnancy.
- "Tell me about the uterus — what is the size, and is it tender?" — A bulky tender uterus suggests endometritis or adenomyosis; an empty, non-enlarged uterus in the context of a positive pregnancy test raises strong suspicion for ectopic pregnancy.
- "Tell me about the adnexae — are there any masses or tenderness?" — Adnexal mass with tenderness in a pregnant patient is highly suspicious for ectopic pregnancy; in non-pregnant patients, consider ovarian cyst, ovarian torsion, or tubo-ovarian abscess.

#### Lower Abdominal Pain — Testicular Examination (Male Patients)

With consent and chaperone:
- "On inspection, is there any scrotal swelling or redness?" — Epididymo-orchitis, testicular torsion.
- "On palpation, are there any scrotal masses or tenderness?" — Tenderness localised to the epididymis suggests epididymo-orchitis; a high-riding, horizontally oriented testicle with absent cremasteric reflex suggests torsion.

#### Upper Abdominal Pain — Cardiac Examination (Summarised)

- "On cardiac auscultation, is the heart rate and rhythm regular?" — Atrial fibrillation as a risk factor for mesenteric ischaemia; cardiac arrhythmia as a cause of upper abdominal symptoms.
- "Are there any murmurs?" — Aortic stenosis relevant in the context of superior mesenteric artery territory ischaemia; new murmurs in the context of systemic illness.

#### Upper Abdominal Pain — Respiratory Examination (Summarised)

- "On respiratory examination, is air entry equal bilaterally?" — Reduced air entry at the right base raises concern for right lower lobe pneumonia as a cause of right upper quadrant or epigastric pain.
- "Are there any added sounds — crepitations or bronchial breathing?" — Crepitations suggest consolidation or pulmonary oedema; bronchial breathing indicates lobar consolidation.

The secondary respiratory and cardiac examinations in the context of upper abdominal pain are brief and targeted — the candidate is not expected to perform a full respiratory or cardiovascular examination within the two-minute physical examination window. The purpose is to address key diagnostic possibilities, not to perform a comprehensive system examination.

### Step 5 — Point-of-Care (Office) Tests

Office tests are bedside investigations immediately available in general practice or emergency settings that provide rapid diagnostic information. They are considered part of the physical examination request in the AMC Clinical Examination and should be included as the final component of the structured physical examination sequence.

Office tests are distinct from formal laboratory investigations or imaging: they are rapid, inexpensive, immediately actionable, and accessible at the point of care. The candidate must select only those tests relevant to the clinical scenario and be prepared to justify each choice.

For abdominal pain presentations, the following office tests are most frequently appropriate:

- **Urinalysis (urine dipstick):** Relevant in virtually all abdominal pain presentations. Detects haematuria (renal calculus, renal malignancy), nitrites and leucocytes (urinary tract infection, pyelonephritis), protein (nephrotic syndrome, pre-eclampsia), and glucose (diabetic ketoacidosis presenting with abdominal pain). Urine dipstick is inexpensive, universally available, and provides immediate clinical information.

- **Urine pregnancy test:** Mandatory in any female patient of reproductive age presenting with lower abdominal pain, particularly in the context of missed or irregular periods, vaginal bleeding, or when ectopic pregnancy or complications of early intrauterine pregnancy are in the differential. A positive result fundamentally changes the clinical management pathway.

- **Capillary blood glucose (BSL):** Relevant when diabetic ketoacidosis is a differential consideration — for example, in a patient with known diabetes presenting with nausea, vomiting, and diffuse abdominal pain, or in an undiagnosed diabetic presenting with constitutional symptoms. Hyperglycaemia in this context requires urgent further evaluation.

- **Electrocardiogram (ECG):** While more commonly requested in chest pain presentations, an ECG is relevant in upper abdominal pain when inferior myocardial infarction must be excluded — a must-not-miss diagnosis that frequently presents as epigastric pain, nausea, and diaphoresis, particularly in elderly, diabetic, or female patients.

- **Urine dipstick for microscopy, culture, and sensitivity (MC&S) — mid-stream specimen:** If the dipstick suggests infection, a formal MSU specimen should be collected for laboratory analysis, though the initial dipstick result guides immediate management.

## Differential Diagnosis

The following differential diagnosis table applies to the high-frequency AMC examination scenario of acute abdominal pain:

| Category | Diagnosis | Key Distinguishing Features |
|----------|-----------|----------------------------|
| Common | Appendicitis | Right iliac fossa pain, anorexia, nausea, fever; rebound tenderness at McBurney's point; elevated WCC |
| Common | Urinary tract infection / pyelonephritis | Dysuria, frequency, loin pain, costovertebral angle tenderness; positive dipstick for nitrites and leucocytes |
| Common | Ovarian cyst (rupture or torsion) | Sudden onset unilateral pelvic pain in reproductive-age female; adnexal mass/tenderness on bimanual examination; negative pregnancy test |
| Common | Gastroenteritis | Diarrhoea, vomiting, colicky diffuse pain; no peritoneal signs; exposure history |
| Common | Constipation / faecal impaction | Chronic or subacute presentation; hard faeces on rectal examination; colonic loading on plain X-ray |
| Important | Pelvic inflammatory disease | Young sexually active female; cervical motion tenderness, adnexal tenderness, vaginal discharge; negative pregnancy test |
| Important | Renal colic | Colicky loin-to-groin pain, haematuria on dipstick; restlessness |
| Important | Bowel obstruction | Colicky pain, distension, absolute constipation, vomiting; high-pitched bowel sounds; dilated loops on X-ray |
| Important | Mesenteric ischaemia | Severe pain out of proportion to signs; atrial fibrillation; elderly patient; elevated lactate |
| Must Not Miss | Ectopic pregnancy | Reproductive-age female; amenorrhoea; unilateral pelvic pain; haemodynamic instability; positive pregnancy test; empty uterus on bimanual/ultrasound |
| Must Not Miss | Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm | Older male with vascular risk factors; severe abdominal/back pain; haemodynamic instability; pulsatile mass |
| Must Not Miss | Inferior myocardial infarction | Epigastric pain, nausea, diaphoresis; ECG changes (ST elevation in II, III, aVF); elderly, diabetic, or female patient |
| Must Not Miss | Perforated viscus | Sudden severe abdominal pain; board-like rigidity; absent bowel sounds; free air under diaphragm on erect CXR |

## Red Flags — Must Not Miss

- **Haemodynamic instability (hypotension, tachycardia, pallor)** — Suggests significant haemorrhage (ruptured ectopic pregnancy, AAA rupture, massive gastrointestinal bleed) or septic shock. Requires immediate resuscitation and urgent surgical/gynaecological review. Do not delay definitive management to complete investigation.

- **Positive pregnancy test with lower abdominal pain and haemodynamic compromise** — Ectopic pregnancy must be assumed until excluded. This is a life-threatening emergency. Urgent gynaecological review and bedside ultrasound are mandatory; do not await formal imaging if the patient is haemodynamically unstable.

- **Board-like rigidity with absent bowel sounds** — Indicates generalised peritonitis from perforated viscus (peptic ulcer, appendicitis, diverticulitis). Requires nil by mouth, intravenous access, analgesia, and urgent surgical review.

- **Fever with tachycardia and localised peritonism** — Meets criteria for intra-abdominal sepsis. Sepsis bundles should be initiated. Source control (surgical drainage or operative intervention) may be required urgently.

- **Postural hypotension with pallor** — Indicates clinically significant volume depletion, possibly from haemorrhage or dehydration. Even with a normal supine blood pressure, a postural drop identifies patients at risk of haemodynamic deterioration.

- **Epigastric pain with diaphoresis and nausea in the absence of localising abdominal signs** — Must prompt immediate ECG to exclude inferior myocardial infarction before attributing symptoms to a gastrointestinal cause.

- **Severe pain out of proportion to physical signs in an elderly or anticoagulated patient** — Raises concern for mesenteric ischaemia, a condition with high mortality if not diagnosed early. Lactate measurement and urgent vascular surgery review are required.

- **Cervical motion tenderness in a pregnant patient** — Positive cervical excitation in the context of a positive pregnancy test is highly suspicious for ectopic pregnancy and warrants urgent obstetric/gynaecological assessment.

## Investigations

**Bedside:**
- Urinalysis (urine dipstick — protein, blood, nitrites, leucocytes, glucose, ketones)
- Urine pregnancy test (all reproductive-age females)
- Capillary blood glucose (BSL)
- 12-lead ECG (epigastric pain, suspected MI, or cardiac risk factors)
- Temperature (if not already obtained as part of vital signs)
- Oxygen saturation via pulse oximetry

**Bloods:**
- Full blood count (FBC) — haemoglobin (haemorrhage, anaemia), white cell count (infection/inflammation)
- C-reactive protein (CRP) — acute phase marker of inflammation or infection
- Urea, electrolytes, creatinine (UEC) — renal function, electrolyte derangement
- Liver function tests (LFTs) — hepatobiliary pathology, jaundice
- Lipase or amylase — pancreatitis
- Lactate — mesenteric ischaemia, sepsis, haemodynamic compromise
- Coagulation studies — if haemorrhage or coagulopathy suspected
- Group and crossmatch — if surgical intervention anticipated
- Serum beta-hCG (quantitative) — if urine pregnancy test positive, to guide management of ectopic pregnancy
- Blood cultures (x2) — if sepsis suspected

**Imaging:**
- Transabdominal pelvic ultrasound — first-line for gynaecological pathology, gallstones, renal calculi, AAA screening
- Transvaginal ultrasound — superior to transabdominal in early pregnancy and pelvic pathology; essential to localise pregnancy and assess adnexae
- Erect chest X-ray — free air under diaphragm indicating perforated viscus
- Plain abdominal X-ray (AXR) — bowel obstruction, faecal loading, calcified renal calculi (limited utility in most other contexts)
- CT abdomen and pelvis with IV contrast — definitive imaging for most acute abdominal surgical conditions; preferred in suspected appendicitis, diverticulitis, bowel obstruction, mesenteric ischaemia

**Specialist:**
- Colonoscopy — in the elective setting, for lower gastrointestinal bleeding, change in bowel habit, or suspected colorectal malignancy (not acute)
- Proctosigmoidoscopy — point-of-care assessment of distal rectum and anal canal; available in rural or GP settings
- Laparoscopy — diagnostic and therapeutic in suspected ectopic pregnancy, appendicitis, or gynaecological pathology

## Management

**Acute Management (Emergency Setting):**

1. **Resuscitation first** — Airway, Breathing, Circulation. Establish large-bore intravenous access. Commence IV fluid resuscitation if haemodynamically compromised. Administer oxygen if saturations are reduced.

2. **Analgesia** — Adequate analgesia should not be withheld pending diagnosis. Intravenous morphine titrated to effect is appropriate for severe pain; there is no evidence that adequate analgesia masks clinically important physical signs.

3. **Nil by mouth** — In any patient where surgical intervention may be required (peritonism, suspected perforation, bowel obstruction, suspected ectopic pregnancy).

4. **Surgical or gynaecological referral** — Prompt in the context of peritonitis, suspected ectopic pregnancy, bowel obstruction, or AAA.

5. **Sepsis management** — If sepsis is suspected: blood cultures, IV antibiotics within one hour (broad-spectrum empirical coverage), IV fluids, urine output monitoring, lactate measurement. Refer to local sepsis management protocol.

**Specific Conditions:**

- **Appendicitis:** Surgical review for appendicectomy. Laparoscopic appendicectomy is the standard approach. Pre-operative IV antibiotics (e.g., cefazolin + metronidazole).

- **Ectopic pregnancy:** Immediate gynaecological review. Depending on haemodynamic stability and beta-hCG level: expectant management, medical management with methotrexate (haemodynamically stable, unruptured, declining beta-hCG), or surgical management (laparoscopic salpingectomy or salpingotomy).

- **Pelvic inflammatory disease:** Empirical antibiotic therapy covering Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, and anaerobes (e.g., doxycycline + metronidazole ± ceftriaxone, according to Australian STI management guidelines). Sexual health referral and partner notification.

- **Urinary tract infection/pyelonephritis:** Empirical antibiotics (trimethoprim or nitrofurantoin for uncomplicated lower UTI; cephalosporin or fluoroquinolone for pyelonephritis, guided by local resistance patterns). Adequate hydration. Urine MC&S to guide definitive therapy.

**Ongoing Management:**
- Review all investigation results and adjust management accordingly
- Patient education regarding diagnosis, treatment plan, and expected course
- Outpatient follow-up arranged as appropriate
- Ensure documentation of all clinical findings, investigations, and management decisions

## Communication and Patient Education

Effective communication at this station requires the candidate to demonstrate both clinical competence and patient-centred care, even during the physical examination component.

**Before Examination:**
- Introduce yourself, confirm patient identity, and explain what you are going to do and why
- Obtain informed consent for each component of the examination, particularly for intimate examinations (pelvic, testicular, rectal)
- Request a chaperone before any intimate examination — this is non-negotiable and must be verbalised explicitly in the station

**Addressing the Pelvic or Rectal Examination:**
- Explain to the patient what the examination involves and what you are looking for
- Reassure the patient that you will stop at any time if they are uncomfortable
- Ensure a same-gender chaperone is present or offered where possible
- Document consent and chaperone presence

**Explaining Findings:**
- After the physical examination, communicate findings to the patient in clear, non-technical language: "I found that when I press on the lower right part of your abdomen, it is quite tender. This is consistent with possible inflammation in that area, which is why we need to run some tests to find the exact cause."
- Avoid premature diagnostic labelling before investigation results are available
- Acknowledge uncertainty honestly: "We need some blood tests and possibly a scan to be sure of what is causing your pain."

**Key Patient Questions to Anticipate:**
- "Is it serious?" — Acknowledge their concern, explain the need for investigations, and reassure that you are taking their symptoms seriously.
- "Will I need an operation?" — Explain that this depends on the results of investigations and that you will discuss all options with them.
- "How long will I be here?" — Acknowledge uncertainty and commit to keeping them informed.

**Written Information and Follow-Up:**
- Provide written instructions regarding symptoms that should prompt return to the emergency department
- Arrange appropriate follow-up — general practitioner, gynaecologist, surgeon, or sexual health clinic as indicated
- Ensure the patient understands all medications prescribed (dosing, duration, side effects)

## AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

- **Ask for findings one specific element at a time.** A broad request such as "I would like to examine the abdomen" will prompt the examiner to ask "What are you looking for?" — wasting time and suggesting a lack of structure. Ask instead: "On inspection, is the abdomen moving symmetrically with respiration?"

- **Never omit rectal examination in an abdominal pain case.** Digital rectal examination is a mandatory component of the abdominal examination in any patient presenting with abdominal pain. Omitting it will cost significant marks and may be interpreted as incomplete clinical assessment.

- **Always request vital signs, specifically postural blood pressure.** Even if you obtained a standard blood pressure earlier in the station, explicitly requesting postural drop demonstrates clinical sophistication and may reveal haemodynamic compromise not apparent on supine measurement.

- **State consent and chaperone before any intimate examination — every time.** The examiner is specifically looking for this. In the examination context, failure to verbalise consent and request of a chaperone before pelvic, testicular, or rectal examination is a patient safety concern that can result in failing the station.

- **Tailor secondary systems to the clinical context.** Upper abdominal pain warrants a brief cardiac and respiratory examination; lower abdominal pain warrants pelvic or testicular examination. Do not perform secondary examinations that are irrelevant to the presenting differential.

- **Office tests are part of the physical examination — include them.** Urine dipstick and urine pregnancy test (in reproductive-age females) should be requested as standard in abdominal pain stations. Failing to include office tests leaves a gap in your clinical assessment that the examiner will note.

- **Jaundice, lymphadenopathy, and oedema are NOT default general appearance findings.** Include only those general appearance features directly relevant to the differential diagnoses generated in history taking. Reciting a complete list of general appearance findings wastes time and signals poor clinical prioritisation.

- **Complete the key system fully before moving to secondary systems.** Do not move to a pelvic or cardiac examination before completing all elements of the abdominal examination. The primary system examination must be complete and uninterrupted.

- **Practise delivering the full physical examination sequence aloud within two minutes.** Time pressure is a significant cause of failure in this component. Without timed practice, candidates frequently exceed the allocated window and run out of time for differential diagnosis and management discussion.

- **ECG is an office test — not an investigation.** When epigastric pain or upper abdominal pain is in the differential and inferior myocardial infarction must be excluded, request an ECG as an office test during the physical examination component, not as a subsequent investigation.

- **Percussion tenderness and rebound tenderness are distinct findings — ask for both.** In the context of suspected peritoneal irritation, both percussion tenderness and rebound tenderness should be specifically requested, as either may be positive and the distinction may be clinically relevant.

- **Bimanual examination must include three components: cervical motion tenderness, uterine assessment, and adnexal assessment.** Candidates who ask only about cervical motion tenderness and move on will miss adnexal masses or tenderness that may be the key examination finding (e.g., adnexal mass in ectopic pregnancy).

## Safety Net

The following safety net instructions are appropriate for a patient presenting with acute or subacute abdominal pain who is being assessed or discharged from an acute setting:

**Return Immediately to the Emergency Department if You Experience:**
- Sudden severe worsening of abdominal pain, particularly if the pain becomes constant rather than colicky
- Rigidity or board-like hardness of the abdomen
- Fainting, dizziness, or feeling as though you might lose consciousness — especially when standing up
- Heavy vaginal bleeding (if female and pregnant or potentially pregnant)
- Vomiting blood or passing blood per rectum
- High fever (temperature above 38.5°C) unresponsive to paracetamol
- Inability to keep down any oral fluids
- No urine output for more than eight hours

**Follow-Up Arrangements:**
- Review with your general practitioner within 24–48 hours if discharged home
- Return for all scheduled investigation results (blood tests, urine culture, ultrasound)
- Attend the gynaecology or surgical clinic as arranged if referral has been made
- Do not delay seeking care if symptoms deteriorate before your scheduled appointment

**Medications and Self-Care:**
- Take all prescribed medications as directed; complete the full course of any antibiotics
- Maintain adequate oral hydration; consider oral rehydration solutions if vomiting has been a feature
- Avoid anti-inflammatory medications (ibuprofen, naproxen, aspirin) until the cause of abdominal pain has been confirmed, as these may worsen gastrointestinal mucosal pathology
- Written information about your diagnosis and management plan has been provided; keep this for reference

**Contact Details:**
- In an emergency: call 000 (triple zero) or present directly to your nearest emergency department
- For non-urgent concerns: contact your general practitioner during business hours
