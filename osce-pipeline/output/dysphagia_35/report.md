# Dysphagia — AMC Clinical Examination History Taking Station

## Clinical Presentation

A 57-year-old woman presents to a general practice or outpatient setting with a new complaint of difficulty swallowing. Dysphagia in this age group demands a structured, systematic approach because the differential diagnosis spans a spectrum from benign oesophageal strictures to life-threatening malignancy. The clinical imperative is to characterise the dysphagia precisely — localising it anatomically, defining its pattern in relation to food consistency, and screening rigorously for red flags that mandate urgent investigation.

This station is one of the most diagnostically demanding history-taking presentations in the AMC Clinical Examination because it requires candidates to simultaneously pursue multiple diagnostic pathways: malignancy, structural obstruction, motility dysfunction, and neurological causes. A candidate who defaults to a single diagnosis without methodically ruling out cancer will fail to demonstrate the clinical reasoning expected at this level. The station is designed specifically to test red flag identification, and in many iterations the correct final diagnosis depends entirely upon whether the candidate elicits the presence or absence of systemic red flags.

The clinical importance of this presentation extends beyond the examination room. Dysphagia carries a population risk of significant underlying pathology — oesophageal adenocarcinoma is rising in incidence in Australia, with Barrett's oesophagus secondary to chronic gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) representing a well-established precursor. In any patient over 50 with progressive dysphagia, the working diagnosis must include malignancy until endoscopic evaluation proves otherwise.

---

## Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

The examiner expects a structured, efficient history within six minutes that demonstrates four core competencies: systematic symptom characterisation using an organised framework; methodical differential diagnosis reasoning; red flag identification with appropriate clinical response; and clear, empathic communication with the patient.

**Opening and Rapport:** Introduce yourself, confirm the patient's identity, and invite the patient to describe the problem in their own words with an open-ended question. Respond specifically to what the patient volunteers — do not default to a formulaic empathic statement that does not match what was actually said. If the patient says "food is sticking when I swallow," acknowledge that specific concern rather than offering a generic "I understand you're worried."

**Systematic History Taking:** Progress logically from symptom characterisation to anatomical localisation to pattern analysis to associated symptoms to red flag screening to relevant past history and medications. Do not jump between domains or collect isolated facts without a clear investigative logic.

**ICE (Ideas, Concerns, Expectations):** Elicit the patient's own beliefs about the cause, their primary concerns (many will fear cancer), and what they hope will come from this consultation. In the context of dysphagia in a middle-aged woman with risk factors, the concern about cancer is both common and appropriate — it must be acknowledged directly and addressed with honesty.

**Closing:** Summarise your findings, offer your working diagnosis with a brief explanation in lay terms, provide a clear management plan including the need for urgent investigation, and provide a safety net.

## Systematic Approach

### Step 1 — Symptom Characterisation: Dysphagia Versus Odynophagia

The first clinical task is to precisely define what the patient means by "difficulty swallowing." This distinction has direct diagnostic implications and should be the opening characterisation question.

**Dysphagia** refers to a mechanical or functional difficulty in moving a bolus from the mouth to the stomach. The patient experiences food as difficult to move, catching, or sticking — but the act of swallowing is not painful.

**Odynophagia** refers to pain on swallowing. This may co-exist with dysphagia but represents a distinct symptom with a different differential — including oesophageal ulceration, severe oesophagitis, candidal oesophagitis, and oesophageal spasm.

**Ask:** *"When you say difficulty swallowing — can you tell me more about that? Is it that the food won't go down, or is it that swallowing is painful?"*

Establishing this distinction immediately sharpens the diagnostic pathway. The remainder of the characterisation assumes the patient has true dysphagia.

---

### Step 2 — Anatomical Localisation: Oropharyngeal Versus Oesophageal

Anatomical localisation is achieved by asking about the timing and site of symptoms within the swallowing sequence.

**Oropharyngeal (High) Dysphagia:**
- Difficulty initiating the swallow — the patient cannot get the bolus started
- Choking or coughing immediately upon swallowing as food enters the airway
- Nasal regurgitation of food or liquid
- Changes in voice quality — wet, gurgling, or hoarse voice — reflecting pooling of secretions or vocal cord involvement
- Drooling or inability to manage oral secretions
- This pattern suggests a problem at the level of the pharynx, larynx, or upper oesophageal sphincter — raising differentials of neurological disease, head and neck malignancy, Zenker's diverticulum, or pharyngeal abscess

**Ask:** *"Does the difficulty happen right at the start — when you try to start swallowing? Do you choke or cough when you swallow? Does food ever come back out through your nose? Has your voice changed at all?"*

**Oesophageal (Low) Dysphagia:**
- Difficulty occurs seconds after initiating the swallow, once food enters the oesophagus
- Sensation of food sticking, catching, or not going down — localised to the mid-chest or substernal region
- The swallowing initiation itself is normal

**Ask:** *"Do you feel the food getting stuck — and if so, where? Does it feel like it catches in your throat, or more in your chest?"*

Note that patient localisation of oesophageal obstruction is often imprecise — they may point to the lower neck or upper sternum even when the obstruction is at the gastro-oesophageal junction. Treat the reported location as an approximate guide only.

---

### Step 3 — Timing and Progression

**Duration:** When did the dysphagia begin? Has it been present for six weeks or six months? Progressive dysphagia of any duration in a patient with risk factors for malignancy is urgent.

**Frequency and Pattern:** Is it present with every meal, or intermittent? Intermittent dysphagia to solids and liquids suggests a functional or motility cause (oesophageal spasm). Progressive daily dysphagia to solids suggests structural obstruction — either malignancy or stricture.

**Progression:** Is it worsening over time? A gradually worsening course with initial difficulty for solids progressing to difficulty with soft foods and eventually liquids is highly characteristic of oesophageal carcinoma. This pattern of progressive dysphagia is a critical red flag.

**Ask:** *"When did you first notice this? Has it been happening every time you eat, or does it come and go? Overall, would you say it is getting gradually worse over time?"*

---

### Step 4 — Pattern of Dysphagia: Solids, Liquids, or Both

This is one of the highest-yield questions in the station and directly distinguishes structural obstruction from motility dysfunction.

**Dysphagia to solids only:**
Suggests a structural or obstructive lesion — carcinoma, benign stricture, oesophageal web, or extrinsic compression. Initially the lumen may accommodate liquids but not solid boluses. As an obstruction worsens, even liquids become difficult.

**Dysphagia to both solids and liquids from the outset:**
Suggests a motility disorder — achalasia, diffuse oesophageal spasm, systemic sclerosis, or neuromuscular disease. In motility disorders, the oesophagus cannot propel any bolus regardless of consistency.

**Ask:** *"Does the difficulty happen with all foods — solids and liquids equally — or mainly with solid foods?"*

In achalasia specifically, patients may report difficulty with both consistencies, may describe food regurgitation hours after eating (particularly at night), and may have found that increasing intra-abdominal pressure (standing, performing a Valsalva manoeuvre) helps food pass — a phenomenon not seen in carcinoma.

---

### Step 5 — Associated Gastrointestinal Symptoms

Dysphagia is a gastrointestinal symptom and must be assessed in the context of the full gastrointestinal system review.

**Heartburn and GORD Symptoms:**
- Heartburn — retrosternal burning, typically worse postprandially and on lying flat
- Acid or metallic taste in the mouth (waterbrash or acid reflux)
- Sudden hypersalivation — a reflex response to oesophageal acid exposure
- Nocturnal regurgitation
- Prior use of proton pump inhibitors or H2 antagonists, and whether these have provided relief

Chronic, inadequately treated GORD is the primary risk factor for Barrett's oesophagus and subsequent oesophageal adenocarcinoma. A patient who reports long-standing reflux that has recently become poorly responsive to their usual medication, now presenting with new dysphagia, must be investigated urgently for carcinoma.

**Ask:** *"Have you had heartburn or reflux in the past? Do you take any medication for it — is it helping as well as it used to? Do you get a sour or metallic taste in your mouth? Do you ever notice a sudden flood of saliva?"*

**Other GI Symptoms:**
- Nausea and vomiting — haematemesis is a critical red flag (see below)
- Abdominal bloating, altered bowel habit
- Malaena or rectal bleeding

### Step 6 — Red Flag Screening (Systematic and Specific)

The central purpose of this station is red flag identification. Every candidate must address red flags comprehensively and explicitly.

**General Oncological Red Flags:**
- Unexplained weight loss — ask specifically: how many kilograms, over what period, whether intentional
- Anorexia — loss of appetite or early satiety
- Fatigue and lethargy — systemic symptoms of malignancy
- Night sweats

**Head and Neck Cancer Specific Red Flags:**
- Persistent sore throat — particularly unilateral, lasting more than three weeks
- Otalgia (ear pain) — referred pain from an oropharyngeal or hypopharyngeal malignancy via the glossopharyngeal or vagus nerves; unilateral ear pain in a patient with dysphagia is a red flag for pharyngeal cancer
- Voice change — hoarseness or a change in voice quality may indicate laryngeal involvement by malignancy or recurrent laryngeal nerve involvement by thyroid or oesophageal cancer
- Neck lump — cervical lymphadenopathy from head and neck malignancy, or a thyroid mass causing extrinsic oesophageal compression

**Ask:** *"Have you noticed a lump in your neck? Have you had a sore throat that won't go away? Have you had any ear pain — particularly on one side? Has your voice changed at all?"*

**Oesophageal and Gastric Cancer Specific Red Flags:**
- Haematemesis — vomiting blood or coffee-ground material suggests ulceration of a malignant lesion
- Malaena — dark, tarry stools indicating upper GI haemorrhage

**Ask:** *"Have you ever vomited blood, or noticed blood in your vomit? Have you noticed your stools have changed — particularly any dark or black, tarry stools?"*

**Lung Cancer Red Flags (as a mediastinal mass causing extrinsic oesophageal compression):**
- Persistent cough — particularly a new, unexplained cough
- Haemoptysis — coughing up blood

**Ask:** *"Have you developed a new cough? Have you ever coughed up any blood?"*

---

### Step 7 — Neurological and Autoimmune Screening

Motility disorders represent a distinct diagnostic group requiring targeted questions.

**Neurological Causes of Dysphagia:**
All neurological causes of dysphagia produce oropharyngeal dysphagia with prominent difficulty initiating the swallow and may be accompanied by focal neurological deficits. Screen for:
- Limb weakness — upper or lower motor neurone pattern (stroke, motor neurone disease, myasthenia gravis, multiple sclerosis)
- Sensory disturbance — numbness, paraesthesiae (stroke, multiple sclerosis, diabetic autonomic neuropathy)
- Visual disturbance — diplopia or blurred vision (myasthenia gravis, multiple sclerosis)
- History of diabetes — which predisposes to autonomic neuropathy
- History of stroke or transient ischaemic attack
- Neurodegenerative history — Parkinson's disease (characterised by oropharyngeal dysphagia due to disruption of the complex motor programme for swallowing initiation)

**Ask:** *"Have you noticed any weakness in your arms or legs? Any numbness or tingling? Any problems with your vision? Have you been told you have diabetes? Have you ever had a stroke or any problems with your nervous system?"*

**Autoimmune and Connective Tissue Disease:**
Systemic sclerosis (scleroderma) causes oesophageal smooth muscle atrophy and fibrosis, producing severe oesophageal dysmotility with both solid and liquid dysphagia. Screen for the systemic features common to connective tissue diseases:
- Skin changes — tightening, thickening, or tethering of skin
- Raynaud's phenomenon — colour changes in the fingers with cold exposure
- Joint pains and swelling — inflammatory arthropathy
- Rashes — malar rash (SLE), heliotrope rash (dermatomyositis), photosensitive rashes

**Ask:** *"Have you noticed any joint pains or swelling? Any rashes? Have you noticed your fingers changing colour in the cold?"*

---

### Step 8 — Medications and Lifestyle Risk Factors

**Medications known to cause dysphagia:**
- ACE inhibitors — may cause angioedema of the oropharyngeal mucosa or a dry cough that disrupts swallowing
- Antipsychotics and sedatives — impair the neuromuscular coordination of swallowing
- Anticholinergics — cause xerostomia (dry mouth), making bolus formation and propulsion difficult
- Bisphosphonates (alendronate) — direct oesophageal mucosal injury causing oesophagitis and stricture formation
- NSAIDs — oesophageal ulceration
- SSRIs — associated with oesophageal dysmotility

**Ask:** *"Are you taking any regular medications? Have you recently started any new medications?"*

**GORD and Peptic Ulcer Disease Risk Factors:**
- Tobacco smoking — ask for current and past history (note: past smoking history is relevant to cancer risk even after cessation)
- Alcohol consumption — quantity, type, frequency
- Dietary habits — fatty, spicy foods; excessive coffee or tea consumption
- Obesity — body mass index is the strongest modifiable risk factor for GORD and oesophageal adenocarcinoma

**Past Medical History:**
- Prior diagnosis of GORD, Barrett's oesophagus, peptic ulcer disease
- Prior head and neck radiation — radiation strictures may occur years post-treatment
- Prior caustic ingestion
- Diabetes mellitus — autonomic neuropathy; also a risk factor for candidal oesophagitis in immunocompromised patients
- Stroke, Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis, myasthenia gravis

**Family History:**
- First-degree family history of upper gastrointestinal malignancy (gastric cancer, oesophageal cancer, colorectal cancer)

---

### Step 9 — ICE (Ideas, Concerns, Expectations)

**Ideas:** *"What do you think might be causing this difficulty with swallowing?"* Many patients with progressive dysphagia and weight loss privately fear cancer — they may or may not have verbalised this. Understanding their pre-existing illness model allows the clinician to calibrate their explanation appropriately.

**Concerns:** *"Is there anything in particular you're worried about?"* Acknowledge cancer as a legitimate concern. Do not minimise. If the clinical picture is consistent with malignancy, it is both honest and compassionate to acknowledge this possibility while explaining that investigation is needed to determine the diagnosis with certainty.

**Expectations:** *"What were you hoping we could do for you today?"* Most patients with dysphagia expect an explanation, a plan for investigation, and reassurance that their symptoms are being taken seriously.

## Red Flags — Must Not Miss

The following red flags in the context of dysphagia mandate urgent investigation and should not be dismissed on the basis of a normal physical examination. A normal physical examination does not exclude oesophageal malignancy.

- **Unintentional weight loss** — Systemic catabolism from malignancy. Quantify the weight loss (kilograms over a defined period) and confirm it is unintentional. Weight loss greater than 5% of body weight over three months is clinically significant. Mandates urgent upper GI endoscopy.

- **Progressive dysphagia from solids to liquids** — The hallmark of an obstructing lesion that is enlarging over time. The lumen progressively narrows until even liquids cannot pass. This trajectory is strongly associated with oesophageal carcinoma and requires urgent endoscopic assessment.

- **New onset dysphagia in a patient with longstanding inadequately controlled GORD** — Transition from symptomatic reflux to dysphagia may represent development of a malignant stricture on a background of Barrett's oesophagus. The fact that previously effective medication (e.g., a proton pump inhibitor) is now failing to control symptoms and new dysphagia has appeared is a critical clinical pivot point.

- **Haematemesis** — Vomiting blood or coffee-ground material in the context of dysphagia implies ulceration of an oesophageal or gastric lesion. This may represent haemorrhage from a malignant ulcer. Requires emergency assessment.

- **Malaena** — Dark tarry stools in the context of dysphagia indicate upper gastrointestinal haemorrhage from an oesophageal or gastric source.

- **Anorexia** — Loss of appetite or early satiety in conjunction with dysphagia suggests systemic malignancy.

- **Neck lump** — Cervical lymphadenopathy suggests regional spread of a head and neck or oesophageal malignancy. A thyroid mass may produce compressive symptoms. Any new neck lump in the context of dysphagia requires urgent assessment.

- **Persistent unilateral otalgia** — Referred pain from oropharyngeal or hypopharyngeal carcinoma via the glossopharyngeal or vagus nerve. A patient complaining of unilateral ear pain without evidence of otitis media must be investigated for malignancy of the pharynx or larynx.

- **Voice change (hoarseness or dysphonia)** — May indicate recurrent laryngeal nerve involvement by thyroid carcinoma, oesophageal carcinoma, or mediastinal lymphadenopathy from lung malignancy. Also seen with direct laryngeal involvement by hypopharyngeal carcinoma.

- **Persistent sore throat (more than three weeks)** — A long-standing unilateral sore throat that does not resolve with standard management is a red flag for oropharyngeal malignancy. This symptom is frequently missed in the examination context because candidates focus exclusively on the oesophagus.

- **Haemoptysis** — Coughing up blood in conjunction with dysphagia should raise concern for lung malignancy causing mediastinal compression of the oesophagus, or for tracheo-oesophageal fistula complicating oesophageal carcinoma.

- **Smoking and alcohol history** — Together, these constitute the dominant risk factor combination for squamous cell carcinoma of the oesophagus and oropharynx. While not a symptom, the presence of significant tobacco and alcohol exposure in a patient with dysphagia elevates the malignancy risk substantially and should influence the urgency of investigation.

## Investigations

**Bedside:**
- Vital signs including BMI calculation
- Oxygen saturation (aspiration risk assessment)
- Urinalysis

**Bloods:**
- Full blood count — anaemia (chronic disease, haemorrhage, malnutrition)
- Erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein — inflammatory or neoplastic process
- Liver function tests and albumin — nutritional status, hepatic metastases
- Urea and electrolytes — dehydration if oral intake severely reduced
- Blood glucose — diabetes (autonomic neuropathy as cause of dysphagia)
- Thyroid function tests — thyroid mass assessment
- ANA, anti-Scl-70, anti-centromere antibodies — if systemic sclerosis or connective tissue disease suspected
- Anti-acetylcholine receptor antibodies — if myasthenia gravis suspected

**Imaging:**
- Chest X-ray — mediastinal widening, pulmonary masses, aspiration pneumonia, hilar lymphadenopathy
- CT neck, chest, abdomen and pelvis with contrast — primary staging investigation for suspected oesophageal, thyroid, or lung malignancy; assessment of mediastinal structures and lymph node involvement
- Barium swallow — useful for identifying pharyngeal diverticulum, high oesophageal strictures, and achalasia (rat-tail or bird-beak appearance); second-line to endoscopy but valuable when endoscopy is contraindicated or for functional assessment

**Specialist Investigations:**
- Upper GI endoscopy (oesophagogastroduodenoscopy, OGD) — the definitive first-line investigation for oesophageal dysphagia. Provides direct visualisation of the oesophageal mucosa, allows biopsy of any suspicious lesion, identifies Barrett's oesophagus, and can detect strictures (including peptic, malignant, and radiation-induced). **A normal physical examination does not replace endoscopy in the investigation of progressive dysphagia — endoscopy remains mandatory.**
- Oesophageal manometry — primary investigation for suspected achalasia or diffuse oesophageal spasm when endoscopy is unremarkable
- 24-hour oesophageal pH monitoring — objective confirmation of GORD and correlation with symptoms
- Modified barium swallow with videofluoroscopy — gold standard for detailed assessment of oropharyngeal swallowing function; performed with a speech pathologist
- Nasendoscopy / flexible laryngoscopy — direct visualisation of the oropharynx, hypopharynx, and larynx for suspected malignancy or vocal cord pathology; performed by ENT
- ENT or gastroenterology referral — as clinically indicated

## Management

**Immediate Priorities:**
Assessment of nutritional status and hydration is the first clinical priority in any patient with established dysphagia. A patient who has been unable to swallow adequately for weeks may be significantly malnourished and dehydrated. Dietitian referral and consideration of supplemental nutritional support (oral supplements initially, nasogastric or parenteral nutrition if severe) may be required while the diagnostic workup proceeds.

**If Malignancy is Suspected (Red Flags Present):**
- Urgent referral to gastroenterology for expedited upper GI endoscopy — in Australia, a two-week urgent referral pathway exists for suspected upper GI malignancy
- Do not delay investigation to trial empirical therapy
- Ensure the patient understands the clinical concern and the purpose of investigation
- MDT involvement will be required if malignancy is confirmed: gastroenterology, upper GI surgery, oncology, radiation oncology, speech pathology, dietetics, palliative care

**If Oesophageal Stricture (Peptic) is the Working Diagnosis:**
- Optimise acid suppression: high-dose proton pump inhibitor (e.g., omeprazole 40 mg twice daily or pantoprazole 40 mg twice daily)
- Endoscopy to confirm the stricture and exclude malignancy — endoscopic dilatation (balloon or Savary-Gilliard bougienage) for symptomatic strictures
- Lifestyle modification addressing GORD risk factors: weight loss, smoking cessation, alcohol reduction, elevation of the head of the bed, avoidance of recumbency within three hours of eating, dietary modification (reducing fatty, spicy, and acidic foods)
- Long-term maintenance PPI therapy and surveillance endoscopy as per Barrett's oesophagus guidelines if applicable

**If Achalasia is Diagnosed:**
- Confirmed by oesophageal manometry (absent peristalsis, elevated lower oesophageal sphincter pressure, incomplete LOS relaxation)
- Treatment options: pneumatic dilatation, per-oral endoscopic myotomy (POEM), laparoscopic Heller myotomy — discussed and planned in consultation with gastroenterology and upper GI surgery
- Botulinum toxin injection to the LOS is an option for patients unfit for definitive intervention

**If Neurological or Motility Disorder is Suspected:**
- Formal swallowing assessment by a speech pathologist — modified barium swallow or videofluoroscopy
- Neurological referral for further workup of the underlying condition
- Aspiration precautions — dietary modification under speech pathology guidance (thickened fluids, modified textures)
- Management of the underlying condition (immunotherapy for myasthenia gravis, disease-modifying therapy for multiple sclerosis, pharmacotherapy for Parkinson's disease)

**Infectious Causes:**
- Peritonsillar abscess: ENT assessment, aspiration or drainage, intravenous antibiotics
- Epiglottitis: emergency management, airway security, intravenous antibiotics

## Communication and Patient Education

**Explaining the Working Diagnosis:**
When red flags are present, it is essential to communicate the clinical concern honestly and compassionately. The explanation should be direct without being alarming. An appropriate formulation is: *"Based on what you've told me today — particularly the weight you've lost and the fact that the difficulty swallowing is happening only with solid foods and has been getting gradually worse — I am concerned about the possibility of a cancer in the food pipe. I want to be clear that at this stage we don't know for certain — the only way we can know is with a test called an endoscopy where we look inside the food pipe with a camera. But given your symptoms, I think it is important we arrange this urgently."*

**Explaining an Oesophageal Stricture:**
*"The most likely cause of your symptoms is what we call a stricture — a narrowing in the food pipe. This happens because of long-term acid reflux that hasn't been fully controlled. The ongoing acid irritation causes inflammation, and over time that leads to scarring which narrows the food pipe and makes it harder for solid food to pass through. The important thing is that before we treat this, we need to look inside the food pipe with a camera to make absolutely sure there is no other cause — including ruling out any possibility of cancer. If it is confirmed to be a scar-related narrowing, we can treat it by gently stretching the food pipe during the endoscopy procedure."*

**Addressing the Concern About Cancer:**
Do not dismiss a patient's fear of cancer, and do not offer false reassurance. The appropriate response is to acknowledge the concern, explain that this is a reasonable and understandable worry, and confirm that the investigation plan is specifically designed to address it. *"It's completely understandable to be worried about cancer — that is one of the things we need to investigate. The endoscopy will give us a clear answer."*

**Explaining the Endoscopy Procedure:**
*"An endoscopy is a test where a thin, flexible tube with a small camera on the end is passed through the mouth and down into the food pipe and stomach. You will be given some sedation so you are comfortable. The test allows the doctor to look directly at the lining of the food pipe and take small tissue samples if needed. It usually takes about 15 to 20 minutes."*

**Questions to Anticipate:**
- Is it cancer? (Acknowledge the concern; explain only investigation will confirm the diagnosis)
- How serious is this? (Validate the concern; emphasise the importance of urgent investigation without speculation about prognosis)
- What happens if it is cancer? (Briefly outline that a specialist team would discuss all treatment options; avoid premature prognosis discussion)
- Can I keep eating normally? (Dietary advice: soft or semi-solid foods in the interim; ensure adequate nutrition and hydration; urgent dietitian review if weight loss is significant)

**Written Information:**
Provide written material on upper GI endoscopy (available from hospital endoscopy units and gastroenterology departments). Provide written safety net instructions specifying symptoms requiring emergency presentation.

## AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

- **Always distinguish dysphagia from odynophagia at the outset.** This is the first and most important characterisation question. Candidates who proceed directly to investigating dysphagia without defining the symptom miss the diagnostic distinction between painful and painless difficulty swallowing.

- **The solid/liquid question is the most discriminating single question in this station.** Dysphagia restricted to solid foods points to structural obstruction (malignancy or stricture); dysphagia to both solids and liquids from the outset points to motility dysfunction (achalasia, neuromuscular disease). Do not omit this question under any circumstances.

- **A normal physical examination does not exclude oesophageal carcinoma.** In the AMC Clinical Examination, a version of this case has been presented where physical examination findings are entirely normal. Candidates who downgrade their diagnosis based on a normal examination and abandon malignancy as the working diagnosis are making a clinically dangerous error. Red flags in the history mandate endoscopy regardless of examination findings.

- **Do not miss the oropharyngeal cancer questions.** Candidates consistently focus on oesophageal pathology and forget to ask about ear pain (otalgia), persistent sore throat, and voice change — symptoms that point specifically to oropharyngeal and hypopharyngeal carcinoma. These questions must be asked in every dysphagia history.

- **Chronic GORD plus new progressive dysphagia is a malignancy red flag combination.** A patient who has managed their reflux with proton pump inhibitors for years and now reports new progressive dysphagia and that their medication is "no longer working" must be investigated urgently for Barrett's oesophagus progression to oesophageal adenocarcinoma.

- **Structure your differential as groups, not isolated diagnoses.** In your closing summary, organising your differential into logical groups — malignancies, structural/obstructive causes, motility disorders, infections, medications — demonstrates systematic clinical reasoning and is more efficient in the time available than listing individual diagnoses sequentially.

- **Include lung cancer as a differential for dysphagia.** Mediastinal masses — including lung cancer and its associated lymphadenopathy — can compress the oesophagus externally and produce dysphagia. This is not an obscure association; it is a well-established mechanism. A cough and haemoptysis screen belongs in this history.

- **Previous smoking history (even remote) is clinically relevant.** Do not dismiss a history of smoking that ceased years prior. Tobacco exposure increases the lifetime risk of oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma, lung cancer, and head and neck cancers. A 10 pack-year history, even if the patient stopped smoking a decade ago, is a relevant risk factor that should be documented and incorporated into your risk assessment.

- **Achalasia presents with dysphagia to both solids and liquids.** In a younger patient with no red flags and dysphagia to all consistencies from the outset, accompanied by regurgitation of undigested food (often hours after eating and particularly at night), achalasia must be considered. The investigation of choice is oesophageal manometry, not endoscopy alone (endoscopy may appear normal or show only a dilated oesophagus).

- **Ask about medications proactively.** Bisphosphonates (commonly prescribed to postmenopausal women for osteoporosis — an important demographic overlap with this station), NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, and anticholinergics are all associated with dysphagia through distinct mechanisms. A 57-year-old woman is precisely the patient who may be on multiple medications that could contribute to or cause her symptoms.

- **In your closing, identify the specific red flags you found and explain their clinical significance.** Do not simply say "you have some concerning features." Be specific: "The weight loss of six kilograms, the fact that the problem is only with solid foods and is getting gradually worse, and your history of long-standing reflux that is no longer controlled by medication — these are the specific findings that make me concerned about a cancer in the food pipe."

- **The endoscopy is the definitive investigation — name it explicitly.** The investigation that will confirm or exclude oesophageal malignancy and stricture is upper GI endoscopy. Candidates must name this investigation explicitly, explain what it involves in simple language, and communicate the urgency of its arrangement.

## Safety Net

**Return to the Emergency Department immediately if:**
- Inability to swallow any food or liquids — total dysphagia with risk of dehydration and aspiration
- Vomiting blood (haematemesis) or passing dark black tarry stools (malaena)
- Sudden severe chest pain (possible oesophageal perforation or spasm)
- Choking episode with difficulty breathing or signs of aspiration
- Sudden onset of severe neck swelling or difficulty breathing (airway compromise from pharyngeal abscess or rapidly enlarging thyroid pathology)
- Rapid deterioration in overall condition — severe weakness, loss of consciousness, inability to manage secretions

**Return to the general practitioner within 48 to 72 hours if:**
- Symptoms are worsening despite dietary modification
- Unable to maintain adequate oral hydration
- New symptoms develop — voice change, neck lump, ear pain, haemoptysis

**Routine follow-up:**
- Endoscopy appointment confirmed within two weeks (urgent referral pathway for suspected malignancy)
- Dietitian review arranged if weight loss or nutritional compromise is present
- Results review appointment scheduled — do not wait for the patient to contact the practice

**Interim dietary advice:**
While awaiting investigation, advise the patient to adopt a soft or semi-solid diet (well-cooked foods, minced or mashed textures), to take small bites, to eat slowly, to remain upright for at least one hour after meals, and to avoid food and drink that exacerbates reflux symptoms (fatty foods, alcohol, caffeine, acidic foods). Advise adequate hydration with water or non-acidic fluids taken throughout the day.

**Written resources:**
Provide written information about the planned endoscopy procedure, the practice's after-hours contact details, and clear written instructions specifying symptoms that require emergency presentation. Ensure the patient has a trusted contact person who can accompany them to the endoscopy procedure given the use of sedation.

## Red Flags — Must Not Miss

- **Unintentional weight loss**
- **Progressive dysphagia from solids to liquids**
- **New onset dysphagia in a patient with longstanding inadequately controlled GORD**
- **Haematemesis**
- **Malaena**
- **Anorexia**
- **Neck lump**
- **Persistent unilateral otalgia**
- **Voice change (hoarseness or dysphonia)**
- **Persistent sore throat (more than three weeks)**

## AMC Exam Key Points

- Always distinguish dysphagia from odynophagia at the outset. This is the first and most important characterisation question. Candidates who proceed directly to investigating dysphagia without defining the symptom miss the diagnostic distinction between painful and painless difficulty swallowing.
- The solid/liquid question is the most discriminating single question in this station. Dysphagia restricted to solid foods points to structural obstruction (malignancy or stricture); dysphagia to both solids and liquids from the outset points to motility dysfunction (achalasia, neuromuscular disease). Do not omit this question under any circumstances.
- A normal physical examination does not exclude oesophageal carcinoma. In the AMC Clinical Examination, a version of this case has been presented where physical examination findings are entirely normal. Candidates who downgrade their diagnosis based on a normal examination and abandon malignancy as the working diagnosis are making a clinically dangerous error. Red flags in the history mandate endoscopy regardless of examination findings.
- Do not miss the oropharyngeal cancer questions. Candidates consistently focus on oesophageal pathology and forget to ask about ear pain (otalgia), persistent sore throat, and voice change
- Chronic GORD plus new progressive dysphagia is a malignancy red flag combination. A patient who has managed their reflux with proton pump inhibitors for years and now reports new progressive dysphagia and that their medication is "no longer working" must be investigated urgently for Barrett's oesophagus progression to oesophageal adenocarcinoma.
- Structure your differential as groups, not isolated diagnoses. In your closing summary, organising your differential into logical groups
- Include lung cancer as a differential for dysphagia. Mediastinal masses
- Previous smoking history (even remote) is clinically relevant. Do not dismiss a history of smoking that ceased years prior. Tobacco exposure increases the lifetime risk of oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma, lung cancer, and head and neck cancers. A 10 pack-year history, even if the patient stopped smoking a decade ago, is a relevant risk factor that should be documented and incorporated into your risk assessment.
- Achalasia presents with dysphagia to both solids and liquids. In a younger patient with no red flags and dysphagia to all consistencies from the outset, accompanied by regurgitation of undigested food (often hours after eating and particularly at night), achalasia must be considered. The investigation of choice is oesophageal manometry, not endoscopy alone (endoscopy may appear normal or show only a dilated oesophagus).
- Ask about medications proactively. Bisphosphonates (commonly prescribed to postmenopausal women for osteoporosis

## Management Summary

1. Immediate Priorities:**
2. If Malignancy is Suspected (Red Flags Present):**
3. Urgent referral to gastroenterology for expedited upper GI endoscopy — in Australia, a two-week urgent referral pathway exists for suspected upper GI malignancy
4. Do not delay investigation to trial empirical therapy
5. Ensure the patient understands the clinical concern and the purpose of investigation
6. MDT involvement will be required if malignancy is confirmed: gastroenterology, upper GI surgery, oncology, radiation oncology, speech pathology, dietetics, palliative care
7. If Oesophageal Stricture (Peptic) is the Working Diagnosis:**
8. Optimise acid suppression: high-dose proton pump inhibitor (e.g., omeprazole 40 mg twice daily or pantoprazole 40 mg twice daily)
