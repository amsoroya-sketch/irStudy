# Left Lower Quadrant Abdominal Pain — History Taking Station

## Clinical Presentation

A 50-year-old man presents to general practice with a four-week history of left lower quadrant (LLQ) abdominal pain. The pain is persistent, dull in character, rated 5/10 in severity, and has been gradually worsening. There is no acute haemodynamic compromise. Associated features include a long-standing history of constipation with hard, difficult-to-pass stools. There are no urinary symptoms, no scrotal swelling or redness, and no groin lumps. In the primary scenario, systemic red flags are absent. A variant presentation introduces unintentional weight loss of five kilograms over two months and fresh blood in the stool, which fundamentally redirects the working diagnosis toward colorectal malignancy.

This station tests the candidate's ability to conduct a structured, time-efficient history in a subacute to chronic abdominal pain presentation — a clinically distinct context from the acute abdomen. The predominant assessment domain is history taking, and candidates must demonstrate systematic clinical reasoning by exploring multiple differential groups, eliciting and appropriately pursuing positive findings, and screening rigorously for red flags before committing to a provisional diagnosis.

The clinical importance of this station is considerable. Left lower quadrant pain in a 50-year-old man encompasses a spectrum from benign conditions such as diverticular disease and constipation, to life-threatening pathology including colorectal carcinoma, bowel obstruction, and mesenteric ischaemia. The ability to distinguish these through structured history taking, using both pattern recognition and red flag exclusion, is a core competency evaluated by the AMC Clinical Examination.

## Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

The AMC Clinical Examination history taking station rewards candidates who demonstrate clear clinical reasoning through organised, purposeful questioning. The examiner is assessing not only whether the correct diagnosis is reached, but whether the candidate follows a logical, reproducible structure that a safe practitioner would use in clinical practice.

**Opening Phase:** Introduce yourself by name and role, confirm the patient's identity, and establish rapport with a genuine open-ended question. Allow the patient to complete their opening statement without interruption. Respond to the patient's emotional cues with brief empathic acknowledgement before transitioning to systematic enquiry.

**Pain Characterisation (SOCRATES):** In a chronic pain presentation, SOCRATES requires two additional dimensions beyond the standard acute assessment: enquiry about nocturnal pain (pain that wakes the patient from sleep is a red flag across all body systems) and the impact of pain on daily functioning and quality of life.

**Systematic Differential Screening:** Structure the history around anatomical groups of differentials — gastrointestinal, renal and urological (KUB), and genital/pelvic — followed by additional differentials as time permits. Within the gastrointestinal group, red flag questions for colorectal malignancy must be addressed explicitly.

**ICE Elicitation:** The patient's ideas, concerns, and expectations should be acknowledged during the opening and revisited at closure. In a case involving chronic pain in a 50-year-old, concern about cancer is common and must be addressed sensitively.

**Closure and Diagnosis Delivery:** Summarise the history, deliver the provisional diagnosis with clinical reasoning, present a structured differential list spanning multiple anatomical groups, and provide a clear management and safety-net plan.

## Systematic Approach

### Opening and Establishing Rapport

Sanitise hands on entering the station. Introduce yourself by name and role. Confirm the patient's preferred name. Use a genuine open-ended opening question:

*"Gary, I understand you've been having some pain. Can you tell me what's been going on?"*

Allow the patient to complete their opening statement without interruption. The patient will typically volunteer the duration, location, and an expression of concern. Respond with brief empathic acknowledgement before proceeding:

*"I do understand this has been stressful for you, Gary, and I want to make sure we get to the bottom of it. I'm going to ask you some questions to better understand your pain, and then we can work out the best way forward together. Does that sound all right?"*

Assess for haemodynamic compromise before proceeding in any acute presentation. In a four-week history, this step remains reasonable but is less critical than in acute abdominal pain.

### SOCRATES Pain Assessment — Adapted for Chronic Presentation

**Site:**
Ask the patient to point to where they feel the pain. In this case: left lower quadrant / left iliac fossa.

**Onset:**
- When did the pain begin? (Four weeks ago in this scenario.)
- Was the onset sudden or gradual?
- Is this the first episode, or have there been similar episodes previously? First episode suggests new pathology; recurrent episodes may indicate a chronic structural condition such as diverticular disease or inflammatory bowel disease.

**Character:**
Offer descriptive prompts without leading: *"Would you describe it as a sharp, stabbing pain, or more of a dull ache?"* The dull aching quality is characteristic of diverticulitis and colonic pathology; colicky pain suggests obstructive or renal aetiology.

**Radiation:**
Does the pain travel to any other area? LLQ pain radiating to the groin suggests ureteric colic; radiation to the back may indicate a vascular or retroperitoneal cause.

**Associated Symptoms — Systematic Gastrointestinal Screen:**
The following six questions form the core gastrointestinal cluster. Each positive finding requires focused follow-up:
- *"Have you had any nausea or vomiting?"*
- *"Have you noticed any bloating or abdominal distension?"*
- *"Are you passing wind normally?"* — Absence of flatus is a key feature of bowel obstruction and must prompt the follow-up question: *"When did you last open your bowels?"*
- *"Have you had any diarrhoea?"*
- *"Have you had any constipation?"* — If positive: *"How long have you had trouble with your bowels? Can you describe what it's like — are your stools hard to pass? Are they hard in consistency?"*
- *"Have you had any fever or chills?"*

**Timing — Critical Addition for Chronic Pain:**
- Duration and progression: Is the pain constant or intermittent? Is it the same, improving, or worsening?
- **Nocturnal pain:** *"Has this pain ever woken you from sleep?"* Pain that wakes a patient from sleep is a red flag across all systems — it suggests inflammatory, infective, or malignant pathology and must never be omitted in a chronic pain history.

**Exacerbating and Relieving Factors:**
- Does anything make the pain better or worse?
- Is the pain related to eating?
- Does movement or position affect the pain?
- In diverticulitis, pain characteristically worsens with walking and positional change, though this is not always elicited in the history.

**Severity:**
Use a validated numerical scale: *"On a scale of zero to ten, where zero is no pain at all and ten is the worst pain you have ever experienced, how would you rate your pain?"* Explain the scale before asking. A severity of 5/10 is consistent with subacute diverticulitis.

**Additional Domain — Impact on Daily Life:**
In any chronic pain presentation, enquire about functional impact: *"How has this pain affected your day-to-day life — your work, your sleep, your ability to do the things you enjoy?"* This demonstrates patient-centred practice and is specifically relevant to AMC Clinical Examination assessment criteria.

### Red Flag Screening — Colorectal Malignancy

In a 50-year-old with a four-week history of LLQ pain and constipation, colorectal cancer must be actively excluded before a benign diagnosis is accepted. The following questions are mandatory and must be asked explicitly:

1. **Rectal bleeding:** *"Have you noticed any blood in your stool? Is it fresh red blood, or have your stools appeared dark or black?"*
   - Fresh blood suggests a colorectal source; malaena indicates proximal bleeding.

2. **Unintentional weight loss:** *"Have you lost any weight recently without trying to? If so, how much, and over what period of time?"*
   - Clinically significant unintentional weight loss is five percent or more of body weight over six to twelve months. Five kilograms in two months is highly significant.

3. **Appetite:** *"Has your appetite changed at all — have you been eating less than usual?"*

4. **Change in bowel habit:** *"Have you noticed any change in your usual bowel pattern — for example, alternating diarrhoea and constipation, or stools that have become narrower?"*

5. **Lumps and bumps:** *"Have you noticed any new lumps or swellings anywhere on your body?"*

6. **Fatigue and anaemia:** *"Are you feeling unusually tired or fatigued? Have you felt dizzy or short of breath?"* — Iron deficiency anaemia from occult colonic blood loss is a common presentation of right-sided colorectal cancer and may coexist with left-sided pathology.

7. **Bowel cancer screening:** *"Have you received a bowel cancer screening kit in the post? Have you completed the test?"*
   - All Australians aged 50 to 74 years are eligible for the National Bowel Cancer Screening Programme (NBCSP), which sends faecal occult blood test (FOBT) kits biannually. A positive FOBT result mandates colonoscopy referral. Enquiring about screening is both clinically relevant and demonstrates knowledge of Australian preventive health policy.

8. **Family history of colorectal cancer:** *"Does anyone in your family — parents, siblings, children — have a history of bowel cancer or bowel polyps?"*

### KUB (Kidney, Ureter, Bladder) Differential Screening

Urological pathology accounts for a significant proportion of LLQ pain, particularly ureteric colic and urinary tract infection. The following three questions efficiently screen this group:

1. *"Have you noticed any pain, burning, or stinging when you pass urine?"*
2. *"Are you passing urine more frequently than usual?"*
3. *"Have you noticed any blood in your urine?"* — Haematuria in the context of LLQ pain and flank radiation is highly suggestive of ureteric colic.

---

### Genital and Pelvic Differential Screening

In a male patient with LLQ pain, the following must be assessed:

**Inguinal hernia:** *"Have you noticed any swelling or lump in your groin, particularly when you cough or strain?"* A cough impulse in the groin is pathognomonic. An irreducible hernia with pain suggests incarceration and requires urgent assessment.

**Epididymo-orchitis:** *"Have you noticed any swelling, redness, or tenderness in your scrotum or testicles? Have you had any pain in your private parts?"*
- Epididymo-orchitis causes scrotal tenderness and swelling, and referred pain may present in the lower abdomen. In a sexually active man, Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae are important causes; in older men, enteric organisms predominate.

---

### Additional Differentials — Time Permitting

If time remains after the core clusters are completed:

- **Shingles (herpes zoster):** *"Have you noticed any rash or blistering on the skin of your abdomen or flank, particularly on one side only?"* Prodromal zoster causes dermatomal pain before the rash appears; the LLQ distribution corresponds to lower thoracic or upper lumbar dermatomes.

- **Mesenteric ischaemia:** *"Have you ever noticed your heart racing or had an irregular heartbeat?"* Atrial fibrillation is a risk factor for mesenteric arterial embolism. Mesenteric ischaemia typically causes severe, diffuse pain disproportionate to examination findings — the clinical triad of severe pain, minimal tenderness on palpation, and a known cardiac arrhythmia should prompt urgent investigation.

- **Abdominal trauma:** *"Have you had any recent injuries or trauma to your abdomen?"*

### Social, Medication, and Background History (SMASH-FM)

Complete the history efficiently with:

- **Smoking status:** Current, ex-smoker, never — pack-year history if relevant.
- **Alcohol:** Standard drinks per week.
- **Illicit substances:** As clinically relevant.
- **Medications:** Including NSAIDs (associated with intestinal perforation and bleeding), anticoagulants (alter presentation of bleeding), and opioids (constipation).
- **Allergies:** Particularly to antibiotics, given that diverticulitis management involves amoxicillin-clavulanate.
- **Past medical and surgical history:** Previous abdominal surgeries are critical — adhesional small bowel obstruction is a leading diagnosis in patients with prior abdominal operations, particularly appendicectomy or bowel resection.
- **Family history:** Colorectal cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, Lynch syndrome.

## Red Flags — Must Not Miss

- **Unintentional weight loss** — Weight loss of five kilograms or more over two months, without dietary change or increased exercise, is highly concerning for underlying malignancy or chronic inflammatory disease. Requires urgent investigation including colonoscopy and CT imaging. Do not accept a diverticulitis diagnosis without explicitly excluding this.

- **Rectal bleeding** — Fresh blood per rectum in a 50-year-old must raise the suspicion of colorectal carcinoma until proven otherwise. Dark or tarry stools (malaena) suggest a more proximal bleeding source. Either finding mandates colonoscopic evaluation.

- **Nocturnal pain** — Pain that wakes the patient from sleep is a red flag in any body system. In the context of abdominal pain, it suggests an organic inflammatory, infective, or malignant cause rather than a functional disorder.

- **Change in bowel habit** — Any new or progressive change in bowel habit in a patient over 45 years of age — particularly narrowing of stool calibre, new-onset constipation, or alternating constipation and diarrhoea — requires colorectal cancer exclusion.

- **Iron deficiency anaemia** — Fatigue, pallor, and dizziness in the context of chronic LLQ pain may represent occult gastrointestinal blood loss from a colonic malignancy or angiodysplasia. Full blood count and iron studies are mandatory.

- **Positive FOBT result** — A positive national bowel screening result mandates urgent colonoscopy referral regardless of symptom severity.

- **Absolute constipation with absent flatus** — Inability to pass stool or flatus in the context of colicky pain, abdominal distension, and prior abdominal surgery is consistent with bowel obstruction until proven otherwise. This requires urgent imaging and surgical assessment.

- **Absent bowel sounds with abdominal pain** — On examination, absent bowel sounds combined with abdominal pain suggest ileus or complete obstruction. Combined with prior surgical history, adhesional small bowel obstruction becomes the primary concern.

- **Fever with peritonism** — Localised or generalised peritonism in the context of fever and LLQ pain raises concern for complicated diverticulitis with perforation, abscess formation, or peritonitis. Urgent surgical assessment and IV antibiotics are required.

- **Haemodynamic instability** — Tachycardia, hypotension, and diaphoresis in a patient with abdominal pain indicate a surgical emergency. Immediate resuscitation and urgent surgical review are required.

## Investigations

**Bedside:**
- Vital signs: blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, temperature, oxygen saturation
- Urinalysis: haematuria (ureteric colic, cystitis, bladder malignancy), nitrites and leucocytes (UTI)
- Faecal occult blood test (FOBT) — if not already completed as part of NBCSP

**Bloods:**
- Full blood count (FBC): leucocytosis in infection/inflammation; anaemia suggests bleeding or malignancy
- C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR): elevated in diverticulitis, IBD, malignancy
- Urea, electrolytes, and creatinine (UEC): renal function assessment, particularly if CT contrast is planned
- Liver function tests (LFTs): assessment of hepatic metastasis if malignancy is suspected
- Coagulation studies if anticoagulated or bleeding is present
- Beta-hCG: not applicable in this male patient

**Imaging:**
- **CT abdomen and pelvis with IV contrast** — the single most important investigation for confirming diverticulitis, excluding perforation and abscess, and evaluating for alternative diagnoses including malignancy and obstruction. This is the preferred investigation for diverticulitis confirmation in Australian emergency and general practice settings.
- Erect chest X-ray: free air under the diaphragm suggests perforation
- Abdominal X-ray: dilated bowel loops in obstruction; limited utility but may be the first imaging obtained in acute settings
- Renal tract ultrasound: useful for evaluating hydronephrosis and ureteric calculi, particularly if CT is deferred
- CT colonography or barium enema: second-line investigation for colorectal malignancy if colonoscopy is not immediately available or feasible

**Specialist:**
- **Colonoscopy** — definitive investigation for colorectal cancer exclusion; indicated in any patient with red flags, positive FOBT, or after resolution of acute complicated diverticulitis (typically six to eight weeks post-resolution)
- Surgical review: for complicated diverticulitis, suspected bowel obstruction, or incarcerated hernia
- Urology review: for confirmed or suspected ureteric calculus or renal malignancy

## Management

### Uncomplicated Acute Diverticulitis — General Practice Management

Uncomplicated diverticulitis is defined by the absence of perforation, abscess, peritonitis, fistula, sepsis, and haemodynamic instability. In this population, outpatient management is appropriate for immunocompetent patients with mild symptoms and reliable follow-up.

**Analgesia:**
Simple analgesics are the first-line approach. Paracetamol 1 gram orally four times daily is appropriate and well-tolerated. Avoid NSAIDs, which may increase the risk of intestinal perforation and bleeding in the context of colonic inflammation.

**Antibiotic Therapy:**
Current evidence and Australian Therapeutic Guidelines support antibiotic therapy for acute diverticulitis. First-line outpatient treatment is amoxicillin-clavulanate (Augmentin Duo Forte) 875/125 mg orally twice daily for five to ten days. This provides adequate coverage for the enteric gram-negative and anaerobic organisms implicated in diverticulitis. In penicillin-allergic patients, alternative regimens include trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole with metronidazole.

**Dietary Modification:**
During the acute phase, a clear fluid to soft low-fibre diet is recommended to reduce intraluminal pressure and bowel workload. Adequate oral hydration is essential. Counterintuitively, a high-fibre diet is not recommended during the acute inflammatory phase, as it may exacerbate symptoms. High-fibre dietary modification is appropriate in the recovery phase to prevent recurrence.

**Indications for Hospital Admission and IV Antibiotics:**
- Failure to improve after 72 hours of outpatient antibiotic therapy
- Immunocompromised state (including diabetes, corticosteroid use, immunosuppressive therapy)
- Inability to tolerate oral intake
- Complicated diverticulitis: perforation, abscess, peritonitis, fistula, or obstruction
- Haemodynamic instability or sepsis

In hospital, intravenous antibiotics covering gram-negative enteric organisms and anaerobes (e.g., piperacillin-tazobactam or metronidazole combined with a third-generation cephalosporin) are used. Surgical consultation is required for complicated disease.

**Long-term Prevention:**
Following resolution, patients should be advised regarding long-term high-fibre diet and adequate hydration to prevent recurrence. Colonoscopy is recommended six to eight weeks after resolution in all patients with complicated diverticulitis, and should be considered in any patient in whom colorectal cancer has not been confidently excluded.

### Colorectal Cancer — Initial Management Steps

If the history reveals unambiguous red flags (unintentional weight loss, rectal bleeding, positive FOBT), the immediate management priorities are:
- Urgent colonoscopy referral under the two-week urgent gastrointestinal referral pathway
- CT chest, abdomen, and pelvis for staging if cancer is confirmed
- Multidisciplinary team (MDT) review
- Haematology review if significant anaemia is identified

### Bowel Obstruction — Emergency Management

If adhesional small bowel obstruction is suspected (prior abdominal surgery, absent flatus for several days, absent bowel sounds, empty rectum on digital rectal examination):
- Urgent emergency department referral
- IV access, IV fluids, nasogastric tube decompression
- CT abdomen and pelvis to confirm diagnosis and assess for vascular compromise
- Urgent surgical review; most adhesional obstruction resolves with conservative management, but vascular compromise requires emergency laparotomy

## Communication and Patient Education

### Explaining Diverticulitis

The diagnosis of diverticulitis should be explained in clear, jargon-free language accessible to a lay person:

*"Gary, based on what you've told me today, I believe you have a condition called diverticulitis. Let me explain what that means. Over time, small pouches or pockets can form in the wall of the large bowel — we call these diverticula. This is often related to having hard stools or difficulty with your bowel motions over a long period of time, which puts pressure on the bowel wall and causes these pockets to form. What has happened in your case is that one of these pouches has become inflamed — that inflammation is what we call diverticulitis, and it is causing the pain you are feeling on the left side of your abdomen."*

**Reasons supporting the diagnosis** should be communicated explicitly to the patient as part of clinical transparency and to demonstrate clinical reasoning:
1. The location of pain — left lower quadrant is the characteristic site for diverticulitis, as the sigmoid colon is situated in this region
2. The long-standing history of constipation — this is the primary risk factor for diverticulum formation
3. The absence of red flags — no bleeding, no weight loss, no nocturnal pain — which makes a sinister cause less likely at this stage

### Explaining Bowel Cancer as a Differential (Sensitive Disclosure)

When bowel cancer is the leading provisional diagnosis, sensitivity is essential. The following approach is recommended:

*"Gary, during our conversation I have been thinking carefully about a number of possible explanations for your symptoms. I want to be honest with you because I think you deserve that. I am concerned about the possibility of a bowel cancer. I say this because you have been losing weight without trying to — five kilograms in two months is significant — and you have also noticed blood in your stool. Together, these are symptoms that I take seriously. I want to reassure you that we are going to investigate this thoroughly and promptly."*

Avoid euphemisms such as "nasty growth" or "abnormal growth" — these terms confuse patients and may generate anxiety without providing clarity. If the patient understands the word cancer, use it clearly and compassionately.

### Addressing Patient Concerns (ICE)

A 50-year-old man presenting after four weeks of unresolved pain is likely to have concerns about serious pathology. Directly elicit and address these:
- *"What has been going through your mind about what might be causing this pain?"*
- *"Is there anything specific you have been worried about?"*
- *"What were you hoping we might do today to help?"*

These questions demonstrate patient-centred practice and frequently reveal clinically important information about the patient's health literacy, level of concern, and previous experiences with illness.

## AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

- **The dominant assessment area determines your focus.** When history taking is the primary domain, examiner scoring is weighted heavily on the quality and organisation of your history — not just on arriving at the correct diagnosis. A candidate who reaches the correct diagnosis through an unstructured, disorganised history will score lower than a candidate who works systematically through a well-reasoned differential list, even if they identify the provisional diagnosis slightly later.

- **Never skip red flags in a chronic pain case.** The most common reason for failing a diverticulitis or LLQ pain station is making the diagnosis of diverticulitis or constipation without explicitly screening for colorectal cancer red flags. Examiner prompts notwithstanding, a candidate who names diverticulitis without asking about weight loss and rectal bleeding has demonstrated unsafe clinical reasoning.

- **Probe all positive findings.** When a patient reports a positive symptom — constipation, weight loss, change in bowel habit — do not proceed to the next question. Ask at least one follow-up question to characterise it. For constipation: duration, stool consistency, straining, pain on defaecation. For weight loss: how many kilograms, over what time frame, was it intentional. AMC examiners specifically assess whether candidates demonstrate this probing behaviour.

- **Ask about nocturnal pain in all chronic pain presentations.** This question is non-negotiable in a four-week history. Pain that wakes from sleep implies organic pathology and shifts the differential toward inflammatory, infective, and malignant causes.

- **Ask about bowel cancer screening.** Every Australian patient aged 50 to 74 years presenting with LLQ symptoms should be asked whether they have completed their NBCSP FOBT. This question demonstrates integration of Australian preventive health guidelines and is highly regarded by AMC examiners.

- **Use structured differential groups, not random lists.** Examiners do not reward long, single-system differential lists. A structured differential spanning GI, KUB, and genital/pelvic groups — each with a briefly stated reason for inclusion or exclusion — demonstrates the breadth of clinical reasoning being assessed. Three well-explained differentials from different groups outperform ten unexplained GI diagnoses.

- **Distinguish diverticulosis from diverticulitis.** Diverticulosis is the asymptomatic presence of colonic diverticula; diverticulitis is inflammation of those diverticula, manifesting as pain, fever, and localised tenderness. These are not interchangeable. Using the incorrect term in the diagnostic explanation demonstrates imprecision and will be noted by the examiner.

- **Prior abdominal surgery is a diagnostic pivot point.** Any patient with previous abdominal surgery presenting with colicky pain, absent flatus, and a quiet abdomen on auscultation should be considered to have adhesional small bowel obstruction until proven otherwise — even if the exam stem suggests a different diagnosis. Do not anchor on the stem label; reason from the clinical findings presented during the encounter.

- **In sensitivity-requiring diagnoses, use calibrated language.** When delivering a working diagnosis of bowel cancer, use clear and compassionate language: "I am concerned about the possibility of a bowel cancer." Avoid euphemisms. Ensure the patient understands the word being used, provide reasons for the concern, acknowledge uncertainty, and immediately outline the plan for investigation.

- **The final differential list must be explained with reasons.** For each differential, state why it is on your list (supporting features) and, where relevant, why you consider it less likely (absence of key features). This approach — diagnosis with reasoning and counter-reasoning — is what AMC assessors mean when they refer to "clinical reasoning."

- **Recognise diagnostic momentum as a cognitive hazard.** If the exam stem labels the case as "diverticulitis" but the history yields absent flatus, prior appendicectomy, absent bowel sounds, and no constipation, your clinical reasoning must override the label. Examiner scenarios are designed to test whether candidates reason from evidence, not from stem labels. The diagnosis follows the clinical findings, not the referral letter.

## Safety Net

Before concluding the consultation, all patients with LLQ pain managed in the community must receive explicit safety-net instructions. These should be communicated verbally and, where possible, supported with written materials.

**Return immediately to the Emergency Department if:**
- The pain becomes significantly worse, spreads across the whole abdomen, or becomes constant and severe
- High fever develops (temperature above 38.5°C) or you feel rigors (shaking chills)
- You feel faint, dizzy, or develop a rapid heart rate
- You begin vomiting and cannot keep fluids down
- You notice blood in your stool for the first time, or bleeding increases in volume
- Your abdomen becomes hard, rigid, or tender to the slightest touch
- You feel unwell in a way you cannot describe — trust your instincts

**Return to general practice if:**
- Symptoms are not improving after 48 to 72 hours of antibiotic therapy
- You complete the antibiotic course but pain persists
- You develop any new symptoms
- You are concerned at any time

**Follow-up timeline:**
- Review in general practice at 48 to 72 hours to assess treatment response
- CT abdomen and pelvis to confirm diagnosis if not already performed
- Colonoscopy six to eight weeks after resolution of acute episode in all patients with complicated diverticulitis, or where colorectal cancer has not been confidently excluded
- Ongoing dietary counselling regarding high-fibre diet and adequate hydration for long-term prevention of recurrence
- Ensure NBCSP bowel cancer screening is completed if overdue
