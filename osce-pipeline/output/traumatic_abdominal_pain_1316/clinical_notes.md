# Traumatic Abdominal Pain — Left Upper Quadrant Injury in a Paediatric Patient

## Clinical Presentation

A 16-year-old male presents to the emergency department following a bicycle fall, complaining of left upper quadrant (LUQ) abdominal pain. This is a traumatic mechanism involving blunt abdominal injury, and the clinical priority is identifying injury to solid intra-abdominal organs — principally the spleen and left kidney — as well as adjacent structures including the ribs, lung, and great vessels. The LUQ is a high-yield anatomical region in trauma because it harbours the spleen (the most commonly injured solid organ in blunt abdominal trauma), the left kidney, the tail of the pancreas, the splenic flexure of the colon, and the lower left thoracic cage.

This station tests the candidate's ability to pivot from a standard abdominal pain structure to a trauma-oriented history, applying systematic organ-system questioning in the context of an injury mechanism rather than a medical illness. The clinical task is not simply to document pain characteristics — it is to screen for life-threatening haemorrhage, identify which organs may have been injured, and formulate an immediate investigation and management plan appropriate to an emergency department setting.

For AMC Clinical Examination candidates, this case demands fluency with the FAST ultrasound, awareness of haemodynamic assessment, and the ability to explain a provisional diagnosis and differential to a frightened adolescent patient and, implicitly, a concerned family. The station assesses history-taking skill (13 marks), physical examination knowledge (5 marks), and communication quality (12 marks), making clear that the ability to communicate findings and management in plain language carries significant weight.

---

## Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

This is a five-minute focused history-taking station in the emergency department context. The examiner expects the candidate to demonstrate four core competencies in sequence:

**1. Immediate safety and haemodynamic assessment:** Before pursuing detailed history, acknowledge the mechanism and rapidly screen for haemodynamic instability. This is not optional — a candidate who launches directly into SOCRATES without acknowledging the possibility of haemorrhagic shock has failed to prioritise appropriately.

**2. Structured trauma history using a modified SOCRATES framework:** Apply SOCRATES to the abdominal pain, then systematically screen each anatomical system at risk based on the mechanism and site of injury. Candidates should move through spleen, kidney, gastrointestinal tract, chest wall and lung, and cardiovascular structures in an organised, unhurried fashion.

**3. Mechanism exploration:** The fall itself must be explored as a distinct clinical element. The mechanism determines the probability of specific injuries — direct handlebar blow to the LUQ, height of fall, whether the patient struck their head, and whether they lost consciousness are all clinically essential data points.

**4. Diagnosis, differential, and management communication:** At station close, the candidate must provide a clear, jargon-limited explanation of the provisional diagnosis and differential, followed by a structured investigation and initial management plan. This communication must be empathic, age-appropriate, and logically organised.

ICE (Ideas, Concerns, Expectations) should be woven into the encounter — a frightened teenager will have significant anxiety about what has happened to his body, and eliciting his concerns before delivering the management plan builds rapport and demonstrates patient-centred care.

## Systematic Approach

### Opening and Haemodynamic Orientation

Begin with a warm, empathic introduction that acknowledges the patient's distress before any formal questioning. A 16-year-old presenting after a bicycle fall in the emergency department is frightened, may be in pain, and may have bystanders (family, friends) nearby. Establishing rapport immediately demonstrates communication competence.

**Opening statement:**
*"James, I'm Dr [Name], one of the emergency doctors. I can see you've had a scary fall today — I want to make sure we find out exactly what's happened and sort you out quickly. I'm going to ask you a few questions, and then we'll run some tests to check everything is okay. Is that alright?"*

Before proceeding, perform a rapid visual haemodynamic screen:
- Is the patient diaphoretic, pale, or visibly distressed?
- Is he sitting comfortably or lying still (peritonism)?
- What is his general level of responsiveness?

Ask directly:
- *"Do you feel dizzy or lightheaded at all?"*
- *"Do you feel like you might faint?"*

These questions screen for active haemorrhage and haemodynamic compromise. A positive response to either should trigger immediate escalation — nursing staff, senior review, and concurrent resuscitation — before history-taking is completed.

---

### SOCRATES Assessment of the Abdominal Pain

**Site:**
- *"Can you point to where the pain is worst?"*
- Confirm LUQ localisation. Note whether the pain is well-localised (organ capsule distension, e.g., splenic haematoma) or diffuse (haemoperitoneum causing peritoneal irritation).

**Onset:**
- Pain beginning immediately after the fall suggests direct organ injury or early haemorrhage. Delayed onset (hours after the injury) raises the possibility of delayed splenic rupture — a clinically important entity where an initial subcapsular haematoma ruptures into the peritoneal cavity hours to days after the initial trauma.

**Character:**
- Dull, constant aching pain suggests solid organ haematoma or haemorrhage.
- Sharp, pleuritic pain worsening with breathing raises concern for rib fractures or pneumothorax.
- Referred left shoulder-tip pain (Kehr's sign) — the classic presentation of diaphragmatic irritation by sub-diaphragmatic blood from splenic injury — is a high-yield finding: *"Does the pain go anywhere? Does it travel up to your left shoulder at all?"*

**Radiation:**
- Ask specifically about left shoulder-tip radiation (Kehr's sign — splenic injury).
- Ask about flank and loin-to-groin radiation (renal injury or ureteric clot passage).

**Associated symptoms:**
- Covered in detail under organ-system screening below.

**Timing:**
- Pain present since the fall (acute onset, trauma-consistent). Ask whether it is constant or comes and goes: *"Has the pain been there the whole time since you fell, or does it come and go?"* Constant pain is consistent with haemorrhage or significant organ injury.

**Exacerbating and relieving factors:**
- *"Does moving around or changing position make the pain worse or better?"*
- Movement-aggravated pain suggests peritoneal irritation (haemoperitoneum).
- Pain worsening with inspiration suggests rib fracture or diaphragmatic involvement.

**Severity:**
- *"On a scale of zero to ten, how bad is the pain right now, where zero is no pain at all and ten is the worst pain you've ever felt?"*
- Severe pain (≥7/10) in the context of trauma warrants urgent analgesia and expedited investigation.

---

### Mechanism of Injury — Essential Exploration

The mechanism of injury is the second major clinical element of this history and must be explored systematically. It directly determines the probability distribution of injuries.

**Describe the incident:**
*"Can you walk me through exactly what happened when you fell? I want to understand how it happened — what part of your body took the impact."*

Key questions:
- *"Did the handlebars hit your stomach directly?"* — A direct handlebar blow to the LUQ is the classic mechanism for splenic injury in cyclists.
- *"Did you land on your left side?"* — Left-sided impact increases probability of splenic, left renal, and left rib injury.
- *"Did you hit your chest at all?"* — Left lower chest trauma is directly associated with splenic and left renal injury.
- *"Did you injure any other part of your body?"* — Screen for concomitant injuries: long bone fractures, pelvic injury, thoracic injury.
- *"Did you hit your head?"* — This is a priority question. Head injury takes precedence over abdominal injury in the management hierarchy.
- *"Did you lose consciousness at any point, even briefly?"* — Loss of consciousness mandates head injury workup (CT brain) before the abdominal workup.

**If there is any suggestion of head injury:** The abdominal history is temporarily de-prioritised. Neurological status — GCS, confusion, headache, nausea, vomiting, visual changes, focal neurology — must be screened immediately.

### Organ-System Screening — Trauma-Specific History

Once SOCRATES and mechanism are established, proceed to systematic organ-system questioning targeted at the anatomical structures at risk in LUQ trauma. This is the conceptual core of the station: the candidate is not investigating a medical cause of abdominal pain — they are screening for traumatic injuries to specific organ systems.

#### Splenic System
The spleen is the organ most commonly injured in blunt abdominal trauma. Key questions:
- *"Are you feeling dizzy or faint?"* — Active haemorrhage from splenic injury causes hypovolaemia and lightheadedness.
- *"Has anyone mentioned that you look pale?"* — Indirect screen for haemorrhagic pallor.
- *"Do you feel pain in your left shoulder?"* — Kehr's sign (sub-diaphragmatic blood irritating the left hemidiaphragm, referred via the left phrenic nerve to the C3–C5 dermatome at the left shoulder tip).

#### Renal and Urinary Tract System
Left renal injury is the second major concern in this mechanism.
- *"Have you passed any urine since the fall?"*
- *"Was there any blood in your urine — did it look pink, red, or brown?"* — Haematuria (macroscopic or microscopic) is the hallmark finding of renal tract injury.
- *"Were you passing any clots?"* — Patients may not recognise clots as blood; this question catches injuries that produce clot rather than frankly bloody urine.
- *"Do you have any difficulty or pain when trying to pass urine?"* — Urethral or bladder injury.
- *"Any pain in your back or flank?"* — Retroperitoneal haematoma from renal injury.

#### Gastrointestinal Tract
Hollow viscus injury is less common than solid organ injury in blunt trauma but must not be missed:
- *"Do you feel sick to your stomach, or have you vomited?"*
- *"Have you vomited any blood?"* — Haematemesis suggests upper GI injury (rare in isolated blunt trauma but must be asked).
- *"Have you noticed any blood in your stools, or do your stools look black?"* — Rectal bleeding suggests lower GI injury; melaena requires significant time to develop and would not be present acutely, but fresh rectal blood from colonic injury is possible.
- *"Have you passed any wind since the fall?"* — Absent bowel sounds and failure to pass flatus may suggest ileus or bowel injury (more relevant to examination than history).

#### Respiratory System — Ribs, Lung, Pleura
Left lower rib fractures (ribs 9–11) are a classic associated injury with splenic trauma. Pneumothorax and pulmonary contusion are additional concerns.
- *"Do you have any difficulty breathing, or does it hurt to breathe deeply?"* — Pleuritic chest pain is the hallmark of rib fractures; pneumothorax presents with progressive dyspnoea.
- *"Are you feeling short of breath?"*
- *"Do you have a cough? Have you coughed up any blood?"* — Haemoptysis may indicate pulmonary contusion.

#### Cardiovascular System
In significant left-sided chest trauma, myocardial contusion and cardiac tamponade — though uncommon — are must-not-miss diagnoses.
- *"Do you have any chest pain, separate from the abdominal pain?"*
- *"Do you notice your heart racing or beating irregularly?"* — Tachycardia may indicate haemorrhage or, rarely, arrhythmia from myocardial contusion.

### Past Medical History, Medications, and Social Context

**Past medical history:**
- *"Do you have any medical conditions I should know about?"*
- Specifically ask about prior abdominal surgery (adhesions, surgical anatomical changes), haematological conditions (coagulopathy, haemophilia — critical in trauma), and immunological conditions (asplenia — patients post-splenectomy are at risk of overwhelming post-splenectomy infection and require specific antibiotic prophylaxis).

**Medications:**
- *"Are you taking any regular medications or blood-thinning tablets?"*
- Anticoagulants (warfarin, novel oral anticoagulants) dramatically increase haemorrhagic risk in trauma and must be identified immediately, as reversal agents may be required.
- NSAIDs and aspirin increase bleeding risk.

**Allergies:** Document formally.

**Last oral intake:**
- *"When did you last eat or drink anything?"* — Essential if surgical management (general anaesthesia) is likely.

**Social context:**
- Brief screen: school attendance, living situation, support at home.
- Consider mechanism plausibility: does the history of a bicycle fall explain the injury pattern, or should non-accidental injury be considered? In a 16-year-old, this is less likely but remains a clinical responsibility, particularly if the mechanism seems inconsistent with the injury severity.

---

### ICE — Ideas, Concerns, and Expectations

Elicit ICE naturally within the consultation rather than as a formal appendage at the end:

- **Ideas:** *"What's your understanding of what might have happened inside when you fell?"*
- **Concerns:** *"Is there anything in particular you're worried about?"* — Common concerns in adolescent patients following trauma include fear of permanent injury, fear of surgery, and worry about informing parents.
- **Expectations:** *"What are you hoping we'll do for you today?"*

Addressing ICE before delivering the management plan ensures patient-centred communication and builds trust for what may be a frightening series of investigations and potential surgical admission.

## Differential Diagnosis

In LUQ traumatic abdominal pain following blunt mechanism, the differential is anatomically structured. The priority hierarchy is determined by the risk of haemodynamic compromise and mortality.

| Category | Diagnosis | Key Distinguishing Features |
|----------|-----------|----------------------------|
| Common | Soft tissue injury / muscle contusion | LUQ tenderness, no haemodynamic instability, no haematuria, FAST negative |
| Common | Rib fractures (left 9–11) | Pleuritic pain, point tenderness over ribs, pain with inspiration, confirmed on CXR |
| Common | Splenic contusion / subcapsular haematoma | LUQ pain, Kehr's sign, haemodynamic stability, FAST may show perisplenic fluid |
| Important | Left renal contusion / laceration | Flank pain, haematuria (macroscopic or microscopic), confirmed on CT urogram |
| Important | Pulmonary contusion | Dyspnoea, haemoptysis, reduced air entry left base, CXR opacification |
| Important | Left pneumothorax | Dyspnoea, pleuritic pain, reduced breath sounds left, CXR confirms |
| Must Not Miss | Splenic rupture (acute or delayed) | Haemodynamic instability, peritonism, haemoperitoneum on FAST; delayed rupture 48–72 hours post-injury |
| Must Not Miss | Renal pedicle injury / Grade IV–V renal laceration | Severe haematuria, haemodynamic compromise, retroperitoneal haematoma on CT |
| Must Not Miss | Hollow viscus injury (gastric, colonic perforation) | Peritonism, absent bowel sounds, free air on AXR/CT, sepsis developing over hours |
| Must Not Miss | Cardiac tamponade | Haemodynamic instability, muffled heart sounds, JVD, Beck's triad; FAST shows pericardial effusion |
| Must Not Miss | Traumatic aortic injury | Haemodynamic instability, widened mediastinum on CXR, interscapular pain; rare in isolated bicycle trauma but must be considered in high-energy mechanisms |

## Red Flags — Must Not Miss

- **Haemodynamic instability (hypotension, tachycardia)** — Indicates active haemorrhage, most likely from splenic rupture or major vascular injury. Requires immediate fluid resuscitation, surgical team activation, and emergency FAST. Do not delay investigation for a complete history.

- **Loss of consciousness** — Even brief LOC mandates urgent neurological assessment and CT brain before abdominal workup. Intracranial injury takes priority.

- **Kehr's sign (left shoulder-tip pain)** — Pathognomonic of sub-diaphragmatic irritation by blood, most commonly from splenic injury. Elevates clinical suspicion for significant haemoperitoneum.

- **Macroscopic haematuria** — Indicates renal tract injury until proven otherwise. Mandates urgent urine dipstick, urinalysis, and CT urogram/contrast-enhanced CT abdomen and pelvis.

- **Dyspnoea or pleuritic chest pain** — Raises concern for pneumothorax, haemothorax, or pulmonary contusion. Requires urgent CXR and oxygen saturation monitoring. A tension pneumothorax is a clinical emergency requiring immediate needle decompression.

- **Peritonism (guarding, rigidity, rebound tenderness)** — Indicates haemoperitoneum or hollow viscus perforation with peritoneal contamination. Requires urgent surgical review.

- **Delayed deterioration** — A patient who appears stable initially but deteriorates 24–72 hours after injury should be considered to have delayed splenic rupture until proven otherwise. Admission for observation is mandatory in significant LUQ trauma.

- **Anticoagulant or antiplatelet medication use** — Dramatically increases haemorrhagic risk; requires urgent haematology notification and consideration of reversal (Vitamin K, prothrombin complex concentrate for warfarin; andexanet alfa for Factor Xa inhibitors).

- **Disproportionate pain severity relative to mechanism** — Should raise suspicion for significant organ injury not yet apparent clinically, or in a paediatric/adolescent patient, the possibility of non-accidental injury if the mechanism is inconsistent.

## Investigations

**Bedside:**
- Urine dipstick — immediate screening for haematuria (renal and urinary tract injury); a positive result for blood mandates formal urinalysis and imaging
- ECG — screen for arrhythmia (myocardial contusion) if there is any chest pain or palpitations
- Continuous pulse oximetry
- Vital signs monitoring (HR, BP, RR, SpO₂, GCS) — haemodynamic trending is the single most important monitoring parameter

**Bloods:**
- Full blood examination (FBE) — baseline haemoglobin, haematocrit; note that FBE may be normal acutely in haemorrhage (haemodilution has not yet occurred)
- Group and screen (blood group and crossmatch) — prepare for potential transfusion; in significant trauma this is performed urgently
- Coagulation studies (INR, APTT) — particularly if anticoagulant use is suspected or if surgery is anticipated
- Serum electrolytes, urea, creatinine, eGFR — baseline renal function
- Liver function tests, lipase — hepatic and pancreatic injury
- Venous blood gas — rapid screen for lactataemia (tissue hypoperfusion) as a surrogate marker of haemodynamic compromise

**Imaging:**
- **FAST ultrasound (Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma)** — the first-line imaging investigation in haemodynamically unstable or potentially unstable trauma patients; assesses for free fluid (haemoperitoneum) in hepatorenal, splenorenal, and pelvic pouches, and pericardial effusion; rapid, bedside, repeatable
- **Chest X-ray (erect or supine)** — screens for rib fractures, pneumothorax, haemothorax, pulmonary contusion, mediastinal widening
- **Contrast-enhanced CT abdomen and pelvis** (CT trauma/whole-body CT as clinically indicated) — definitive characterisation of solid organ injury grade, vascular injury, hollow viscus perforation, and retroperitoneal haematoma; performed in haemodynamically stable patients; grading of splenic and renal injuries directly guides management (operative vs non-operative)
- **CT brain** — if loss of consciousness, persistent altered mental status, or neurological symptoms are present

**Specialist:**
- Surgical review — mandatory for any suspected solid organ injury with haemoperitoneum, or haemodynamic instability
- Urology review — if significant renal injury (Grade III–V) is identified on imaging
- Paediatric surgical review — appropriate for a 16-year-old; management algorithms for solid organ injury in adolescent and paediatric patients increasingly favour non-operative management with close monitoring

## Management

### Immediate Resuscitation (if haemodynamically compromised)

- Position: supine, establish IV access (two large-bore peripheral IV cannulae)
- Oxygen supplementation targeting SpO₂ ≥ 95%
- IV fluid resuscitation: balanced crystalloid (Hartmann's solution or 0.9% normal saline); avoid over-resuscitation in suspected ongoing haemorrhage (permissive hypotension strategy, targeting systolic BP 80–90 mmHg until haemorrhage control is achieved surgically)
- Activate major haemorrhage protocol if haemodynamically unstable: notify blood bank, activate surgical and anaesthetic teams
- Analgesia: IV morphine (weight-based dosing) or IV fentanyl with titration; analgesic requirement in a 16-year-old should be managed according to paediatric weight-based protocols; withholding analgesia pending diagnosis is not appropriate and is not recommended by current evidence-based practice

### Investigation-Directed Management

- **FAST positive (free fluid) in stable patient:** Proceed immediately to contrast-enhanced CT abdomen and pelvis to grade the injury and plan definitive management.
- **FAST positive in unstable patient:** Immediate surgical intervention (emergency laparotomy) without delay for CT scanning.
- **FAST negative in stable patient with significant mechanism:** Observation, serial abdominal examination, and consideration of CT scanning based on clinical trajectory. A negative FAST does not exclude injury — it has limited sensitivity for solid organ injury without haemoperitoneum.
- **Haematuria confirmed:** Formal urinalysis, renal function tests, and CT with urographic phase to assess renal tract integrity.

### Definitive Management by Injury Type

**Splenic injury (Grades I–III in haemodynamically stable patients):** Non-operative management (NOM) is now the standard of care in stable patients, including adolescents, at a trauma centre with surgical backup. This involves ICU or high-dependency admission, serial haemoglobin measurements, continuous haemodynamic monitoring, and serial abdominal examination. Grade IV–V injuries, haemodynamic instability, or failure of NOM require operative intervention (splenorrhaphy or splenectomy).

**Renal injury (Grades I–III):** Non-operative management with bed rest, hydration, and serial urinalysis. Higher-grade injuries (Grade IV–V with vascular involvement) require urology review and consideration of angiographic embolisation or surgical intervention.

**Rib fractures:** Adequate analgesia (oral or IV), physiotherapy for breathing exercises to prevent atelectasis and pneumonia, and respiratory monitoring. Intercostal nerve blocks may be considered for severe pain.

**Pneumothorax:** Small pneumothorax — observation with supplemental oxygen. Moderate-to-large pneumothorax — intercostal catheter drainage. Tension pneumothorax — immediate needle decompression (second intercostal space, midclavicular line) followed by formal drainage.

**Admission and observation:**
- All patients with significant LUQ trauma should be admitted for a minimum of 24 hours of observation, even if initial imaging is unreassuring, given the risk of delayed splenic rupture.
- Vital signs monitoring every 1–2 hours.
- Serial abdominal examination.
- Repeat haemoglobin at 4–6 hours post-injury.

## Communication and Patient Education

### Explaining the Clinical Situation to the Patient

Given the patient is 16 years old — a competent adolescent under Australian law — communication should be directed primarily to the patient while acknowledging that parents or guardians are likely present and will need to be involved in consent discussions, particularly regarding any operative intervention.

**Age-appropriate explanation of concern:**
*"James, after hearing about your fall and asking you these questions, I'm concerned that you may have injured some of the organs inside your abdomen — particularly your spleen, which is on the left side of your stomach, and your kidney on the same side. I'm not certain which one has been injured, or how seriously, and that's exactly what the tests we're going to do will help us find out."*

**Explain the investigations:**
- *"The first thing we're going to do is a special ultrasound scan called a FAST scan — it stands for Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma. It's a quick, painless scan we do right at the bedside that tells us if there's any fluid or blood in your abdomen."*
- *"We're also going to take some blood from you — to check your blood count and make sure you're not losing blood internally — and we'll get a urine sample to check for any blood in your urine, which would suggest a kidney injury."*
- *"We'll also do a chest X-ray to check your ribs and lungs."*

**Explain the admission:**
*"Regardless of what those initial tests show, we're going to keep you here in hospital for observation. Even if the first scan looks okay, some injuries — like a bruised spleen — can sometimes develop problems hours after the initial injury, and we need to be here to catch that quickly if it happens."*

**Explain the possibility of surgery:**
*"If the scans do show a significant injury to your spleen or kidney, there's a possibility we might need to perform surgery to fix it. I know that sounds frightening, but our surgical team would talk you through that carefully before anything happened, and it's not something we'd do unless it was necessary."*

### Addressing Patient and Family Concerns

Common concerns to anticipate and address proactively:
- *"Will I be okay?"* — Reassure that he is in the right place, being assessed thoroughly, and the team will act quickly on any concerning findings.
- *"Do I need an operation?"* — Honest acknowledgement that this is possible, but that the team will make decisions based on what investigations show.
- *"How long will I be here?"* — At minimum overnight; possibly longer depending on findings.
- *"Can I play sport again?"* — Premature to address acutely, but acknowledge the question and commit to discussing it once the extent of injury is known.

## AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

- **Never skip the haemodynamic screen in trauma history.** Even in a history-taking-only station, asking about dizziness, syncope, and pallor demonstrates that you understand haemodynamic stability is the first clinical priority in abdominal trauma. Examiners are specifically looking for this.

- **Explore the mechanism of injury as a separate, structured element.** Many candidates apply SOCRATES and move directly to systems review without exploring how the injury occurred. The mechanism — direct handlebar blow, height of fall, side of impact, head strike — directly determines the injury differential and must be documented.

- **Always ask about loss of consciousness.** Head injury takes priority over abdominal injury. A candidate who pursues an extensive abdominal history while failing to ask about LOC has demonstrated a dangerous clinical omission.

- **Ask specifically about Kehr's sign (left shoulder-tip pain).** This is a classic AMC examination finding in splenic injury cases. It demonstrates anatomical knowledge of referred pain pathways and directly supports a diagnosis of splenic haemorrhage.

- **Haematuria is the key diagnostic pivot.** If the patient reports blood in the urine, this shifts the primary diagnosis from splenic to renal injury. Always ask specifically about haematuria and passage of clots in LUQ trauma.

- **Introduce the FAST scan by name and explain it.** The examiner expects candidates to name the FAST scan as the first-line imaging investigation in trauma and to explain it in lay terms. Candidates who jump to CT as the first investigation demonstrate an incorrect understanding of the trauma investigation algorithm.

- **Do not omit blood group and crossmatch.** In any patient with significant abdominal trauma, preparation for potential blood transfusion is mandatory. Mentioning FBE without crossmatch is an incomplete management response.

- **Mention delayed splenic rupture in your differential.** This is a high-yield, examiners' favourite finding. Stating that a patient with initial haemodynamic stability can deteriorate 24–72 hours later due to delayed rupture of a subcapsular haematoma demonstrates advanced clinical reasoning and justifies admission for observation.

- **Frame the differential in anatomical systems, not as a random list.** Present differentials as: solid organs (spleen, kidney) → hollow viscus → chest wall → cardiopulmonary → cardiovascular. This structured approach is more clinically coherent and impressive than listing diagnoses in isolation.

- **Address the adolescent patient's perspective in communication.** The examiner will note whether you acknowledge the patient's age and emotional state. An empathic opening, use of first name, and age-appropriate language score communication marks that many candidates forfeit by treating the encounter as purely technical.

- **Acknowledge that a normal initial FAST does not exclude injury.** Demonstrating awareness that the FAST has limitations (particularly for solid organ injury without haemoperitoneum) and that serial examination and repeat imaging may be required reflects clinical nuance that differentiates candidates.

- **Connect soft tissue injury as a common and reassuring differential.** Mentioning that the most common outcome of this mechanism is soft tissue (muscle and subcutaneous) injury — which requires only analgesia and follow-up — demonstrates balanced clinical reasoning and prevents the candidate from appearing to catastrophise every case of traumatic abdominal pain.

## Safety Net

Before the patient leaves the emergency department — and critically, if at any point during the observation period the patient is discharged — the following safety net instructions must be provided, ideally in written form, to both the patient and their guardian:

**Return immediately to the emergency department if any of the following develop:**
- Sudden worsening of abdominal pain or new onset of severe pain
- Dizziness, faintness, or feeling as though you might pass out
- Left shoulder tip pain developing or worsening
- Any blood in the urine
- Shortness of breath or difficulty breathing
- Vomiting, particularly if vomiting blood
- Increasing pallor, cold sweat, or rapid heartbeat
- Any loss of consciousness, confusion, or headache (raising concern for delayed presentation of head injury)

**Follow-up timeline:**
- If discharged after observation: urgent review with the patient's general practitioner within 24–48 hours with a formal discharge summary detailing the investigation results and the injury grade documented on imaging.
- Repeat imaging (ultrasound) at follow-up if initial CT showed a low-grade solid organ injury managed non-operatively, in accordance with local trauma unit protocols.
- Return to full physical activity (including sport) only after imaging confirmation of injury resolution — typically 6–8 weeks for low-grade splenic injury, under guidance of the surgical or urology team managing the case.
- The patient and family should be given the direct phone number for the treating ward or surgical team, and instructed not to hesitate to call if concerned about any symptom change in the first 72 hours.
