Central Abdominal Pain: Bowel Obstruction, Strangulated Hernia, and Mesenteric Ischaemia — AMC Clinical Examination History Taking Station

Clinical Presentation

A 50-year-old man presents to the emergency department with severe, acute-onset central abdominal pain of several hours' duration. The pain is rated as maximally severe and has been partially addressed with analgesia prior to assessment. This presentation represents one of the highest-stakes acute abdomen scenarios encountered in clinical practice and in the AMC Clinical Examination, as the anatomical localisation to the central abdomen encompasses a broad and potentially lethal differential diagnosis spanning gastrointestinal, vascular, urological, and gynaecological pathology.

The central abdomen is clinically distinct from the right iliac fossa or left iliac fossa in that pain here does not reliably localise to a single organ system. Patients frequently describe periumbilical, epigastric, and hypogastric pain interchangeably as "central," meaning the clinician must treat this as a diffuse abdominal presentation and systematically exclude all relevant differential groups. The examiner expects the candidate to recognise this diagnostic uncertainty early and respond with a structured, comprehensive history rather than premature diagnostic closure.

This station has significant clinical importance because it encompasses time-critical diagnoses — including bowel obstruction, strangulated hernia, mesenteric ischaemia, and ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm — where delayed recognition carries a high risk of morbidity and mortality. The AMC Clinical Examination assesses the candidate's ability to methodically identify diagnostic patterns through systematic history taking, apply appropriate red flag screening, and synthesise a prioritised differential diagnosis within the constraints of the consultation time.

Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

This is a history taking station in which the candidate is expected to take a structured four-to-six minute history, acknowledge the patient's distress appropriately, apply SOCRATES methodology to the presenting complaint, systematically screen all relevant differential diagnostic groups, elicit red flags, and conclude with a prioritised working diagnosis and differential list.

The station follows a defined structure. The candidate opens by acknowledging the patient's distress and establishing rapport before initiating the history — emotional acknowledgement is a scored domain. The candidate then explores the presenting pain using SOCRATES, followed by a systematic review of gastrointestinal, urological, vascular, and relevant other systems. A focused red flag screen for sinister pathology is mandatory. The station concludes with the candidate synthesising findings, stating a working diagnosis with supporting evidence, listing appropriate differentials, and outlining an investigation and management framework. In stations where a physical examination card is provided, the candidate reads the findings, incorporates them into the diagnostic synthesis, and proceeds accordingly.

The examiner's core expectation for this station is that the candidate identifies bowel obstruction early through targeted questioning — specifically the absence of flatus and failure to open the bowels — and then systematically elicits the cause of obstruction. Candidates who screen only for the primary presentation without exploring its aetiology will not achieve a passing performance.

Socrates Flow

Systematic Approach

Opening and Emotional Acknowledgement

Before commencing the history, the candidate must assess dynamic stability. This is done rapidly: enquire about or confirm blood pressure, pulse rate, respiratory rate, and temperature. This signals to the examiner that the candidate recognises this as an acute and potentially haemodynamically significant presentation.

The candidate then introduces themselves professionally and responds to the patient's distress with genuine acknowledgement before transitioning to structured questioning. An appropriate opening acknowledges the emotional dimension of the presentation:

"I can see that you're in a great deal of pain and discomfort. I want to help you, and to do that I need to ask you some questions to better understand what is happening. Is that alright with you?"

This approach demonstrates the communication skills scored as a discrete domain in the AMC Clinical Examination. Candidates must not proceed directly to clinical questioning without first addressing the patient's distress, as this will be marked down in the communication domain.

SOCRATES Assessment of the Presenting Pain

Site: Ask where the pain is located. If the patient describes it as "central," follow up with clarification: "Can you show me — is it around your belly button, above it, or below it?" In this station, the patient will typically describe periumbilical pain that resists precise localisation. Accept this and proceed, recognising that the entire central abdomen must be considered.

Onset: Establish when the pain began and whether onset was sudden or gradual. Sudden, severe onset raises concern for vascular catastrophes including ruptured AAA and mesenteric ischaemia. Gradual onset over hours to days is more consistent with bowel obstruction or inflammatory pathology.

Character: Ask whether the pain is sharp, dull, cramping, colicky, or constant. Colicky pain — waxing and waning in intensity — is characteristic of hollow viscus obstruction. Constant, severe pain suggests peritoneal inflammation or vascular compromise.

Radiation: Enquire whether the pain radiates anywhere. Radiation to the back raises concern for AAA or pancreatitis. Radiation to the groin or loin suggests ureteric colic.

Associated symptoms: This is the most diagnostically productive component of the SOCRATES assessment in this presentation. Mandatory associated symptom enquiry includes:
- Nausea and vomiting: present in bowel obstruction, mesenteric ischaemia, and strangulated hernia
- Abdominal distension: characteristic of bowel obstruction
- Passage of flatus: absence is a cardinal feature of bowel obstruction. If the patient reports absent flatus, immediately follow up with "When did you last pass wind?" — even a few hours of absent flatus is significant in this context
- Bowel habit: "When did you last open your bowels?" Failure to open the bowels for several days, combined with absent flatus and distension, is the clinical triad of bowel obstruction
- Haematemesis or rectal bleeding: dark or fresh blood per rectum raises concern for ischaemic colitis, mesenteric ischaemia, and gastrointestinal malignancy
- Fever and rigors: suggest sepsis, peritonitis, strangulation, or perforation

Timing: Is this the first episode? Has the patient experienced similar episodes before? Recurrent episodes suggest intermittent obstruction, incarcerated hernia with spontaneous reduction, or intermittent volvulus.

Exacerbating and relieving factors: Ask whether any position changes, eating, or activity alters the pain. In early small bowel obstruction, there may be no consistent relieving factor. Relationship to meals may suggest ischaemic bowel disease or mesenteric angina if chronic.

Severity: Ask the patient to rate the pain on a scale of zero to ten. Severe pain (eight to ten out of ten) with minimal abdominal findings on examination — pain out of proportion to clinical signs — is the hallmark clinical feature of mesenteric ischaemia.

Systematic Differential Group Screening

Following SOCRATES, the candidate moves through each differential diagnostic group in a structured sequence. The examiner marks each domain; omitting a group risks losing marks even if the primary diagnosis is correct.

Gastrointestinal Group:
- Nausea, vomiting, anorexia
- Abdominal distension and bloating
- Passage of flatus — absence is critical
- Bowel habit: diarrhoea, constipation, time since last bowel action
- Blood in stool: fresh (haematochezia) or dark/melaena
- Changes in stool calibre or bowel habit (colorectal malignancy)
- Jaundice (hepatobiliary pathology)

Gastrointestinal Red Flag Symptom Screen:
- Unintentional weight loss
- Loss of appetite
- Unexplained fatigue or anaemia
- Any lumps or bumps noticed
- Family history of bowel cancer
- Any prior positive faecal occult blood test or colonoscopy findings

Urological Group (KUB — Kidneys, Ureters, Bladder):
- Dysuria
- Urinary frequency
- Haematuria (frank or microscopic)
- Loin pain
- Absence of urinary symptoms reduces the probability of renal colic but does not exclude it entirely

Genitourinary Group — Male Specific:
- Scrotal swelling, redness, or pain: raises concern for testicular torsion or epididymo-orchitis
- Groin lumps or swellings: this is the critical question for inguinal hernia — "Have you noticed any lump or swelling in your groin?"
- Any change in testicular size or consistency

Vascular Group — Must Not Miss:
This group is particularly important in a 50-year-old male with severe central pain.

For ruptured or leaking Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA):
- Known AAA or prior vascular imaging
- History of hypertension, dyslipidaemia, smoking
- Family history of AAA or aortic disease
- Pain radiating to the back or flank

For Mesenteric Ischaemia:
- History of atrial fibrillation or other cardiac arrhythmia: "Have you ever been told your heart beats irregularly?"
- History of known cardiac disease, prior myocardial infarction, or peripheral vascular disease
- History of thromboembolism (deep venous thrombosis, pulmonary embolism)
- Cardiovascular risk factors: hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidaemia, smoking
- Palpitations or sensation of fast or irregular heartbeat

The clinical signature of mesenteric ischaemia is severe, central abdominal pain disproportionate to relatively modest physical examination findings. This is the cardinal diagnostic clue. In a patient with atrial fibrillation and severe central pain, mesenteric ischaemia must be at the top of the differential until excluded.

Cardiorespiratory Group:
- Chest pain, dyspnoea: inferior myocardial infarction can present with epigastric or periumbilical pain
- Cardiac history and cardiac risk factors as above
- Respiratory symptoms suggesting pneumonia with referred abdominal pain (less common but relevant in lower lobe pneumonia)

Dermatological:
- Vesicular rash: shingles (herpes zoster) can present with severe unilateral abdominal pain prior to rash eruption. While classically unilateral, it may be reported by the patient as "central." Ask: "Have you noticed any blisters or rash on your skin, particularly around the trunk?"

Trauma:
- Any recent abdominal trauma, even minor
- Seat belt injury
- Any recent procedural interventions

Surgical and Medical History

Previous surgical history is critical in this station. Prior abdominal surgery is the most common predisposing cause of small bowel obstruction due to adhesion formation. Ask specifically:
- "Have you had any previous operations on your abdomen or elsewhere?"
- Document the type of surgery, site, and date
- Appendicectomy, cholecystectomy, bowel resection, gynaecological surgery, and any laparotomy all predispose to adhesion formation

Previous hernia history:
- Prior diagnosis of inguinal, umbilical, incisional, or femoral hernia
- Prior hernia repair (mesh placement creates adhesions)
- Any history of groin or abdominal wall swelling that comes and goes

Current medications: particularly anticoagulants (raise concern for mesenteric venous thrombosis or intramural haematoma), NSAIDs (gastrointestinal ulceration), and immunosuppressants (atypical presentations of intra-abdominal pathology).

Allergies.

Family history: colorectal cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, AAA, cardiac disease.

Social history: smoking (vascular risk), alcohol (pancreatitis, hepatic disease), diet, functional status, and occupation (heavy lifting predisposing to hernia).

Physical Examination Card Integration

In the online AMC examination format, a physical examination card is provided following history taking. In the face-to-face examination, a full abdominal examination is conducted. The candidate reads the card and incorporates findings into the diagnostic synthesis.

Typical physical examination findings for small bowel obstruction:
- Temperature: normal (fever suggests strangulation or perforation)
- Generalised abdominal tenderness without rebound (rebound tenderness suggests peritoneal involvement)
- Guarding on palpation
- Hyperresonance to percussion (from air trapping)
- Absent bowel sounds on auscultation (late bowel obstruction; note that in early obstruction, high-pitched or "tinkling" bowel sounds are characteristically present)
- Digital rectal examination: empty rectum (consistent with complete obstruction)

For strangulated inguinal hernia:
- Tender, irreducible swelling in the groin
- The two critical questions the candidate must ask or elicit during physical examination are:
1. Is the hernia tender?
2. Is the hernia reducible?
- An irreducible, tender groin lump in a patient with features of bowel obstruction confirms strangulated inguinal hernia until proven otherwise

For mesenteric ischaemia:
- Irregular pulse rhythm (atrial fibrillation)
- Generalised abdominal tenderness that is disproportionately mild relative to the severity of pain reported
- Dark or blood-stained stool on digital rectal examination (late finding, representing ischaemic mucosal sloughing)
- Tachycardia; later, signs of sepsis and haemodynamic compromise

Redflags Panel
Clinically Enriched

Red Flags — Must Not Miss

  • Haemodynamic instability (hypotension, tachycardia) — In a patient with central abdominal pain, hypotension mandates immediate exclusion of ruptured AAA, septic shock from bowel perforation or strangulation, and haemorrhagic pancreatitis. Initiate resuscitation in parallel with assessment. Do not defer haemodynamic management while taking history.

  • Pain out of proportion to clinical examination findings — The hallmark of mesenteric ischaemia. If a patient describes excruciating pain (eight to ten out of ten) but abdominal palpation reveals only modest tenderness without guarding, mesenteric ischaemia must be the leading diagnosis. This discordance is a surgical emergency requiring urgent CT angiography and vascular surgery input.

  • Absent flatus combined with failure to open bowels — The combination of no bowel action for more than two days and absent flatus is pathognomonic of complete bowel obstruction until proven otherwise. This requires urgent imaging and surgical review.

  • Irreducible, tender groin or abdominal wall swelling — An irreducible hernia with features of bowel obstruction and local tenderness indicates strangulation — compromised blood supply to incarcerated bowel. This is a time-critical surgical emergency. Delay beyond several hours risks bowel necrosis.

  • Atrial fibrillation with acute severe abdominal pain — This combination must trigger immediate consideration of mesenteric ischaemia due to embolism. Rapid deterioration is expected. CT mesenteric angiography and urgent surgical or interventional radiology consultation are mandatory.

  • Fever with abdominal pain and peritoneal signs — Fever combined with guarding, rigidity, or rebound tenderness indicates peritonitis from perforation or strangulation. This is a surgical emergency requiring immediate resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and operating theatre preparation.

  • Dark or melaena stools with severe abdominal pain — Suggests upper gastrointestinal haemorrhage, ischaemic colitis with mucosal necrosis, or advanced mesenteric ischaemia with bowel infarction. Requires urgent resuscitation and haemoglobin measurement.

  • Pulsatile central abdominal mass — This is a ruptured or rapidly expanding AAA until proven otherwise. Do not delay for imaging if the patient is haemodynamically unstable — this requires immediate vascular surgical intervention.

  • Previous abdominal aortic aneurysm on surveillance — Any known AAA patient presenting with sudden severe abdominal or back pain must be treated as a ruptured AAA until imaging excludes it. Do not miss this history point.

  • Generalised peritonism with rigid abdomen — Board-like rigidity, generalised guarding, and diffuse rebound tenderness indicate generalised peritonitis — perforated viscus, ruptured appendix or diverticulum, or bowel infarction. Requires emergency laparotomy.

Investigations

Bedside:
- Vital signs with continuous monitoring (blood pressure, heart rate, oxygen saturation, temperature, respiratory rate)
- 12-lead ECG — identify atrial fibrillation (mesenteric ischaemia risk), inferior ST changes (cardiac source of pain), arrhythmia
- Urinalysis — haematuria (renal colic), infection (urinary tract pathology), glycosuria (diabetic emergency)
- Bedside glucose — diabetic ketoacidosis can present with acute abdominal pain
- Erect abdominal X-ray and erect chest X-ray — mandatory first-line imaging:
- Erect AXR: multiple air-fluid levels with small bowel dilation (>3 cm) confirm small bowel obstruction; Rigler's sign or free air confirms perforation
- Erect CXR: free air under the diaphragm indicates visceral perforation
- Supine AXR: complementary to erect for assessing bowel gas pattern and colonic dilation

Bloods:
- Full blood count — anaemia (chronic blood loss from malignancy), leucocytosis (infection, ischaemia, strangulation)
- Electrolytes, urea, creatinine — dehydration, electrolyte disturbance from vomiting and obstruction, renal function
- C-reactive protein — inflammatory marker elevated in infection, ischaemia, and strangulation
- Serum amylase and lipase — exclude acute pancreatitis
- Liver function tests — hepatobiliary pathology
- Coagulation studies — if mesenteric ischaemia or AAA suspected, or surgical preparation
- Group and screen or crossmatch — if AAA or surgical emergency suspected
- Troponin — if inferior MI is a differential
- Lactate — elevated in bowel ischaemia and sepsis; a serum lactate greater than 2 mmol/L in the context of abdominal pain raises urgent concern for ischaemic bowel
- Blood cultures — if systemic sepsis suspected

Imaging:
- CT abdomen and pelvis with intravenous contrast (contrast-enhanced CT) — the definitive investigation for most causes of acute abdominal pain including bowel obstruction (identifies level and cause), mesenteric ischaemia (CT mesenteric angiography), strangulated hernia, AAA, and intra-abdominal mass
- CT angiography of the abdomen — if AAA or mesenteric vascular occlusion is the primary concern
- Abdominal ultrasound — bedside assessment for AAA, free fluid, hepatobiliary pathology; limited utility for bowel obstruction in the acute setting but accessible and radiation-free

Specialist:
- Surgical consultation — urgent for bowel obstruction, strangulated hernia, suspected AAA, or peritonitis
- Vascular surgery — for AAA and mesenteric ischaemia
- Gastroenterology — if inflammatory bowel disease or gastrointestinal malignancy is the primary consideration after acute emergency excluded
- Cardiology — if arrhythmia management (atrial fibrillation) is required in the context of mesenteric ischaemia

Management

Initial Resuscitation (All Presentations)

  • Establish intravenous access — two large-bore peripheral cannulae
  • Intravenous fluid resuscitation: crystalloid (normal saline or Hartmann's solution) titrated to haemodynamic response
  • Nil by mouth — in anticipation of surgical intervention and to reduce ongoing bowel distension
  • Adequate analgesia — parenteral opioids are appropriate and do not mask clinical signs in modern emergency practice; do not withhold analgesia pending diagnosis
  • Urinary catheter with strict fluid balance monitoring
  • Nasogastric tube — essential for decompression in established bowel obstruction; reduces distension, nausea, and aspiration risk

Small Bowel Obstruction — Conservative Initial Management

  • Nil by mouth and nasogastric decompression (formal "drip and suck" protocol)
  • Intravenous fluid replacement to correct dehydration and electrolyte disturbance (particularly hypokalaemia from vomiting)
  • Serial abdominal examinations to detect deterioration, developing peritonism, or failure to resolve
  • Repeat erect AXR at 6–12 hours to assess progress
  • Most simple adhesional small bowel obstruction resolves with conservative management within 24–48 hours
  • Failure to resolve, clinical deterioration, signs of strangulation, or complete obstruction mandate operative intervention (laparotomy or laparoscopic adhesiolysis)

Strangulated Inguinal Hernia — Surgical Emergency

  • Do not attempt manual reduction of a strangulated hernia (risks reducing gangrenous bowel)
  • Emergency surgical referral and preparation for theatre
  • Broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics (covering gram-negative organisms and anaerobes) — prior to surgical repair given risk of bowel necrosis
  • Operative management: herniorrhaphy with assessment of bowel viability; resection of necrotic bowel if required

Mesenteric Ischaemia — Time-Critical Vascular Emergency

  • Urgent CT mesenteric angiography to confirm diagnosis and identify the site and nature of vascular occlusion (embolic vs thrombotic)
  • Anticoagulation with intravenous unfractionated heparin if embolic aetiology confirmed (in absence of contraindication)
  • Rate control and consideration of cardioversion for atrial fibrillation (underlying cause of embolism in embolic mesenteric ischaemia)
  • Urgent vascular surgery consultation — open embolectomy or thrombolysis depending on clinical state and centre capability
  • Emergency laparotomy if peritonitis or bowel necrosis is present — bowel resection with or without primary anastomosis depending on intraoperative findings
  • Broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics
  • ICU admission for postoperative monitoring

Treatment of Underlying Cause

The fundamental management principle for bowel obstruction is to identify and treat the underlying cause. Decompression and fluid resuscitation address the sequelae; cure requires removal of the obstructing pathology — be it adhesion division, hernia repair, tumour resection, or volvulus reduction.

Communication and Patient Education

Explaining Small Bowel Obstruction from Adhesions

Use clear, non-technical language. Structure the explanation around what is happening, why it has happened, and what will be done:

"The most likely explanation for your symptoms is a condition called small bowel obstruction. This means that your bowels — the tubes that carry food through your abdomen — have become blocked. In your case, the most likely cause of the blockage is scar tissue that formed after your previous operation. After any abdominal surgery, bands of scar tissue called adhesions can develop, and occasionally these bands wrap around a section of bowel and squeeze or kink it, causing a blockage. When the bowel is blocked, food, fluid, and gas cannot pass through, which is why you haven't been able to pass wind or open your bowels, and why your abdomen feels bloated and painful."

Reasons supporting this diagnosis to share with the patient:
- You have not been passing wind or opening your bowels
- Your abdomen feels distended and uncomfortable
- You have had previous abdominal surgery — a known risk factor for adhesion formation
- On examination, bowel sounds are absent and there is excess air in the abdomen

Explaining Strangulated Inguinal Hernia

"You have a condition called a strangulated inguinal hernia. A hernia is a defect in the muscular wall of the abdomen through which a small pouch or sac has pushed out. In your case, this sac is located in the groin. Part of your bowel has slipped into this sac and has become stuck — we call this incarceration. When the bowel gets stuck and its blood supply becomes compressed, it becomes what we call strangulated — this is a serious situation that requires urgent surgery."

Explaining Mesenteric Ischaemia

"You have a condition called mesenteric ischaemia. Your heart has been beating irregularly — a condition called atrial fibrillation. When the heart beats irregularly, there is a risk that a small blood clot can form inside the heart. In your case, it appears that one of these clots has travelled through the bloodstream and has lodged in one of the arteries supplying blood to your intestines, blocking it. Without adequate blood supply, the bowel tissue begins to suffer, which is causing your severe pain. This is a serious condition that requires urgent treatment."

Addressing Common Patient Concerns

  • Patients frequently express fear about surgery — acknowledge this and explain what the surgical team will do to ensure their safety
  • Patients may ask whether this will happen again — explain that after surgery, adhesiolysis or hernia repair significantly reduces recurrence risk, though no procedure eliminates it entirely
  • Patients with mesenteric ischaemia from AF should be counselled about anticoagulation to prevent recurrence once the acute episode is managed

AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

  • Never omit dynamic stability assessment at the start of an acute abdomen station. Confirming haemodynamic parameters before sitting down to take the history is a marked item and demonstrates clinical prioritisation. A haemodynamically unstable patient requires immediate action before history taking proceeds.

  • Respond to patient distress before transitioning to structured questioning. A patient who says "I just can't take this anymore" is expressing distress, not only information. Acknowledge the distress explicitly and briefly before beginning SOCRATES. Failure to do so costs communication marks.

  • Ask about flatus and bowel action as separate, specific questions. Asking only "are you constipated?" is insufficient. Many candidates miss the bowel obstruction diagnosis because they conflate constipation with obstruction. Ask separately: "Are you passing wind?" and "When did you last open your bowels?" These are the diagnostic questions that unlock the bowel obstruction diagnosis.

  • Treat the absence of flatus as a positive finding requiring follow-up. If a patient reports not passing wind for several hours, immediately clarify: "When was the last time you passed wind?" Short durations may be normal; days without flatus is pathological.

  • Mesenteric ischaemia is identified by pain out of proportion to examination findings. If the history card or examiner indicates severe generalised pain but physical examination findings are relatively mild, mesenteric ischaemia must be your first differential. Do not dismiss this discordance — it is the cardinal clinical sign.

  • Screen for atrial fibrillation and cardiovascular risk factors in all central abdominal pain cases. This is not optional in a 50-year-old male. Asking about palpitations, irregular heartbeat, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, and prior cardiac events is a mandatory component of the vascular differential screen.

  • Previous surgical history is the single most important risk factor for adhesional bowel obstruction. Do not move through this item superficially. Ask specifically what operation, when, and where — a prior appendicectomy, cholecystectomy, or laparotomy all significantly elevate the pre-test probability of adhesional obstruction.

  • When a groin lump is found, ask two questions — tender? reducible? — before formulating your diagnosis. An irreducible, tender hernia is strangulated until proven otherwise. A reducible, non-tender hernia in the context of bowel obstruction is incarcerated without strangulation. The distinction has immediate management implications.

  • State your working diagnosis first, then your differentials. The examiner is marking whether you can synthesise findings and commit to a diagnosis. Hedging without stating a primary diagnosis is penalised. Use the formula: "Based on the history of absent flatus, no bowel action for three days, distension, and prior abdominal surgery, my working diagnosis is small bowel obstruction, most likely from adhesions."

  • When listing differentials, prioritise by clinical plausibility, not alphabetically. Structure differentials as: causes of obstruction first (adhesions, malignancy, hernia, volvulus), then vascular (mesenteric ischaemia, AAA), then other abdominal causes (pancreatitis, IBD), then extra-abdominal (inferior MI, lower lobe pneumonia). This demonstrates clinical reasoning.

  • Request erect AXR and erect CXR as first-line imaging, not CT. While CT is the definitive investigation, the erect abdominal X-ray is the appropriate first step in acute bowel obstruction and can be ordered in the initial management response. State both: first AXR (erect and supine), then CT for confirmation and cause identification.

  • Maintain your systematic structure even after the diagnosis becomes apparent. A candidate who stops screening other differential groups as soon as bowel obstruction becomes evident will miss marks awarded for completing the systematic assessment. In a six-minute station, completing the full structure is both feasible and expected.

Safety Net

Immediate return to emergency department if:
- Pain becomes suddenly much worse, or changes in character from colicky to constant — may indicate strangulation, perforation, or bowel necrosis
- Development of fever or rigors
- Abdominal rigidity or becoming hard to touch
- Inability to keep fluids down (persistent vomiting)
- No improvement or deterioration despite initial management
- Development of haemodynamic symptoms: dizziness, fainting, pallor, rapid heartbeat

In patients managed conservatively for bowel obstruction:
- Serial review every four to six hours in hospital with repeat clinical examination
- Serial AXR at 6–12 hour intervals to document resolution or progression
- Absence of clinical and radiological improvement within 24–48 hours mandates surgical review and operative planning
- Clear written instructions must not be given for home discharge — patients with diagnosed bowel obstruction require inpatient management

For patients with identified atrial fibrillation as a precipitant:
- Anticoagulation counselling and cardiology follow-up are mandatory prior to discharge from any future episode
- Provide written information about the signs of mesenteric ischaemia and the importance of reporting acute abdominal pain to an emergency department without delay

Patient education materials:
- Written information on bowel obstruction, its causes, and warning signs
- Dietary advice from a hospital dietitian for patients recovering from obstruction or bowel surgery
- Referral letter to surgical outpatient clinic for follow-up at two to four weeks post-discharge
- Information on the National Bowel Cancer Screening Programme for patients aged 50 and over (in cases where malignancy was identified as a concern during investigation)
