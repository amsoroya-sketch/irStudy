Gastro-Oesophageal Reflux Disease: Counselling Station

Clinical Presentation

A 35-year-old man presents to a general practice setting with a several-month history of worsening heartburn. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy has already been performed and the results are available for discussion. Endoscopic findings demonstrate oesophagitis, a small sliding hiatus hernia, absence of peptic ulceration, negative Helicobacter pylori testing, and no features of Barrett's oesophagus. The candidate is required to explain these investigation results in plain language, take a focused relevant history to identify modifiable risk factors, explain the diagnosis and its complications, and outline a structured management plan.

This station is clinically important for AMC candidates because gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) is one of the most prevalent conditions in Australian primary care, presenting across a spectrum of severity from intermittent mild heartburn to confirmed erosive oesophagitis with potential for malignant transformation. The confirmed endoscopic finding of oesophagitis — rather than symptomatic GORD alone — significantly changes the management threshold and necessitates proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy rather than simple antacid use. Candidates who conflate symptom-only GORD management with confirmed mucosal disease management will underscore on treatment decisions.

The station carries a dual burden of history taking and communication, with four distinct tasks embedded within a single encounter: explaining investigation results, taking a focused risk factor history, explaining the diagnosis and complications, and presenting a management plan. Efficient time management is therefore an essential skill, and candidates who spend disproportionate time on one component at the expense of others risk failing despite sound clinical knowledge.

Counselling Pathway

Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

This is a four-task communication station integrating focused history taking with comprehensive patient counselling. The examiner assesses the candidate's ability to translate clinical findings into accessible patient language, elicit modifiable risk factors efficiently, explain a diagnosis with its implications, and negotiate a practical management plan using shared decision-making principles.

Opening and rapport: Begin with a warm, professional greeting and an open-ended question to invite the patient's narrative. Allow the patient to briefly articulate their understanding and concerns before transitioning to structured content delivery. A natural entry point such as "Can you tell me what has been happening for you recently, and what you were told before coming in today?" establishes rapport and surfaces the patient's existing understanding.

ICE framework elicitation: Before explaining results, briefly assess the patient's Ideas (what they believe is causing their symptoms), Concerns (what they are most worried about — cancer is the dominant concern in a patient presenting for endoscopy results), and Expectations (what they hope to learn or achieve today). This prevents the common candidate error of launching into results delivery without acknowledging patient anxiety.

Information giving — structured, layered, plain language: Deliver endoscopy results in a logical sequence, checking understanding after each element. Use the "chunk and check" technique: deliver one item of information, confirm comprehension, then proceed. Employ anatomical drawings where genuinely helpful — a hiatus hernia is one of the few GORD-related findings where a simple sketch of the diaphragm, oesophagus, and herniated gastric fundus meaningfully aids patient comprehension.

Focused risk factor history: Transition naturally from results explanation to risk factor assessment. Frame this as personalised care: "Now that we know what the endoscopy shows, I'd like to ask you a few questions so I can tailor your treatment plan." Cover lifestyle, dietary, medication, and body habitus factors concisely. This component should occupy no more than two to three minutes.

Diagnosis and complications: Clearly name the diagnosis using both medical terminology and its plain-language equivalent. Outline relevant complications in plain terms without causing undue alarm. Acknowledge that early, well-treated oesophagitis carries an excellent prognosis.

Management plan and shared decision making: Present non-pharmacological measures first, then pharmacological therapy. Involve the patient in decisions where appropriate — for example, timing of PPI administration. Agree on a follow-up plan. Address medication safety in the context of long-term PPI use.

Safety netting: Provide specific red flag symptoms that warrant urgent re-presentation. Confirm the patient's understanding and invite final questions.

Systematic Approach

Task 1: Explaining Endoscopy Results

Explaining investigation results to a patient requires a structured, plain-language approach. Begin by confirming what the patient already knows about the procedure before delivering findings.

Explaining the procedure itself:

"We performed an endoscopy for you. This involved passing a thin, flexible camera down your food pipe — what doctors call the oesophagus — and into your stomach to take a direct look at the lining."

Finding 1 — Oesophagitis:

"What we found is that there is some inflammation of the lining of your food pipe. We call this oesophagitis. Think of it like the lining being irritated and a little raw — similar to what happens when acid from the stomach repeatedly comes back up and makes contact with the food pipe lining."

Clarify immediately that oesophagitis is reversible with appropriate treatment, and that the aim of management is to heal this inflammation and prevent recurrence.

Finding 2 — Absence of ulcers and absence of Barrett's oesophagus:

"The good news is that we did not find any ulcers — no open sores. We also looked very carefully for any pre-cancerous changes in the lining, which we call Barrett's oesophagus. We did not find any of these changes. We took tissue samples under a microscope to make sure, and there are no concerning features."

This addresses the patient's unspoken but highly prevalent fear: cancer. Naming it explicitly and then dismissing it with evidence is more reassuring than omitting it entirely.

Finding 3 — Hiatus hernia:
This requires the most careful explanation due to the non-causal relationship between the hiatus hernia and the oesophagitis — a distinction that is consistently tested.

"We also found what is called a hiatus hernia. Let me draw this for you, as it is easier to understand with a picture."

[Draw: chest cavity above, abdominal cavity below, diaphragm as a horizontal muscle, oesophagus descending through an opening in the diaphragm, with a small portion of the stomach herniated upward through the diaphragmatic opening into the chest.]

"Normally, your stomach sits entirely in your abdomen, below this large muscle called the diaphragm. In your case, the top portion of your stomach has slipped upward through the opening in this muscle and sits slightly in your chest. We call this a hiatus hernia. Importantly, this hernia is not the cause of your inflammation. It may sometimes make reflux symptoms a little worse, but the hernia itself has not caused the damage we are treating. Most people with a hiatus hernia have no symptoms at all."

This explicit clarification of the non-causative role of the hiatus hernia is a key discriminator in this station. Candidates who state or imply that the hernia caused the oesophagitis demonstrate an inaccuracy that examiners will note.

Finding 4 — Helicobacter pylori negative:

"We also tested for a bacteria called Helicobacter pylori, which is a common bug that can cause ulcers and increase the risk of stomach problems. The test came back negative — so you do not have this infection, which is reassuring."

Task 2: Focused Risk Factor History

The history in this station is deliberately narrow and time-limited. It is not an open-ended exploratory history — it is a targeted risk factor assessment to identify modifiable contributors to GORD and oesophagitis that will directly inform the management plan.

Opening:

"Now that I have explained the results, I would like to ask you a few questions about your lifestyle and other factors, so I can put together the best management plan for you."

Substances:
- "Do you smoke? If so, how many cigarettes per day?"
- "Do you drink alcohol? How often, and roughly how much on a typical occasion?"

Dietary habits:
- "Do you eat a lot of fatty or fried food, such as takeaway meals?"
- "Do you eat spicy food regularly?"
- "How much coffee or tea do you drink each day?"
- "Do you drink fizzy or carbonated drinks, energy drinks, or colas?"
- "Do you eat chocolate regularly?" (Chocolate, along with coffee and alcohol, reduces lower oesophageal sphincter tone.)

Eating patterns:
- "Do you tend to eat large meals, or eat late at night and then go to bed shortly after?"

Medications:
- "Are you taking any regular medications — including over-the-counter medications or supplements?"
- Specifically screen for: non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), aspirin, bisphosphonates (alendronate), doxycycline, and calcium channel blockers — all of which either directly damage the oesophageal mucosa or reduce lower oesophageal sphincter tone.

Body habitus:
- "Are you overweight at the moment, or have you put on weight recently?" — Increased intra-abdominal pressure from excess weight is a significant and modifiable driver of reflux. Ask this directly; it is not offensive in a clinical context and directly informs the management plan.

Stress:
- "Have you been under significant stress lately?" — Stress does not cause GORD but can exacerbate symptom perception and eating behaviours.

Prior treatment:
- "Have you tried any medications for this already? If so, which ones, and did they help?" — If the patient is already on a PPI, establish the dose, timing, and compliance, as subtherapeutic dosing or incorrect timing (e.g., taking PPI with food rather than thirty minutes before meals) is a frequent cause of treatment failure.

Red flags — brief screen only (given confirmed endoscopy):
Given that endoscopy has already excluded malignancy, an exhaustive red flag screen is not the priority here. A brief, targeted check is appropriate:
- Unintentional weight loss
- Progressive dysphagia (difficulty swallowing)
- Haematemesis or malaena

Task 3: Explaining the Diagnosis and Complications

Naming the diagnosis:

"Based on your symptoms and your endoscopy results, you have a condition called gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, or GORD. This means that acid from your stomach is coming back up into your food pipe more than it should. The acid is causing the inflammation we saw — the oesophagitis. The good news is that this is a very treatable condition, and the inflammation we found is reversible with the right treatment and lifestyle changes."

Explaining the underlying mechanism in plain language:

"Between your food pipe and your stomach, there is a ring of muscle that acts like a valve. In GORD, this valve is not closing as tightly as it should, which allows stomach acid to come back up. Over time, repeated exposure to acid causes the inflammation we found."

Complications — to explain clearly but without causing undue anxiety:

Provide these in plain language, framed as the reason why treatment is important:

  • Peptic ulceration: "If the inflammation is not treated, it can progress to open sores — ulcers — in the food pipe lining. These can bleed."
  • Bleeding and anaemia: "Bleeding from the food pipe can lead to anaemia — a low blood count — causing fatigue and weakness."
  • Oesophageal stricture: "Repeated scarring from inflammation can cause narrowing of the food pipe, making it difficult to swallow. We call this a stricture."
  • Barrett's oesophagus: "If acid irritation continues over many years, the lining of the food pipe can change into an abnormal type of tissue. This is called Barrett's oesophagus. Although you do not have this at present, it is one of the reasons we treat GORD seriously — because this changed lining carries a small but real risk of developing into cancer over time."
  • Oesophageal adenocarcinoma: "In a small number of people with long-standing, untreated GORD and Barrett's oesophagus, cancer of the food pipe can develop. This is why we want to treat your condition thoroughly and follow you up."

"All of these complications are preventable with proper treatment. The fact that we have caught this at the stage of oesophagitis — without ulcers, without Barrett's — means you are in a very good position to get on top of this."

Task 4: Management Plan

Non-Pharmacological Management (Lifestyle and Dietary Modification)

Present lifestyle measures first. These are first-line for mild intermittent GORD and remain essential adjuncts for confirmed oesophagitis.

Dietary modifications:
- Reduce or eliminate fatty and fried foods
- Reduce or eliminate spicy foods
- Eliminate or substantially reduce caffeine: coffee, tea, cola, energy drinks, chocolate
- Eliminate or substantially reduce carbonated (fizzy) drinks
- Reduce citrus fruits and tomato-based products
- Reduce or eliminate alcohol
- Eat smaller meals; avoid large meals, particularly in the evening
- Avoid eating within two to three hours of bedtime
- Eat slowly and chew food thoroughly

Positional and behavioural modifications:
- Elevate the head of the bed by fifteen to twenty centimetres (using blocks or a wedge, not simply extra pillows, which are ineffective)
- Avoid lying down for at least two hours after meals
- Avoid tight-fitting clothing around the abdomen

Smoking cessation: Smoking reduces lower oesophageal sphincter tone and delays gastric emptying. Smoking cessation is both a specific recommendation for GORD and a general health priority.

Weight reduction: In overweight or obese patients, weight loss is among the most effective single interventions for reducing reflux. Even a five to ten percent reduction in body weight produces clinically significant improvement. Refer to a dietitian if appropriate.

Medication review: If the patient is taking NSAIDs, aspirin, or other mucosal irritants, explore whether these can be ceased or substituted. In this case, if aspirin is being used for migraine prophylaxis, acknowledge this but make clear that alternative migraine management will be addressed at a separate consultation — it is not within the scope of today's station to redesign the migraine management plan.

Pharmacological Management

Management algorithm based on frequency and severity:

The Australian guidelines use a step-up approach for uncomplicated GORD, but confirmed oesophagitis on endoscopy bypasses the lower rungs of this ladder entirely.

For mild intermittent GORD (symptom-only, no mucosal disease):
1. Trial antacids (e.g., Mylanta, Gaviscon) — available over the counter; liquid formulations are marginally more effective than tablets
2. If antacids fail: H₂-receptor antagonist (e.g., famotidine) — note that ranitidine has had availability issues in Australia due to contamination concerns and famotidine is the preferred H₂ blocker
3. If H₂ blocker fails: PPI

For confirmed oesophagitis (this patient):
Proceed directly to high-dose PPI therapy. Antacids and H₂ blockers are insufficient for confirmed mucosal disease.

"Given that we have confirmed inflammation in your food pipe on the camera test, I am going to prescribe a medication called a proton pump inhibitor — a PPI. The most common one is omeprazole."

Specific prescribing for oesophagitis:
- High-dose PPI: omeprazole 20 mg twice daily (or equivalent: esomeprazole, pantoprazole, lansoprazole)
- Duration: minimum four to eight weeks for oesophagitis healing
- Timing: take thirty minutes before the first meal of the day (and before the evening meal if dosing twice daily) — taking PPI with food significantly reduces efficacy
- If symptoms are predominantly nocturnal or sleep-related, evening dosing is appropriate

PPI safety and long-term considerations — step-down therapy:

This is an important concept in contemporary Australian general practice and should be communicated to the patient:

"PPIs are effective medications, but we do not want to use the highest dose forever if it is not necessary. Once your inflammation has healed — usually after six to eight weeks — we will review whether we can reduce the dose or use it only when you have symptoms."

Explain step-down strategy:
1. High dose → standard dose → alternate day dosing → as-required use
2. Abrupt cessation is not recommended as it produces rebound acid hypersecretion

Long-term PPI risks (for patient education and PBS authority prescribing awareness):
- Interstitial nephritis (renal)
- Hypomagnesaemia
- Increased risk of Clostridium difficile and community-acquired pneumonia
- Reduced bone mineral density with prolonged use (increased fracture risk in older patients)
- Vitamin B₁₂ and iron malabsorption with very long-term use

In Australia, high-dose ongoing PPI prescribing for chronic GORD requires PBS authority approval — a regulatory mechanism designed to encourage prescribers to reassess the ongoing indication at each renewal.

Surveillance and Follow-up

  • Repeat upper GI endoscopy: for confirmed oesophagitis without metaplasia, surveillance endoscopy is recommended approximately every one to two years, or sooner if symptoms fail to resolve or new alarm features develop
  • If medical therapy fails despite optimal dosing, compliance, and lifestyle adherence: refer for surgical assessment — laparoscopic Nissen fundoplication is the definitive surgical procedure, in which the gastric fundus is wrapped around the lower oesophagus to reinforce the lower oesophageal sphincter

H. pylori Management (if positive — for completeness)

Although this patient's H. pylori testing is negative, candidates should be familiar with management in the event results differ in a future station variant:

  • First-line eradication (triple therapy): PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin for seven days — available in Australia as a combined pack (HP7)
  • Second-line (quadruple therapy): if triple therapy fails, due to increasing clarithromycin resistance
  • Test of cure: urea breath test (UBT) is the investigation of choice — performed at least four weeks after completing antibiotics and at least two weeks after ceasing PPI to avoid false negatives. Faecal antigen testing is an alternative but less commonly used. Repeat endoscopy for eradication testing is not routinely indicated unless there is another clinical reason.

Red Flags — Must Not Miss

The following alarm features in any patient with GORD symptoms or known oesophagitis require urgent investigation (typically repeat endoscopy or urgent specialist referral) and must not be attributed to acid reflux alone:

  • Progressive dysphagia (difficulty swallowing, particularly solids) — may indicate oesophageal stricture or malignancy; distinguish from odynophagia (painful swallowing, which suggests severe mucosal ulceration or infection)
  • Odynophagia (pain on swallowing) — suggests advanced mucosal disease, severe ulceration, or superimposed candidal oesophagitis
  • Unintentional weight loss — a cardinal alarm symptom in any gastrointestinal presentation; requires exclusion of malignancy
  • Haematemesis (vomiting blood) — indicates active bleeding from oesophageal ulceration, varices, or malignancy; requires urgent endoscopic assessment
  • Malaena (black, tarry stools) — indicates upper gastrointestinal blood loss; urgent investigation required
  • Iron deficiency anaemia — may represent occult gastrointestinal blood loss from ulceration or malignancy
  • Persistent vomiting — particularly if new, forceful, or associated with weight loss; requires exclusion of obstruction or malignancy
  • Sentinel symptoms of Barrett's / adenocarcinoma: longstanding GORD (>five years), male sex, obesity, smoking — this cluster warrants earlier endoscopic surveillance even in the absence of alarm symptoms
  • Failure to respond to adequate PPI therapy — if symptoms persist despite high-dose PPI for four to eight weeks with confirmed compliance, re-evaluate the diagnosis; consider eosinophilic oesophagitis, functional disease, or malignancy

Investigations

Bedside:
- Urea breath test (H. pylori — test of cure post-eradication, or primary diagnostic test in non-endoscoped patients)
- Weight and BMI measurement
- Blood pressure (cardiovascular risk co-assessment)

Bloods:
- Full blood count (FBC) — screen for iron deficiency anaemia suggesting occult blood loss
- Iron studies, serum ferritin — confirm iron deficiency if FBC is abnormal
- Urea and electrolytes (U&E), creatinine — baseline renal function before long-term PPI therapy; nephritis surveillance
- Serum magnesium — baseline and monitoring in long-term PPI use
- Liver function tests — if alcohol use is significant

Imaging / Endoscopic:
- Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (already performed in this case) — the gold standard investigation; allows direct visualisation, biopsy, and grading of oesophagitis (Los Angeles Classification Grade A–D), identification of hiatus hernia, Barrett's oesophagus, malignancy
- Barium swallow — now largely superseded by endoscopy but occasionally used to assess motility, stricture anatomy, or large hiatus hernia
- Oesophageal pH monitoring (ambulatory 24-hour pH impedance study) — used when symptoms persist despite PPI therapy and the diagnosis requires objective acid exposure quantification; specialist investigation

Specialist:
- Oesophageal manometry — assesses lower oesophageal sphincter pressure and peristaltic function; used pre-operatively before fundoplication
- Surgical referral — for consideration of laparoscopic Nissen fundoplication in refractory GORD or when long-term PPI therapy is not tolerated

Clinically Enriched

Management

Non-pharmacological (applicable to all patients):

Lifestyle and dietary modification as detailed in the systematic approach above. These measures are not optional adjuncts — they are first-line therapy for symptom-only GORD and essential co-interventions alongside PPI therapy for oesophagitis. Weight reduction should be specifically targeted in overweight patients and is the most evidence-supported lifestyle intervention for GORD.

Pharmacological — stepped approach:

GORD Severity First-Line Therapy Escalation
Mild intermittent (no mucosal disease) Antacid (Mylanta, Gaviscon) — liquid form preferred → H₂ blocker (famotidine) → PPI if inadequate response
Frequent or severe symptoms (no mucosal disease) PPI (omeprazole 20 mg daily, thirty minutes before breakfast) Increase dose or frequency if inadequate
Confirmed oesophagitis (this patient) High-dose PPI (omeprazole 20 mg twice daily) for minimum four to eight weeks Reassess; step down after symptom control achieved
H. pylori positive (not this patient) PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin × 7 days (HP7) Quadruple therapy if failure; UBT test of cure at ≥4 weeks

Step-down strategy after oesophagitis healing:
Once symptoms are controlled and healing is confirmed, titrate PPI to the lowest effective dose:
1. Reduce from twice daily to once daily
2. Reduce to alternate day dosing
3. Transition to as-required use if symptoms are intermittent and well-controlled
4. Avoid abrupt cessation — reduces rebound acid hypersecretion

Surgical:
- Laparoscopic Nissen fundoplication: considered when medical therapy fails, when patient cannot tolerate long-term PPI, or when there is a large symptomatic hiatus hernia. Not indicated as initial management. The fundus of the stomach is wrapped (360 degrees) around the lower oesophagus to augment the lower oesophageal sphincter.

Surveillance:
- Repeat upper GI endoscopy: one to two years for confirmed oesophagitis without metaplasia. Frequency increased if oesophagitis is severe or incompletely healed at follow-up.
- Barrett's oesophagus (if subsequently identified): formal surveillance program with biopsies at defined intervals per Gastroenterological Society of Australia (GESA) guidelines.

Communication and Patient Education

Explaining oesophagitis in plain language:
Use the language of inflammation and irritation rather than disease — this is less alarming and more accurate for the patient's current stage. The food pipe is irritated and raw; the cause is acid coming back up from the stomach; the inflammation is healable; the goal of treatment is to reduce acid, allow healing, and prevent recurrence.

Addressing the most common patient fear — cancer:
Patients presenting for endoscopy results almost universally fear a cancer diagnosis. Name this directly: "I know many people worry about whether this could be something more serious. I want to be clear that we checked very carefully for any pre-cancerous or cancerous changes and found none." This explicit reassurance, grounded in the objective endoscopic findings, is far more effective than general reassurance.

Explaining the hiatus hernia without causing alarm:
The hernia finding frequently generates disproportionate patient concern, particularly if the patient has looked it up online. Be clear: most hiatus hernias cause no symptoms; this hernia did not cause the oesophagitis; it does not require surgical treatment at this stage; it is a common, incidental finding.

Explaining why PPI cannot simply be taken forever:
Use straightforward language: "This medication is very effective, but we do not want you to take the highest dose indefinitely if it is not needed. Once your inflammation has healed, we will work on finding the lowest dose that keeps your symptoms under control. We will reduce the dose gradually — stopping suddenly can cause symptoms to come back."

Framing lifestyle change as treatment, not punishment:
Patients are more likely to adhere to lifestyle changes when they understand the mechanism: "Caffeine, alcohol, fatty food, and smoking all relax the valve between your food pipe and stomach, making it easier for acid to come back up. Reducing these is not just about general health — it directly reduces the acid damage to your food pipe."

Written information:
Provide a printed summary of dietary triggers to avoid, PPI dosing instructions (dose, timing relative to meals, duration), and a clear list of red flag symptoms. NPS MedicineWise and the Gastroenterological Society of Australia provide patient-facing resources suitable for use in Australian general practice.

Follow-up plan:
- Review in four to six weeks to assess symptom response to PPI and lifestyle changes
- Repeat endoscopy at one to two years
- Earlier review if red flag symptoms develop

AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

  • Do not conflate symptom-only GORD with endoscopically confirmed oesophagitis. Once mucosal disease is confirmed, go directly to high-dose PPI. Beginning with antacids or H₂ blockers in a patient with proven oesophagitis is a significant management error that will cost marks.

  • The hiatus hernia is NOT the cause of the oesophagitis. State this clearly and unambiguously when explaining results. Candidates who imply or state a causal link will lose marks. The correct explanation is: "Most hiatus hernias are asymptomatic; the hernia may worsen reflux in some cases, but it did not cause your inflammation."

  • Use the "chunk and check" technique throughout results explanation. Deliver one piece of information, confirm comprehension, then proceed. Do not recite all findings consecutively without checking understanding — the examiner is assessing communication quality, not information volume.

  • Draw the hiatus hernia diagram. For this specific finding, a simple anatomical sketch meaningfully improves patient understanding and demonstrates patient-centred communication. It is one of the very few situations in an AMC communication station where a drawing genuinely adds clinical value.

  • Manage time across all four tasks. Allocate approximately ninety seconds to two minutes for results explanation, two minutes for focused history, two minutes for diagnosis and complications, and the remaining time for management. Candidates who spend five minutes on results explanation and then run out of time for management will fail a station they otherwise know well.

  • Weight loss is the single most commonly forgotten non-pharmacological intervention. Ask about it explicitly in the history and recommend it explicitly in the management plan. Dietary advice without weight reduction advice is an incomplete response.

  • PPI timing matters. When counselling on PPI use, specify that the medication should be taken thirty minutes before eating, not with food or after eating. This is clinically significant — acid suppression is substantially reduced when PPIs are taken with meals.

  • The step-down PPI concept demonstrates contemporary Australian GP practice. Mentioning step-down therapy (dose reduction once healing is achieved, transition to as-required use where appropriate) signals familiarity with evidence-based prescribing and PBS authority requirements. This differentiates candidates who know the concept from those who only know to prescribe.

  • Safety net for cancer must be explicit, not implied. When discussing red flags, name dysphagia, weight loss, and haematemesis specifically. Do not say "come back if things get worse" — specify the symptoms.

  • H. pylori management must be complete. If asked about H. pylori (in this station or a variant), the answer is triple therapy (HP7 pack), followed by a urea breath test at four or more weeks post-treatment as the test of cure. Do not substitute serology for the UBT — serology cannot confirm eradication.

  • Acknowledge the patient's concerns before launching into results. Asking "What were you hoping to find out today?" or "Is there anything specific you have been worried about?" before delivering results is not time wasteful — it is what earns marks in the communication domain and takes less than twenty seconds.

  • Atypical GORD presentations may arise in related stations. Chronic cough, hoarse voice, dental erosion, and nocturnal symptoms are extra-oesophageal manifestations of GORD. If the presenting complaint is cough and acid reflux is suspected, high-dose PPI for a defined trial period is both diagnostic and therapeutic.

Safety Net

Provide the patient with specific, actionable guidance on when to seek urgent medical attention. Vague instructions such as "come back if you feel unwell" are insufficient. The following instructions should be communicated verbally and reinforced in writing:

Return urgently (same day, emergency department if necessary) if you experience:
- Difficulty or pain on swallowing that is new, persistent, or worsening — this may indicate a stricture or, rarely, a new cancer and requires prompt endoscopic reassessment
- Vomiting blood, or material that looks like coffee grounds — indicates active upper GI bleeding
- Black, tarry, or very dark stools — indicates digested blood from the upper GI tract; requires urgent assessment
- Significant unintentional weight loss over a short period

Return to your GP within one to two weeks if:
- Symptoms are not improving despite commencing PPI and lifestyle changes
- You develop new or worsening chest pain — cardiac causes must always be excluded
- You experience significant side effects from medication (e.g., diarrhoea, headache, muscle cramps)

Routine follow-up:
- Review appointment in four to six weeks to assess response to PPI therapy and lifestyle changes
- Repeat upper gastrointestinal endoscopy at approximately one to two years to confirm healing of oesophagitis and screen for any interval change
- Earlier endoscopy if any alarm symptoms develop before the scheduled review

Ongoing monitoring on long-term PPI:
- Periodic renal function and magnesium levels if PPI use continues long-term
- Annual medication review to reassess ongoing indication and trial step-down therapy
