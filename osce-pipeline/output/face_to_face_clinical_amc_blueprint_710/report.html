The AMC Clinical Examination: Blueprint, Station Architecture, and Candidate Framework

Clinical Presentation

The AMC Clinical Examination is a high-stakes, face-to-face objective structured clinical examination (OSCE) assessing the clinical competency of international medical graduates seeking registration to practise medicine in Australia. The examination is designed at the standard of a newly graduated Australian intern, with the expectation that candidates demonstrate safe, patient-centred clinical practice across the breadth of general clinical medicine. Understanding the structural architecture of the examination — its station types, scoring philosophy, time constraints, and assessment domains — is a prerequisite for effective preparation and is itself a core competency that separates prepared candidates from those who approach the examination without strategic insight.

The examination comprises twenty stations in total, of which sixteen are assessed performance stations and four are designated rest stations. Each performance station runs for ten minutes: two minutes of stem reading outside the room, followed by eight minutes of clinical performance within the room. The clinical content spans five broad topic areas — medicine and surgery (assessed together and constituting the largest proportion), physical examination, paediatrics, obstetrics and gynaecology, and psychiatry and mental health. The exact distribution of stations across these domains varies between examination cohorts, with the most consistent finding across recent examination cycles being that at least fifty percent of assessed stations are drawn from medicine and physical examination combined.

The examination format includes role-played patient encounters (the most common format), telephone consultation stations, video-based assessment scenarios (particularly in mental health), handover and clinical communication tasks, and procedural or equipment-based tasks such as medication charting and urinalysis interpretation. Candidates who approach the examination without awareness of these station variants risk significant performance loss through lack of mental preparation. The following framework provides a systematic account of each station type, the clinical skills being assessed, the time management demands particular to each, and the communication and clinical reasoning standards expected by AMC examiners.

Counselling Pathway

Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

The AMC Clinical Examination assesses candidates against a defined mark sheet anchored to examiner satisfaction at the standard of a competent Australian intern. While the mark sheet introduces an element of objectivity, the fundamental standard remains the professional judgement of the examiner regarding whether the candidate's performance meets the threshold of safe, effective, patient-centred clinical practice in the Australian healthcare context.

The examination employs four distinct Predominant Assessment Areas (PAAs), each of which defines the design rationale for a given station, the weighting of tasks within that station, and the scoring criteria applied by the examiner. These four PAAs are:

  1. History Taking — Systematic clinical history with diagnosis and differential formulation
  2. Diagnostic Formulation — Integration of history and physical examination findings to establish a diagnosis
  3. Management, Counselling, and Education — Patient-centred management planning and clinical communication
  4. Physical Examination — Performed clinical examination with accurate identification and reporting of findings

Each PAA appears across all topic areas. A paediatrics station may be structured as a counselling PAA; an obstetrics station may be structured as a diagnostic formulation PAA. The PAA is identifiable from the stem and task instructions, and correctly identifying the PAA is the first clinical decision a candidate must make upon reading the stem.

Systematic Approach

PAA 1: History Taking Stations

History taking stations allocate six to seven minutes for history acquisition, with the remaining one to two minutes allocated to communicating a diagnosis and differential diagnoses. The predominant clinical skill being assessed is the structured, logical, and patient-centred acquisition of clinical history.

Expected structure:

  • Opening: Professional introduction, confirmation of patient identity and presenting complaint, establishment of rapport
  • History of Presenting Complaint: Systematic exploration using a validated symptom framework (SOCRATES for pain; chronological symptom progression for systemic presentations). The history should identify key discriminating symptoms, characterise the complaint fully, and establish temporal relationships between symptoms.
  • Systematic Probing of Positive Findings: When a clinically significant symptom is elicited, the candidate must probe it specifically. A positive response to haemoptysis, for example, requires immediate characterisation (volume, frequency, relationship to cough, associated symptoms) rather than simple acknowledgement and progression to the next item.
  • Red Flag Screening: Mandatory systematic exclusion of sinister pathology. For a respiratory presentation, this includes constitutional symptoms (unintentional weight loss, night sweats, fatigue), haemoptysis, progressive dyspnoea at rest, and risk factors for malignancy or pulmonary embolism.
  • Relevant Background History: Past medical history, surgical history, medication list (including over-the-counter agents and supplements), allergy status, family history of relevant conditions, social history (smoking, alcohol, occupation, travel, living situation), and functional status.
  • ICE Framework: Elicitation of the patient's Ideas, Concerns, and Expectations is an integral component of every history, not an optional adjunct. In Australian clinical practice, this is fundamental to patient-centred care and is explicitly assessed in the AMC examination. "What do you think might be causing your symptoms?" (Ideas); "Is there anything particular you were worried it might be?" (Concerns); "What were you hoping we might be able to do today?" (Expectations).
  • Closing the History: Summarise the key findings, confirm accuracy with the patient, and transition to diagnosis and differentials.

Diagnosis and Differentials (final 1–2 minutes):

The candidate must present a prioritised differential diagnosis list with explicit clinical reasoning — not simply a list of conditions. The examiner expects the candidate to integrate the history findings and explain why each diagnosis is being considered. The list must include the most likely diagnosis, important alternative diagnoses, and at least one must-not-miss diagnosis even if it is clinically improbable. Memorised recall lists without accompanying reasoning will not meet the AMC standard.

PAA 2: Diagnostic Formulation Stations

Diagnostic formulation stations integrate history acquisition with physical examination findings to establish a diagnosis. The large majority of these stations (approximately ninety percent) follow Group A format: four minutes of history taking, followed by a structured verbal request for physical examination findings from the examiner, concluding with a diagnosis and differential presentation. A minority of stations (Group B) provide a printed physical examination and investigation card to the candidate at the end of a six-minute history, after which the candidate formulates and presents their diagnosis.

Group A Structure and Time Allocation:

The compressed four-minute history demands disciplined prioritisation. The candidate must identify the single most diagnostically important line of questioning and pursue it efficiently, while retaining sufficient breadth to avoid anchoring bias. The recommended time distribution for the remaining four minutes following the history prompt bell is: two minutes for the physical examination request task, and two minutes for diagnosis and differential formulation and presentation.

The Physical Examination Request Task (Asking Findings from the Examiner):

This task is consistently identified as a source of candidate failure and requires specific preparation. AMC mark sheet language specifies that the examiner will provide findings only when each specific component of the examination is requested individually. The examiner is instructed to respond to non-specific requests with the question: "What would you like to know?"

The correct approach is a structured, sequential verbal examination conducted as a clinical dialogue:

  • "I would like to perform a respiratory examination. Starting with inspection — is there any chest wall deformity? What is the chest wall movement like? Is it symmetrical? Are there any intercostal recessions?"
  • The examiner confirms each finding individually before the candidate proceeds.
  • "I would like to palpate. Can you tell me the tracheal position? Is chest expansion symmetrical? Is tactile vocal fremitus increased or decreased at the bases?"
  • "I would like to percuss. Is there any dullness or hyper-resonance? Are there any differences between the zones?"
  • "I would like to auscultate. Are breath sounds present and equal bilaterally? Are there any added sounds — crackles, wheeze, or pleural rub?"

Candidates must study the specific components of each system examination to perform this task competently. The time constraint is absolute: this task must be completed within two minutes, requiring fluency and preparatory drill.

Common failure modes in Group A:

  • Exceeding two minutes on the physical examination request task, leaving insufficient time for diagnosis and differentials
  • Non-specific requests that generate "What would you like to know?" responses and consume additional time
  • Presenting only one or two differentials before time expires
  • Failure to provide clinical reasoning with the diagnosis

PAA 3: Management, Counselling, and Education Stations

Counselling stations are characterised by extended stems of two to three pages, designed to provide the candidate with a confirmed or near-confirmed diagnosis and sufficient background information to proceed directly to management planning and patient communication. The stem is intentionally dense; however, only two to three key clinical facts within it are diagnostically determinative. Efficient stem reading during the two-minute pre-station period requires rapid identification of the diagnosis, the patient's key clinical parameters, and any specific concerns or context flagged in the stem.

Core Tasks in Counselling Stations:

  1. Explain the diagnosis to the patient in plain language
  2. Present the management plan clearly, specifically, and with patient-centred framing
  3. Address the patient's concerns and hidden agenda
  4. Obtain informed consent where relevant
  5. Arrange follow-up and safety netting

Patient-Centred Communication — The Non-Negotiable Standard:

The AMC examination explicitly assesses patient-centred approach in counselling stations. In the Australian clinical context, the patient is an active participant in decision-making. The candidate must:

  • Acknowledge and validate the patient's perspective before presenting clinical information
  • Offer management options with a balanced account of benefits and risks rather than directive instruction
  • Respond specifically to patient preferences — if a patient declines a recommended treatment, the candidate must explore the reason and discuss alternatives
  • Avoid paternalistic communication styles
  • Use health literacy-appropriate language, free of jargon

Specificity in Management Counselling:

A common and significant failure mode is vague or deflective management counselling — statements such as "I'll refer you to a specialist" or "We'll get some tests done" without specific content. Counselling stations require detailed, guideline-concordant management knowledge. For a station involving newly diagnosed COPD, for example, the candidate is expected to explain the diagnosis using accessible language, discuss smoking cessation as the single most effective intervention and offer referral to a cessation programme, outline pharmacological management (short-acting bronchodilator for symptom relief, long-acting bronchodilator for maintenance, inhaled corticosteroid if appropriate), discuss pulmonary rehabilitation, address vaccination (influenza, pneumococcal), outline an action plan for exacerbations, and arrange appropriate follow-up. Vague or incomplete management plans do not meet the AMC standard regardless of communication style.

ICE in Counselling Stations:

The patient's concerns in counselling stations are frequently embedded in the stem or will be raised by the role player during the station. The candidate must actively invite the patient's concerns early in the consultation — "Before I go through what I've found, is there anything in particular you were worried about?" — and return to address those concerns specifically and empathically throughout the station. Failing to address a patient's explicitly stated concern is a significant mark-sheet omission.

PAA 4: Physical Examination Stations

Physical examination stations require the candidate to perform a clinical examination on a role player (who may have real or simulated findings), demonstrate correct technique, maintain patient dignity and comfort, and identify and report examination findings accurately to the examiner.

Key Assessment Domains:

  • Technique: Correct procedural steps, appropriate sequencing, and clinical accuracy. Examinations must follow the standard Australian clinical format for the relevant system.
  • Organisation and Sequence: A logical, efficient examination sequence that minimises unnecessary patient repositioning and demonstrates clinical understanding of the system being examined.
  • Patient Comfort, Safety, and Modesty: Candidates must seek consent, warn the patient before potentially uncomfortable manoeuvres, maintain draping and modesty, and demonstrate consideration for patient dignity throughout.
  • Clinical Context Application: The examination approach must be appropriate to the specific clinical scenario. A traumatic abdominal examination, for example, differs structurally and in priorities from an examination for suspected hepatomegaly. Candidates who apply an undifferentiated generic examination framework regardless of clinical context will not achieve full marks.
  • Accuracy of Finding Identification: Candidates must correctly identify and report physical signs left by the examination team — murmurs, organomegaly, lymphadenopathy, neurological deficits — with appropriate characterisation.
  • Equipment Familiarity: Recent examination cycles have included scoring of correct handling and operation of clinical equipment including the otoscope, ophthalmoscope, and urinalysis dipstick. Inability to operate equipment correctly constitutes a scorable deficiency.

Special Station Formats

Telephone Consultation Stations:

The candidate enters a room containing only a telephone handset. The role player is on the other end of the line. The clinical tasks mirror standard consultation formats (history taking, counselling, management advice), adapted for the telephone medium. Candidates must introduce themselves professionally, confirm patient identity and safety, and adapt their communication style for a non-visual consultation. These stations occur across gastroenterology, ophthalmology, paediatrics, and general medicine topics.

Video-Based Mental State Examination Stations:

These stations involve the candidate observing a video of a patient and then performing a mental state examination or formulating a diagnosis and differentials for the examiner. There is no role player. The candidate should direct their commentary to the examiner in a structured, systematic format following standard MSE domains.

Handover and Clinical Communication Stations:

These stations require the candidate to communicate clinical information to a colleague, complete a medication chart, or perform a structured clinical task in the absence of a patient role player. The ISBAR (Identify, Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) framework is the standard structure for clinical handover in Australian healthcare.

Red Flags — Must Not Miss

  • Haemoptysis — In any respiratory presentation, mandates urgent exclusion of pulmonary malignancy and pulmonary embolism; requires immediate investigation planning and appropriate urgency framing
  • Unintentional weight loss — Constitutional red flag across all systems; raises suspicion for occult malignancy or systemic illness; must be specifically screened in all appropriate presentations
  • Progressive dyspnoea at rest — Indicates severe or decompensated cardiopulmonary pathology; requires urgent assessment and cannot be managed conservatively without investigation
  • Suicidal ideation or intent — In any mental health presentation; requires immediate structured risk assessment using Australian clinical standards; safety planning and crisis referral are mandatory
  • Chest pain with radiation, diaphoresis, or haemodynamic instability — Must-not-miss acute coronary syndrome; requires immediate action regardless of station format, including in telephone consultation stations
  • Altered conscious state or confusion in any age group — Requires systematic exclusion of metabolic, neurological, and infective causes; red flag in paediatric, medicine, and aged care stations
  • Domestic violence disclosure — May emerge in any station; candidate must respond with a trauma-informed, non-judgemental approach and knowledge of mandatory reporting obligations and local referral pathways
  • Failure to address a patient's explicitly stated concern — While not a clinical red flag in the traditional sense, failure to respond to a patient's concern in a counselling station represents a structural omission that directly affects mark-sheet scoring

Investigations

Bedside:
Peak flow measurement, pulse oximetry, urinalysis dipstick, ECG, blood glucose level, urine pregnancy test (where clinically indicated)

Bloods:
Full blood count, electrolytes and urea and creatinine, liver function tests, thyroid function tests, HbA1c, fasting lipids, C-reactive protein, D-dimer (if PE suspected and pre-test probability assessed), arterial blood gas (if acute respiratory compromise)

Imaging:
Chest X-ray (PA and lateral), high-resolution CT chest (if interstitial lung disease or malignancy suspected), CT pulmonary angiogram (for PE), abdominal ultrasound, echocardiography (if cardiac failure or valvular disease suspected)

Specialist:
Spirometry with bronchodilator reversibility (COPD/asthma distinction), bronchoscopy (haemoptysis workup), cardiac stress testing, specialist physician or respiratory physician review

Note: In counselling stations, investigation planning is not the primary task — the diagnosis is established in the stem. Investigation content is most relevant to history taking and diagnostic formulation stations.

Management

Acute Management (relevant to emergency or urgent presentations identified in any station):
- Secure airway, establish intravenous access, apply supplemental oxygen as indicated
- Continuous cardiac monitoring and pulse oximetry
- Immediate escalation to senior medical officer or emergency physician
- Activate relevant clinical pathway (e.g., ACS pathway, sepsis protocol)

Chronic Disease Management (relevant to counselling stations):
- Establish the diagnosis clearly and confirm the patient's understanding
- Discuss non-pharmacological interventions first (lifestyle modification, smoking cessation, dietary change, weight management, exercise)
- Introduce pharmacological therapy with specific agent names, doses where appropriate, and clear indication for each agent
- Address adherence barriers proactively
- Arrange appropriate allied health involvement: dietitian, physiotherapist, diabetes educator, pulmonary rehabilitation programme, smoking cessation counsellor
- Identify and refer to relevant specialist services in accordance with Australian referral pathways
- Establish a shared management plan with the patient's active involvement and documented consent

Preventive Care (integrated into management of chronic conditions):
- Ensure age-appropriate vaccinations are current (influenza, pneumococcal, COVID-19)
- Screen for comorbidities appropriate to the presenting condition
- Provide written patient information resources where available

Communication and Patient Education

Explaining the Diagnosis:
Use the patient's own language and health literacy level as a guide. Introduce the diagnosis by name, provide a brief accessible explanation of the condition, and check understanding after each key piece of information using teach-back technique — "I want to make sure I've explained that clearly — can you tell me in your own words what you understand about what we've found?"

Addressing Patient Concerns:
The role player in a counselling station will have a scripted concern — a fear of cancer, a worry about medication side effects, a concern about work capacity, or a question about prognosis. The candidate must invite this concern explicitly and address it directly. Generic reassurance without specific engagement with the patient's stated concern will not satisfy the examiner.

Shared Decision Making in the Australian Context:
Present options rather than directives. Frame treatment choices with balanced information — "There are a few ways we can approach this. I'll explain each one and we can decide together what suits you best." Respect patient autonomy, including the patient's right to decline recommended treatment after being fully informed of the risks of that decision. Document the patient's informed choice.

Health Literacy Considerations:
Avoid medical jargon without explanation. When medical terms are necessary, define them immediately in plain language. Use visual aids or written summaries where appropriate. Confirm comprehension at regular intervals throughout the consultation.

Written Information and Community Resources:
Direct patients to appropriate Australian resources: Lung Foundation Australia for respiratory conditions, Diabetes Australia for diabetes management, Beyond Blue or Lifeline for mental health support. Provide a written summary of the management plan and key information discussed.

AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

  • Identify the Predominant Assessment Area from the stem and task instructions before entering the room. This single act of preparation determines your time allocation, your clinical priorities, and the structure of your performance. A candidate who enters a counselling station with a history-taking mindset will fail to complete the management and education tasks within eight minutes.

  • In diagnostic formulation Group A stations, treat the four-minute history as non-negotiable. Do not attempt to extend history time beyond the prompt bell. The examiner is instructed to redirect you to the next task. Practise completing a clinically adequate history in four minutes through timed drilling.

  • The physical examination request task requires specific, component-by-component questioning. Practise the verbal examination for each major system (respiratory, cardiovascular, abdominal, neurological) as a scripted, efficient dialogue. Time yourself. Fluency is a practised skill, not a natural talent.

  • Counselling stations reward clinical specificity above communication style. A candidate who communicates empathically but provides a vague or incorrect management plan will not pass. Know the Australian guideline-concordant management for every major chronic condition likely to appear in a counselling station.

  • The patient's hidden agenda in a counselling station is always present and always scored. Invite the patient's concerns explicitly and early — do not wait until you have delivered the entire management plan. ICE must be elicited, not assumed.

  • Referral to a specialist is never a complete management plan. It is an appropriate component of management for complex cases, but it must accompany — not replace — specific knowledge of the condition, its first-line management, and the reasoning behind specialist involvement.

  • In Group A diagnostic formulation stations, allocate a maximum of two minutes to the physical examination request task. Practise ending this task efficiently. A two-minute overrun on the physical examination task eliminates your opportunity to present a complete differential diagnosis list, which is a separately scored domain.

  • Telephone consultation stations require an explicit opening identifying yourself and confirming patient identity and safety. Begin every telephone station with: "This is [name], the medical officer at [location]. Am I speaking with [patient name]? Before we continue, I want to make sure you're safe — are you in a safe place to talk?" This demonstrates awareness of telehealth safety standards.

  • Equipment-based stations require pre-examination preparation. Know how to correctly operate an otoscope, ophthalmoscope, and perform urinalysis dipstick interpretation. These are basic clinical skills scored as discrete examination components.

  • Clinical reasoning must accompany every diagnosis. The AMC specifically introduced clinical reasoning assessment to identify candidates who are reciting memorised case recalls without genuine diagnostic understanding. For every diagnosis you name, be prepared to state: "I am considering this diagnosis because the patient presents with [symptom/sign], which is consistent with [pathophysiology/aetiology]."

  • Time management in the examination is a practised clinical skill. The eight-minute performance window is brief. Candidates who have not timed their consultations repeatedly in realistic practice conditions will not have calibrated their pace accurately. Every station type has a different internal time architecture that must be drilled, not assumed.

  • Patient-centred communication is not simply politeness — it is a scored clinical competency. In the Australian clinical context, the patient's preferences, values, and decisions are central to the management plan. Candidates trained in doctor-centred clinical cultures must actively practise shared decision-making frameworks in all consultation types, not only counselling stations.

Safety Net

For candidates preparing for the AMC Clinical Examination, the following safety net framework applies to clinical stations regardless of PAA:

When to Escalate During a Station:
If a role player presents with symptoms or behaviours suggesting acute deterioration — chest pain, shortness of breath, confusion, suicidal intent — the candidate must address the safety issue immediately and explicitly, regardless of the station's primary task. State clearly: "I am concerned about your safety right now. I am going to call for immediate assistance."

Safety Netting Instructions for Common Clinical Scenarios:

  • Respiratory presentations: "If you develop sudden worsening of breathlessness, you cough up blood, you develop chest pain, or you feel faint, please call 000 immediately or present to your nearest emergency department."
  • Cardiac presentations: "If you develop severe chest pain, especially spreading to your arm or jaw, or if you feel sweaty or faint, this is a medical emergency — call 000."
  • Mental health presentations: "If you have any thoughts of harming yourself or ending your life, please contact Lifeline on 13 11 14 at any time, or go directly to your nearest emergency department. You do not need to manage this alone."
  • Paediatric presentations: "If your child develops a high fever that does not respond to paracetamol, has difficulty breathing, becomes very drowsy, develops a rash, or you are concerned at any time, please present to your nearest emergency department immediately. Trust your instincts as a parent."

Follow-Up Timeline:
All clinical consultations must conclude with a specific follow-up plan — not "come back if you're worried" but "I would like to see you again in two weeks to review your blood results and how you are responding to the new medication."

Written Resources:
Direct patients to condition-specific Australian health information: healthdirect.gov.au for general health queries, and the relevant specialist consumer organisation (Lung Foundation Australia, Heart Foundation, Diabetes Australia, Beyond Blue) for condition-specific guidance.
