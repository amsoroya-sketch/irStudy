# Upper Abdominal Pain — Peptic Ulcer Disease History Taking Station

## Clinical Presentation

A 32-year-old male truck driver presents to a general practice clinic with a complaint of upper abdominal pain. The pain is situated in the left upper quadrant or epigastric region, is dull in character, and has a clear relationship to food ingestion. He has been self-medicating with over-the-counter antacids and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). The clinical picture is consistent with peptic ulcer disease (PUD), likely gastric ulcer given the aggravation of symptoms with eating, in the context of NSAID use.

This station is clinically significant for several reasons. Peptic ulcer disease remains prevalent in the Australian community, particularly among patients using NSAIDs, steroids, or bisphosphonates, and among those with *Helicobacter pylori* infection. The occupation as a truck driver may suggest heavy analgesic use for musculoskeletal pain and a lifestyle that includes irregular meals, high caffeine intake, smoking, or alcohol use — all recognised risk factors. The examiner expects the candidate to efficiently characterise the pain, identify risk factors, screen comprehensively for red flags (including upper gastrointestinal malignancy), and arrive at a structured differential diagnosis.

The challenge of this station lies in distinguishing peptic ulcer disease from gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD), pancreatitis, hepatobiliary pathology, and — critically — upper gastrointestinal malignancy. A well-structured, systematic history that moves fluidly through the relevant differential groups while maintaining a natural clinician-patient rapport will achieve the highest marks.

---

## Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

This is a **history taking station** with a primary assessment focus. The examiner's marking criteria weight the structure and sequence of history taking most heavily, alongside the accuracy and completeness of the differential diagnosis. Communication marks are awarded for rapport building, clear questioning, and appropriate closure.

The expected structure for this station is:

1. **Opening and rapport** — introduce yourself, confirm patient identity, confirm haemodynamic stability with the examiner, and invite an open-ended account of the presenting complaint
2. **ICE elicitation (early)** — acknowledge the patient's concerns and establish their ideas and expectations before launching into systematic questioning
3. **SOCRATES pain assessment** — characterise the pain fully before moving into differential-directed questioning
4. **Systematic differential-directed history** — three structured groups: (i) gastro-oesophageal reflux and peptic ulcer disease; (ii) pancreatitis; (iii) hepatobiliary disease; with additional screens for acute coronary syndrome and lower lobe pneumonia
5. **Red flag screen** — integrated throughout, with specific emphasis on features suggesting upper GI malignancy, haematemesis, melaena, and dysphagia
6. **Targeted risk factor history** — medications (NSAIDs, steroids, bisphosphonates), alcohol, smoking, dietary habits (coffee, spicy foods, fatty meals)
7. **Systematic review and closure** — past medical history, family history, allergies, social history, and a structured diagnostic summary with differential diagnosis

## Systematic Approach

### Opening and Early ICE Elicitation

Begin by introducing yourself, confirming the patient's name and date of birth, and briefly checking haemodynamic status with the examiner ("Is the patient haemodynamically stable?"). Proceed to seat yourself at the patient's level and open with a broad, non-leading invitation:

> *"How can I help you today?"* or *"Tell me what has been worrying you."*

Allow the patient to describe the presenting complaint in their own words without interruption. Once an initial account is established, acknowledge the patient's distress and incorporate early ICE:

> *"I'm sorry to hear you've been having this pain. I can understand that must be quite concerning. Could you tell me — what do you think might be causing this discomfort? Is there something in particular you were worried about?"*

Early ICE signals empathy, builds therapeutic rapport, and provides crucial diagnostic information. A patient who volunteers fear of cancer ("My father had stomach cancer") fundamentally changes the priority of your red flag questioning.

---

### SOCRATES Pain Assessment

Systematic characterisation of the pain is mandatory and must be completed before moving to differential-directed questions.

**Site**
Ask the patient to point to where the pain is felt. In this scenario, the patient indicates the left upper quadrant or epigastric region. Note that epigastric pain is the most common site for both peptic ulcer disease and GORD; left upper quadrant pain raises the additional possibility of splenic pathology or gastric disease.

> *"Can you point to exactly where you feel the pain?"*

**Onset**
Determine when the pain first occurred, whether it has been present before, and the mode of onset (sudden versus gradual).

> *"When did this pain first start? Have you had anything like this before?"*
> *"Did it come on suddenly or gradually?"*

**Character**
The quality of the pain carries significant diagnostic weight in this station. A **dull ache** is more characteristic of peptic ulcer disease or gastric cancer. A **burning** quality is more typical of GORD or oesophagitis. A **severe, boring, constant pain** radiating to the back suggests pancreatitis. Elicit this carefully:

> *"How would you describe the pain? Is it sharp, dull, burning, crampy, or like a pressure?"*

**Radiation**
Enquire about radiation systematically. Peptic ulcer disease typically does not radiate. Pancreatitis classically radiates to the back. Biliary colic radiates to the right shoulder tip. In this case, there is no radiation.

> *"Does the pain move or travel anywhere — to your back, shoulder, or chest?"*

**Associations**
Ask about associated symptoms relevant to the upper GI system:

- Nausea and vomiting (and crucially in this station — **haematemesis**)
- Bloating and early satiety
- Bowel habit changes
- Fever and systemic symptoms
- Jaundice, dark urine, pale stools (hepatobiliary)
- Dysphagia (oesophageal pathology, including malignancy)
- Chest pain and shortness of breath (ACS, pneumonia)

**Timing**
Determine the duration, frequency, and pattern of the pain.

> *"How long have you had this pain? Is it there all the time or does it come and go? Is it getting better, worse, or staying the same over time?"*

**Exacerbating and Relieving Factors**
This is the **most diagnostically discriminating** part of the SOCRATES assessment in this scenario. The patient reports that **eating makes the pain worse**. When a patient identifies a relationship between food and pain, pursue this with two critical follow-up questions:

1. *"Is there a specific type of food that seems to bring it on — spicy foods, fatty foods, dairy products, or is it any food in general?"*
2. *"How soon after eating does the pain start?"*

The timing of post-prandial pain differentiates gastric ulcer from duodenal ulcer:
- **Gastric ulcer**: pain typically occurs **immediately or shortly after eating** (food stimulates acid secretion directly onto the ulcer)
- **Duodenal ulcer**: pain typically occurs **1–3 hours after eating** (when the stomach empties acid into the duodenum) and may be relieved by eating ("hunger pain")

Additional exacerbating factor of clinical importance: **nocturnal pain** — waking the patient from sleep with epigastric pain is a classic feature of peptic ulcer disease (particularly duodenal ulcer) and should be asked regardless of the patient's spontaneous report.

> *"Does this pain ever wake you up from sleep at night?"*

Enquire also about positional relief: leaning forward relieves the pain of pancreatitis (a specific and important manoeuvre to ask about in any upper abdominal pain).

**Severity**
Use a validated numerical pain scale to quantify pain intensity.

> *"On a scale of zero to ten, where ten is the worst pain you have ever felt, how would you rate your pain right now?"*

### Differential-Directed Systematic Questioning

Once SOCRATES is complete, move systematically through three primary differential groups. The order can be adjusted based on the predominant site: for **left upper quadrant pain**, prioritise the gastro-oesophageal and peptic ulcer group first.

#### Group 1 — Red Flag Screen (Integrated Throughout)

Red flag questions should be woven naturally into the history rather than delivered as a disconnected list. They are mandatory in any upper abdominal pain history because upper GI malignancy must not be missed.

Specific red flags for this station:

- **Unintentional weight loss**: *"Have you noticed any unintended weight loss over the past few months?"*
- **Loss of appetite**: *"Has your appetite changed at all recently?"*
- **Haematemesis**: *"Have you ever vomited blood, or anything that looked like coffee grounds?"* — this is specific to proximal GI pathology and must be asked in all upper abdominal pain stations
- **Melaena**: *"Have you noticed your stools have been black, tarry, or foul-smelling?"* — melaena indicates upper GI bleeding proximal to the ligament of Treitz
- **Dysphagia**: *"Do you have any difficulty swallowing — either solid food or liquids?"* — raises the possibility of oesophageal or gastric cardia malignancy
- **Anaemia symptoms**: *"Have you felt unusually tired, short of breath, or noticed you look pale?"*
- **Abdominal mass**: *"Have you noticed any lumps or swellings in your abdomen?"*
- **Family history of GI malignancy**: *"Has anyone in your family had stomach cancer, bowel cancer, or any other cancers?"*

#### Group 2 — Gastro-Oesophageal Reflux Disease (GORD) and Peptic Ulcer Disease

Screen for GORD to separate it from PUD, as the two conditions frequently co-exist and share risk factors:

- **Heartburn**: *"Do you get a burning sensation rising from your stomach into your chest or throat after eating?"*
- **Regurgitation**: *"Do you ever get a bitter or acidic taste in your mouth, particularly after meals or when lying down?"*
- **Water brash**: *"Do you ever get a sudden flood of saliva into your mouth?"* — water brash is a pathognomonic feature of GORD
- **Worsening on lying down or bending forward**: these positional triggers are more characteristic of GORD than PUD

For **peptic ulcer disease** specifically, the crucial additional question beyond food-timing (as above) is:

- **Nocturnal pain**: *"Does the pain ever wake you from sleep?"* — this is highly specific for duodenal ulceration
- **Relief with antacids**: *"Does taking antacids help the pain at all?"*

#### Group 3 — Pancreatitis and Pancreatic Pathology

Questions directed at excluding pancreatitis (acute, chronic, and pancreatic malignancy):

- **Back radiation**: already addressed under SOCRATES
- **Positional relief**: *"Does leaning forward or sitting up make the pain better at all?"* — classic for pancreatitis
- **Nausea and vomiting**: typically severe and persistent in acute pancreatitis
- **Alcohol use** (primary risk factor): *"Do you drink alcohol? How often, and how much would you typically drink in a week?"* — quantify in standard drinks
- **Previous biliary or pancreatic disease**: *"Have you ever been told you have gallstones or had problems with your pancreas before?"*
- **Elevated blood fats (hypertriglyceridaemia)**: *"Have you ever been told your blood fats or cholesterol is high?"*
- **Recent abdominal trauma**: *"Have you had any recent injuries or blows to your abdomen?"*
- **Recent infection or viral illness**: *"Have you had any viral illness recently, including flu-like symptoms, fever, or a recent mumps-type illness?"* — viral causes (especially mumps) are relevant in a young patient

#### Group 4 — Hepatobiliary Pathology

Screen for conditions of the liver and gallbladder:

- **Jaundice**: *"Have you noticed any yellowing of your skin or the whites of your eyes?"*
- **Dark urine and pale stools**: *"Has your urine been darker than usual — like tea or cola coloured? Have your stools been pale or clay-coloured?"*
- **Fatty meal-triggered pain**: *"Have you ever noticed pain coming on after eating a particularly large or fatty meal, possibly with nausea or vomiting?"* — classic biliary colic
- **Right upper quadrant localisation**: clarify if pain has ever localised to the right, particularly with radiation to the right shoulder tip
- **Risk factors for hepatitis**: alcohol use (already asked), intravenous drug use, tattoos, blood transfusions, travel, and sexual history where clinically relevant
- **Previous gallstone history**: *"Have you ever been told you have gallstones or had an ultrasound of your abdomen?"*

#### Group 5 — Atypical Presentations (ACS and Lower Lobe Pneumonia)

In any upper abdominal pain, complete the history by excluding atypical presentations of important extra-abdominal conditions:

- **Acute coronary syndrome**: *"Do you have any chest tightness, pain, or pressure in your chest? Any pain radiating to your arm or jaw? Any palpitations or shortness of breath?"*
- **Lower lobe pneumonia**: *"Any cough, shortness of breath, fever, or pleuritic chest pain (pain that worsens when you take a deep breath)?"*

### Risk Factor History — Targeted and Comprehensive

Risk factor assessment for peptic ulcer disease in this clinical context includes:

**Medications (highest yield in this scenario):**
Ask in an open-ended manner first: *"Are you taking any medications, prescribed or over-the-counter?"*

Medications causing gastric mucosal injury include:
- **NSAIDs** (ibuprofen — sold as Nurofen in Australian pharmacies; naproxen; diclofenac): the most common pharmacological cause of PUD in Australia; the patient in this scenario uses Nurofen
- **Aspirin** (including low-dose aspirin)
- **Corticosteroids** (particularly when combined with NSAIDs)
- **Bisphosphonates** (alendronate): cause oesophageal and gastric ulceration
- **Anticoagulants** (warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants): increase bleeding risk from existing ulcers

Over-the-counter antacid preparations commonly encountered in Australian practice include Mylanta (aluminium/magnesium hydroxide), Quick-Eze (calcium carbonate), and Gaviscon (alginate-antacid). Patient self-medication with these agents is an important clue to the duration and nature of upper GI symptoms. The fact that symptoms are no longer controlled by antacids alone, or that the patient has progressed to using NSAIDs for associated discomfort, signals worsening disease.

**Dietary factors:** caffeine (coffee, energy drinks), spicy foods, irregular meal patterns — all relevant in a truck driver with an erratic dietary schedule

**Alcohol:** quantify using standard Australian drink measures; chronic heavy use is a risk factor for both PUD and pancreatitis

**Tobacco smoking:** *Helicobacter pylori* infection rates are higher in smokers; smoking impairs gastric mucosal healing

**Illicit drug use:** cocaine use can cause mesenteric ischaemia; methamphetamine use is associated with gastric complications

**Past medical history:** previous episodes of peptic ulcer disease, previous *H. pylori* eradication therapy, previous upper GI endoscopy, liver disease, renal disease (NSAIDs are nephrotoxic)

**Family history:** gastric cancer, colorectal cancer, familial adenomatous polyposis, Lynch syndrome, *H. pylori* positivity in household contacts

---

### Closure — Diagnostic Summary to the Patient

After completing the history, deliver a structured summary to the patient and present your differential diagnosis clearly and empathetically:

> *"James, after talking with you, I believe the most likely cause of your pain is a condition called peptic ulcer disease — essentially an ulcer, or a sore, on the lining of your stomach. The pain you're experiencing after eating, its dull character, and importantly the fact that you have been taking ibuprofen-containing pain relief, all point towards this diagnosis. Ibuprofen is very effective for pain but it has a well-known side effect of causing irritation and ulcers in the stomach lining.*
>
> *There are other conditions I want to make sure we exclude. Whilst I think this is most likely an ulcer, I also want to rule out any possibility of infection or inflammation of the pancreas, any problems with your gallbladder, and importantly, I want to arrange some investigations to make sure there is nothing more serious, such as any changes in the stomach lining that we would need to investigate further."*

## Differential Diagnosis

| Category | Diagnosis | Key Distinguishing Features |
|----------|-----------|----------------------------|
| **Common** | Peptic ulcer disease (gastric ulcer) | Dull epigastric or LUQ pain, worse immediately after eating, NSAID or *H. pylori* risk factors, antacid use, possible nocturnal pain |
| **Common** | Peptic ulcer disease (duodenal ulcer) | Epigastric pain 1–3 hours post-prandially, nocturnal pain, hunger pain relieved by food or antacids |
| **Common** | Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) | Burning retrosternal or epigastric pain, worsened by lying down, heartburn, regurgitation, water brash, no nocturnal awakening with epigastric pain |
| **Common** | Gastritis (acute or chronic) | Diffuse epigastric discomfort, nausea, associated with NSAID use, alcohol, or *H. pylori*; distinguished from PUD by endoscopy |
| **Common** | Functional dyspepsia | Epigastric discomfort, bloating, early satiety; diagnosis of exclusion; no structural lesion on endoscopy |
| **Important** | Acute pancreatitis | Severe constant epigastric pain radiating to the back, relieved by leaning forward, associated with nausea and vomiting, elevated lipase; alcohol or gallstone aetiology |
| **Important** | Chronic pancreatitis | Recurrent epigastric pain, steatorrhoea, weight loss, diabetes in established disease; alcohol history |
| **Important** | Acute cholecystitis / biliary colic | RUQ pain radiating to right shoulder tip, aggravated by fatty meals, fever and Murphy's sign in cholecystitis; normal between attacks in biliary colic |
| **Important** | Hepatitis (alcoholic, viral, autoimmune) | RUQ discomfort, jaundice, elevated transaminases, malaise, anorexia; risk factor history crucial |
| **Must Not Miss** | Gastric adenocarcinoma | Persistent dull epigastric pain, unintentional weight loss, anorexia, dysphagia, haematemesis or melaena, anaemia, epigastric mass on examination; risk factors: *H. pylori*, smoking, family history, age >45 years |
| **Must Not Miss** | Pancreatic adenocarcinoma | Painless jaundice (head of pancreas) or severe constant back pain (body/tail), weight loss, new-onset diabetes, Courvoisier's sign; typically older patients but must be considered |
| **Must Not Miss** | Oesophageal carcinoma | Progressive dysphagia (solids then liquids), weight loss, odynophagia, haematemesis; risk factors include Barrett's oesophagus, smoking, alcohol |
| **Must Not Miss** | Acute coronary syndrome | Upper abdominal pain may be sole presentation, particularly inferior MI; associated diaphoresis, nausea, radiation to jaw/left arm; ECG mandatory in any atypical upper abdominal pain |
| **Must Not Miss** | Ruptured or leaking aortic aneurysm | Severe tearing abdominal or back pain, haemodynamic instability, pulsatile abdominal mass; rare at age 32 but critical to consider in acute presentations |

## Red Flags — Must Not Miss

- **Haematemesis (vomiting blood or coffee-ground material)** — indicates active upper GI bleeding, most commonly from a peptic ulcer, Mallory-Weiss tear, or upper GI malignancy; requires immediate resuscitation, IV access, urgent endoscopy, and specialist referral; do not discharge without investigation

- **Melaena (black, tarry, foul-smelling stools)** — signifies upper GI bleeding proximal to the ligament of Treitz; melaena from as little as 50–100 mL of blood in the upper GI tract; requires urgent investigation and inpatient assessment

- **Dysphagia (difficulty swallowing)** — any new dysphagia in the context of upper GI symptoms must be investigated to exclude oesophageal or gastric cardia malignancy; progressive dysphagia (solids first, then liquids) is particularly sinister

- **Unintentional weight loss** — any patient with upper abdominal pain and unexplained weight loss requires urgent upper GI endoscopy to exclude malignancy; weight loss >5% of body weight over 6 months is clinically significant

- **Persistent vomiting** — raises concern for gastric outlet obstruction secondary to a large ulcer or pyloric carcinoma; dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, and malnutrition may result

- **Anaemia** — iron deficiency anaemia in a patient with upper GI symptoms strongly suggests chronic blood loss from ulcer disease or malignancy; warrants endoscopic investigation

- **Nocturnal pain awakening from sleep** — whilst classic for duodenal ulcer, this symptom in a middle-aged or older patient with other red flags warrants endoscopy to exclude malignancy

- **Palpable epigastric or abdominal mass** — any abdominal mass in the context of upper GI symptoms is malignant until proven otherwise; urgent investigation required

- **Jaundice** — in the context of upper abdominal pain, suggests biliary obstruction (choledocholithiasis, cholangiocarcinoma, pancreatic head cancer) or hepatitis; requires urgent hepatobiliary workup

- **Family history of gastric cancer or hereditary GI syndromes** — increases prior probability of malignancy; lower threshold for endoscopy even in younger patients

- **Rapidly progressing or new-onset severe symptoms** — any acute deterioration, haemodynamic instability, or signs of peritonism (rigidity, guarding, rebound tenderness) indicates a surgical emergency; perforation of a peptic ulcer presents with sudden onset severe abdominal pain, peritonism, and free air on erect chest X-ray

## Investigations

**Bedside:**
- Vital signs (blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, temperature, oxygen saturation) — haemodynamic stability assessment
- ECG — mandatory to exclude inferior myocardial infarction presenting as epigastric pain
- Urine dipstick — screen for urinary tract pathology, bilirubin
- Stool guaiac (faecal occult blood test) — screen for occult upper or lower GI bleeding
- Fingerprick blood glucose — hyperglycaemia may suggest pancreatitis or new-onset diabetes in pancreatic disease
- Urinary pregnancy test in women of reproductive age — exclude ectopic pregnancy before imaging

**Bloods:**
- Full blood count (FBC) — anaemia (chronic blood loss), leucocytosis (infection/inflammation)
- C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) — inflammatory markers
- Urea and electrolytes (U&E) — elevated urea with normal creatinine ("uraemia") is a classic marker of upper GI haemorrhage
- Liver function tests (LFTs) — hepatitis, biliary obstruction, hepatocellular carcinoma
- Lipase (or amylase) — pancreatitis; lipase is more specific and remains elevated longer
- Serum calcium — hypercalcaemia is a risk factor for pancreatitis; calcium-secreting parathyroid tumours
- Serum triglycerides — hypertriglyceridaemia as a cause of pancreatitis
- Iron studies — iron deficiency anaemia suggesting chronic blood loss
- Coagulation studies — if bleeding is suspected or anticoagulants are used
- *Helicobacter pylori* serology — however, serology cannot distinguish active from past infection; a positive result requires confirmation with a urea breath test or stool antigen test
- Urea breath test (*H. pylori*) — non-invasive, accurate, preferred in primary care to confirm active infection before and after treatment

**Imaging:**
- Erect chest X-ray — free air under the diaphragm indicates perforated viscus (peptic ulcer perforation); also screens for lower lobe pneumonia
- Abdominal X-ray — limited value; may show dilated loops suggesting obstruction or ileus
- Ultrasound abdomen — first-line imaging for hepatobiliary assessment; detects gallstones, biliary dilatation, liver lesions, and free fluid; cannot reliably image the pancreas due to overlying bowel gas
- CT abdomen and pelvis with contrast — gold standard for pancreatitis staging (CT Severity Index), detecting pancreatic or gastric malignancy, and investigating any acute deterioration
- CT pulmonary angiography — if pulmonary embolism is suspected

**Specialist:**
- Upper GI endoscopy (OGD — oesophago-gastro-duodenoscopy) — gold standard investigation for peptic ulcer disease; allows direct visualisation, biopsy for *H. pylori* and histology (to exclude malignancy), and therapeutic intervention (haemostasis); **mandatory for any gastric ulcer** due to malignant potential
- Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) — superior to CT for detecting small pancreatic lesions and assessing biliary pathology
- ERCP (endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography) — both diagnostic and therapeutic for biliary obstruction, choledocholithiasis, or biliary strictures
- Hepatology or gastroenterology referral — for suspected hepatitis, cirrhosis, or malignancy
- Upper GI surgery referral — for suspected malignancy, perforation, obstruction, or refractory ulcer disease

## Management

### Immediate Management

- Confirm haemodynamic stability; if the patient is haemodynamically compromised (haematemesis, melaena, hypotension, tachycardia), activate hospital protocol: two large-bore IV cannulae, IV fluid resuscitation, crossmatch blood, urgent gastroenterology/surgical review, and urgent endoscopy
- Cease the causative agent: **stop NSAIDs immediately** and discuss alternative analgesia (paracetamol is preferred for musculoskeletal pain; consider referral to physiotherapy or rheumatology for ongoing pain management)
- Initiate empirical acid suppression pending investigation

### Pharmacological Management

**Proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy:**
- High-dose PPI is the cornerstone of management: omeprazole 20–40 mg daily (or pantoprazole, esomeprazole, or rabeprazole at equivalent doses)
- Duration: 4–8 weeks for gastric ulcer; 4 weeks for duodenal ulcer
- All gastric ulcers require **repeat endoscopy at 6–8 weeks** to confirm healing and to obtain biopsies if not already done — malignancy cannot be excluded endoscopically without biopsy and must always be followed up

***Helicobacter pylori* eradication therapy (if confirmed):**
Standard first-line quadruple therapy in Australia (following AMC/GESA guidelines):
- **Bismuth quadruple therapy** (14 days): bismuth subcitrate + metronidazole + tetracycline + PPI — preferred in areas of clarithromycin resistance
- **Concomitant quadruple therapy** (14 days): PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin + metronidazole — may be used in settings of known low clarithromycin resistance
- Confirm eradication with urea breath test or stool antigen test **at least 4 weeks after completing treatment** and at least 2 weeks after ceasing PPI

**Antacid and alginate preparations** (Mylanta, Gaviscon, Quick-Eze): symptom relief only; not adequate monotherapy for peptic ulcer disease; advise the patient that these do not heal ulcers

**Analgesia:** paracetamol (1 g four times daily) is safe and appropriate for musculoskeletal pain; **avoid NSAIDs, aspirin, and COX-2 inhibitors** in PUD; if a COX-2 inhibitor is absolutely necessary (e.g., severe inflammatory arthritis), co-prescribe PPI

**Sucralfate** — mucosal cytoprotective agent; occasional use as adjunct in duodenal ulcer disease; limited evidence for superiority over PPI monotherapy

### Lifestyle Modification

- Abstain from or significantly reduce alcohol intake
- Cease smoking (smoking impairs ulcer healing and increases recurrence rates)
- Reduce or eliminate caffeine and spicy foods
- Establish regular, structured meal times where possible (particularly relevant for truck drivers with irregular schedules)
- Elevate head of bed if GORD symptoms are prominent

### Follow-Up

- Review in 2–4 weeks to assess symptom response to PPI and assess compliance with cessation of NSAIDs
- Arrange upper GI endoscopy (OGD) within 6–8 weeks if not already performed — mandatory for gastric ulcer to exclude malignancy
- Confirm *H. pylori* eradication at least 4 weeks post-treatment
- Long-term PPI therapy and gastroprotection should be discussed if the patient requires ongoing NSAID therapy (e.g., concurrent PPI with an NSAID is the recommended approach if NSAID cessation is not possible)

## Communication and Patient Education

### Explaining the Diagnosis

Use simple, jargon-free language. Avoid terms such as "mucosal erosion" or "gastric acid hypersecretion" without explanation. A useful framework:

> *"James, you have what we call a peptic ulcer — this is essentially a sore or raw area on the lining of your stomach. Think of the lining of your stomach like the skin inside your mouth. When it gets damaged, it becomes sore, particularly when it comes into contact with stomach acid — and that's why eating makes it worse, because eating triggers your stomach to produce more acid.*
>
> *The most likely reason this has happened is the pain relief you have been taking — Nurofen contains a medication called ibuprofen, which is very effective but has a well-known side effect of damaging the stomach lining, particularly if taken regularly. The good news is that this is very treatable. We will stop the ibuprofen, start a medication to reduce the acid in your stomach, and we will also test you for a bacterial infection called Helicobacter pylori, which is a common additional cause of these ulcers and is easily treated with a short course of antibiotics."*

### Addressing Common Patient Concerns

- **"Is it cancer?"** — acknowledge the concern directly and honestly: *"That is an important concern to address. At the moment, the pattern of your symptoms points most strongly towards an ulcer rather than cancer. However, one of the important reasons I want to arrange an endoscopy is to look directly at the stomach lining, take a small tissue sample, and make absolutely certain. We do not leave gastric ulcers without a formal look."*
- **"Do I need to stop working?"** — in uncomplicated PUD, there is no requirement to cease work; however, lifestyle modifications regarding meal times, caffeine, and stress are relevant
- **"How long will it take to get better?"** — most peptic ulcers heal within 4–8 weeks of appropriate treatment; symptoms typically improve within 1–2 weeks of commencing PPI
- **"Can I take anything for pain in the meantime?"** — paracetamol is safe; explicitly advise against any NSAID-containing products (Nurofen, Voltaren, Naprogesic)

### Written and Patient Resources

- Provide written information about peptic ulcer disease (available from the Gastroenterological Society of Australia at gesa.org.au)
- Provide a written medication schedule for *H. pylori* eradication therapy if prescribed — compliance with the full 14-day course is critical
- Provide emergency contact information and clear return precautions (see Safety Net below)

## AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

- **Structure is the primary determinant of your mark in this station.** The examiner is assessing whether your history follows a logical, systematic sequence. Practise moving fluently from SOCRATES → red flags → differential-directed groups → risk factors → closure, without appearing to read from a mental checklist.

- **The food-pain relationship is the diagnostic pivot of this case.** When the patient confirms that eating worsens the pain, do not simply record this and move on. Probe with two mandatory follow-up questions: (1) Is it specific food or all food? (2) How soon after eating does the pain start? The timing of post-prandial pain differentiates gastric from duodenal ulcer and may be specifically tested.

- **Haematemesis and melaena are non-negotiable red flag questions in any upper GI station.** Because the bleeding source is proximal, these questions replace or supplement the rectal bleeding question used in lower GI stations. A candidate who fails to ask about haematemesis in an upper abdominal pain station demonstrates a critical gap in clinical reasoning.

- **Ask about nocturnal pain in every peptic ulcer disease history.** Waking from sleep with epigastric pain is a classic feature of PUD and scores marks for candidate completeness.

- **Dysphagia must be asked in this station.** The candidate who asks about dysphagia demonstrates awareness that the differential includes oesophageal and gastric cardia malignancy — this is a specific, high-yield question that separates good candidates from excellent ones.

- **Medications are the most important risk factor to elicit in this scenario.** The patient is taking Nurofen (ibuprofen) and Quick-Eze (antacid). Nurofen is a commonly used brand name in Australia that candidates must recognise as an NSAID. Quick-Eze is a calcium carbonate antacid. The AMC has historically used Australian brand names (Mylanta, Gaviscon, Nurofen, Panadol) in scenario scripts — know the active ingredients behind common Australian OTC products.

- **Gastric ulcers require endoscopy regardless of response to treatment.** Unlike duodenal ulcers (which do not undergo malignant change), gastric ulcers carry a small but real risk of malignancy. Always mention endoscopy as mandatory follow-up for a gastric ulcer when presenting your management plan. Failure to include this may indicate incomplete clinical knowledge.

- **Do not conflate GORD and PUD in your differential.** Many candidates group these as "acid-related diseases" and fail to differentiate them. Heartburn, regurgitation, and water brash point to GORD; post-prandial pain, nocturnal pain, and NSAID use point to PUD. These are distinct conditions with different endoscopic findings and different management priorities.

- **Lean forward test for pancreatitis.** Always ask whether leaning forward relieves the pain when pancreatitis is in the differential. This is a highly specific question that examiners look for because it demonstrates familiarity with pancreatitis semiology.

- **Integrate ACS and lower lobe pneumonia at the end of the history.** These atypical presentations of extra-abdominal disease must be excluded in any upper abdominal pain. Requesting an ECG and chest X-ray at the investigation stage further reinforces this awareness.

- **Deliver your differential diagnosis in structured groups.** When presenting differentials to the examiner, group them by organ system (gastro-oesophageal/peptic; pancreatic; hepatobiliary; cardiovascular/thoracic) rather than listing them randomly. This demonstrates systematic clinical thinking and impresses examiners.

- **Do not over-diagnose.** With a 32-year-old male, the most likely diagnosis is indeed peptic ulcer disease. Whilst malignancy must be mentioned as a "must not miss," presenting it as your primary diagnosis without adequate supporting clinical features will cost communication marks. Calibrate the probability of your differentials to the clinical picture presented.

## Safety Net

Provide clear, explicit verbal and written safety net instructions to the patient before discharge from the consultation.

**Advise the patient to present immediately to the Emergency Department if any of the following occur:**

- Vomiting blood or material that resembles coffee grounds
- Black, tarry, or foul-smelling stools (melaena)
- Sudden severe worsening of abdominal pain, particularly if the abdomen becomes rigid or board-like (may indicate perforation)
- Dizziness, light-headedness, near-fainting, or rapid heart rate (suggesting haemorrhage)
- High fever with worsening abdominal pain

**Advise the patient to return to the GP urgently (within 24–48 hours) if:**

- Symptoms are not improving within 48–72 hours of commencing PPI
- New symptoms develop (jaundice, dysphagia, significant weight loss)
- Antacids are no longer providing any relief whatsoever

**Routine follow-up:**
- Review appointment in 2–4 weeks to assess symptomatic response to PPI
- Upper GI endoscopy appointment: arrange within 6–8 weeks
- *H. pylori* confirmation of eradication: urea breath test to be arranged 4 weeks after completing eradication therapy

**Patient instructions to take home:**
- Written prescription for PPI with clear dosing instructions
- Written list of medications to avoid (all NSAIDs, aspirin unless prescribed for cardiovascular indications)
- Written information sheet on peptic ulcer disease
- Emergency contact details for after-hours GP or nearest emergency department
