# Mittelschmerz — Right-Sided Abdominal Pain in a Young Woman: Communication and Counselling Station

## Clinical Presentation

A 24-year-old woman presents to a general practice or emergency department setting with right-sided abdominal pain. The AMC examiner has deliberately used the broad descriptor "right-sided" rather than specifying right upper quadrant or right lower quadrant, a design choice that has significant clinical implications. Candidates who wait passively for anatomical clarification before committing to a differential list miss the point entirely; the correct approach is to commence a structured, open-ended history that spans all anatomical compartments of the right abdomen before refining the differential as the history unfolds.

This station examines the candidate's ability to simultaneously hold multiple diagnostic possibilities, adapt the differential in real time as history and investigation results become available, and then communicate a diagnosis of mittelschmerz clearly and sensitively to a young woman who may present with specific concerns — either about gallbladder disease (as in her mother's surgical history) or about appendicitis. The communication task requires explaining a physiological but painful gynaecological phenomenon in plain language, addressing patient concerns directly with evidence-based reasoning, providing appropriate management advice, and delivering a structured safety net.

The station is clinically important for AMC candidates because mittelschmerz is frequently encountered in general practice and emergency settings, is easily dismissed or misdiagnosed, and represents an excellent test of a candidate's ability to integrate gynaecological, gastrointestinal, and urological history-taking into a coherent diagnostic framework. The incidental finding of asymptomatic cholelithiasis on ultrasound adds a layer of complexity that tests a candidate's ability to communicate incidental findings accurately without causing unnecessary alarm.

## Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

This station combines history-taking with communication and counselling elements. The examiner expects a structured, patient-centred encounter that moves through five sequential phases without rigid compartmentalisation.

**Phase 1 — Opening and Haemodynamic Stability Screen:** Before any detailed history, the candidate must establish that the patient is haemodynamically stable. A brief, clinically motivated screen for signs of haemodynamic compromise (pallor, diaphoresis, severe constant pain, syncopal episodes) determines the urgency of the encounter and rules out haemoperitoneum from ectopic rupture or ovarian cyst rupture requiring immediate escalation.

**Phase 2 — Open-Ended Elicitation and ICE Framework:** The candidate invites the patient to describe her pain in her own words before applying systematic questioning. Critically, the patient's Ideas, Concerns, and Expectations (ICE) must be actively elicited early, as they shape the entire communication task. In this station, the patient volunteers a specific concern — either gallbladder disease or appendicitis — and this concern must be formally acknowledged and explicitly addressed later in the encounter.

**Phase 3 — Systematic History Across All Relevant Systems:** A full pain history (SOCRATES) is followed by systematic questioning across gastrointestinal, renal/urological, and gynaecological systems, without excluding hepatobiliary questions simply because the patient localises her pain to the right lower quadrant.

**Phase 4 — Information Giving and Diagnosis Explanation:** After reviewing the investigation results, the candidate explains the findings in plain language, establishes the diagnosis of mittelschmerz, addresses the patient's specific concern directly, and provides a brief explanation of incidental findings.

**Phase 5 — Management, Counselling, and Safety Netting:** The candidate provides a clear, practical management plan, delivers tailored safety net instructions with specific red flags, and discusses longer-term options where relevant.

## Systematic Approach

### SOCRATES Pain Assessment

A structured pain history forms the foundation of the clinical encounter. The candidate must address the following domains:

**Site:** Begin with an open question — *"Can you show me exactly where you feel the pain?"* — to allow the patient to localise it herself. In this case she confirms right lower quadrant, but the candidate should not have pre-empted this.

**Onset:** *"When did the pain start? Did it come on suddenly or gradually?"* Mittelschmerz typically begins abruptly, lasting from a few minutes to several hours.

**Character:** *"What does the pain feel like — is it sharp, crampy, dull, or pressure-like?"* Mittelschmerz is characteristically mild to moderate, sharp or crampy, self-limiting.

**Radiation:** *"Does the pain spread anywhere — to your back, shoulder, groin, or inner thigh?"* Shoulder tip radiation would raise concern for diaphragmatic irritation from free intraperitoneal fluid or haemoperitoneum.

**Associations:** *"Do you have any nausea, vomiting, fever, diarrhoea, bloating, or changes in your bowel habits?"* The absence of these features helps distinguish mittelschmerz from appendicitis, biliary colic, and pelvic inflammatory disease.

**Timing:** *"Has this happened before? When exactly did it start — can you relate it to your last period?"* The relationship to day 14 of the menstrual cycle is the single most diagnostically significant feature.

**Exacerbating and Relieving Factors:** *"Does anything make it better or worse? Does it settle with rest or simple pain relief like paracetamol?"* Mittelschmerz typically resolves with rest and simple analgesia.

**Severity:** *"On a scale of zero to ten, how severe is the pain at its worst?"* Mittelschmerz is generally mild to moderate; severe pain warrants further investigation for complications.

### Systematic Differential History — Multi-System Enquiry

The candidate must screen all three major compartments systematically, irrespective of the patient's initial localisation of pain.

**Gynaecological and Reproductive History:**
- Last normal menstrual period: *"When was the first day of your last period?"* — In this case, exactly 14 days prior, consistent with mid-cycle timing.
- Menstrual cycle regularity and length: *"Are your periods regular? How long is your usual cycle?"*
- Sexual activity and contraception: *"Are you currently sexually active? Are you using any contraception?"* — This determines pregnancy risk.
- Pregnancy test: A urine or serum beta-hCG is mandatory in any woman of reproductive age with lower abdominal pain. Ectopic pregnancy must be excluded before a diagnosis of mittelschmerz is accepted.
- Vaginal discharge or bleeding: Discharge raises the possibility of pelvic inflammatory disease; abnormal bleeding may suggest a complication of pregnancy.
- Previous similar episodes: Recurrent mid-cycle pain in prior cycles supports mittelschmerz as a pattern diagnosis.
- Dyspareunia or dysmenorrhoea: Endometriosis may present similarly.

**Gastrointestinal History:**
- Anorexia, nausea, vomiting — cardinal features of appendicitis; their absence is reassuring.
- Migration of pain from periumbilical to right iliac fossa — Klassic Kocher's sign of appendicitis; its absence is clinically significant.
- Change in bowel habit — diarrhoea suggests infectious gastroenteritis or inflammatory bowel disease; constipation may accompany appendicitis or pelvic mass.
- Rectal bleeding or mucus — raises concern for inflammatory bowel disease.

**Renal and Urological History:**
- Dysuria, frequency, haematuria — ureteric calculus and urinary tract infection both cause right-sided pain.
- Loin-to-groin radiation — characteristic of ureteric colic.
- Fever with urinary symptoms — raises concern for pyelonephritis.

**Hepatobiliary History (Do Not Omit):**
The candidate must ask about hepatobiliary symptoms even after the patient localises to the right lower quadrant. The clinical rationale is twofold: gallbladder pain can radiate across the right abdomen, and — critically — if the patient volunteers a concern about gallbladder disease based on a family history, the candidate must be equipped to confirm or refute gallbladder pathology based on targeted questions asked during the history. Key questions include:
- *"Do you have any pain in the right upper abdomen, particularly after fatty meals?"*
- *"Have you noticed any yellowing of your skin or eyes, dark urine, or pale stools?"*
- *"Have you had any nausea or vomiting specifically related to eating?"*

**General and Systemic History:**
- Fever or rigors — infection or peritonitis
- Weight loss — malignancy (rare in this age group but must be screened)
- Past medical and surgical history — prior appendicectomy (modifies the differential), prior pelvic surgery, known endometriosis or ovarian cysts
- Medications — analgesia use, oral contraceptive pill use (may suppress mittelschmerz)
- Family history — as elicited spontaneously in this case, gallbladder disease in a first-degree relative

The ICE framework must be actively applied, not assumed. In this clinical scenario, the patient volunteers her concern spontaneously during the opening statement — either fear of gallbladder disease requiring surgery (as her mother experienced), or fear of appendicitis. The candidate must:

### Interpreting and Explaining the Ultrasound Findings

The ultrasound report in this station contains four elements, each requiring a brief, plain-language explanation:

**1. Ruptured follicle in the right ovary — the primary finding:**
Explain in lay terms before introducing medical terminology. A suitable explanation:

*"In each of your menstrual cycles, one of your ovaries releases an egg. Before it is released, the egg grows inside your ovary in a small fluid-filled sac that we call a follicle. Around day 14 of your cycle — which is exactly where you are now — this sac ruptures to release the egg. When it ruptures, a small amount of fluid and blood is released into your abdomen. This fluid irritates the lining of your abdomen, and that is what is causing your pain."*

This explanation builds the physiological scaffold needed for the patient to understand the diagnosis. The candidate should avoid terms such as "follicular rupture," "corpus luteum," or "haemoperitoneum" without first establishing the anatomical context.

**2. Fluid in the pouch of Douglas — an expected finding:**
*"The scan also shows a small amount of fluid in the space behind your uterus — that is the fluid I just described that was released when the follicle ruptured. This is expected and will be reabsorbed by your body over the next few days."*

**3. Incidental gallstones — no acute inflammation:**
*"The scan has also picked up some stones in your gallbladder. I want to be clear about this: these stones are not causing your pain at the moment. There is no inflammation of the gallbladder, the wall is not thickened, and the bile duct is not blocked. These are what we call incidental findings — they were found on the scan, but they are not what brought you in today."*

Address the patient's concern about gallbladder disease directly: *"I know you were worried about having the same problem as your mother. I can reassure you that today's pain is not coming from your gallbladder, and the stones we have found are not causing any problems right now."*

**4. Non-visualised appendix — a normal ultrasound finding:**
*"I should also mention that we were looking for your appendix on the scan, but we were not able to see it clearly. I want you to know that this is actually a very common finding — up to 80% of normal appendices cannot be seen clearly on ultrasound. It does not mean anything is wrong with your appendix."*

### Addressing the Appendicitis Concern

When the patient expresses concern about appendicitis, the candidate must provide specific, evidence-based reassurance rather than vague dismissal. The reasons appendicitis is clinically excluded in this case must be articulated clearly:

- **No migration of pain** — appendicitis characteristically begins with central or periumbilical pain that migrates to the right iliac fossa over hours. This patient's pain began directly in the right lower quadrant.
- **No anorexia** — loss of appetite is a cardinal feature of appendicitis and is absent here.
- **No nausea or vomiting** — present in the majority of appendicitis cases; absent here.
- **No fever** — appendiceal inflammation is accompanied by a low-grade fever; this patient is afebrile.
- **Temporal relationship to menstrual cycle** — the pain began precisely at day 14, which is the physiological window of ovulation.
- **Non-visualisation of the appendix on ultrasound is not diagnostic of appendicitis** — up to 50–85% of normal appendices are not visualised on ultrasound due to patient habitus, bowel gas, and operator factors. Non-visualisation in the absence of clinical features of appendicitis does not constitute a positive finding.

The Alvarado score, where relevant, can be referenced briefly: this patient scores low due to absence of fever, leukocytosis, and migratory pain, making appendicitis highly unlikely.

## Red Flags — Must Not Miss

- **Haemodynamic instability (tachycardia, hypotension, pallor, diaphoresis)** — Suggests haemoperitoneum from ruptured ectopic pregnancy, ruptured ovarian cyst, or severe torsion. Requires immediate resuscitation and urgent surgical review; do not proceed with routine history-taking.

- **Positive or equivocal urine or serum beta-hCG** — Any pregnancy must be presumed ectopic until proven otherwise in the context of unilateral pelvic pain. Requires urgent pelvic ultrasound and gynaecological review.

- **Shoulder tip pain** — Referred diaphragmatic irritation from significant intraperitoneal haemorrhage; indicates haemoperitoneum and requires immediate surgical assessment.

- **Sudden onset severe pain reaching maximum intensity within seconds** — Suggests ovarian torsion or cyst rupture with haemoperitoneum; these are surgical emergencies with time-critical management windows.

- **Fever with pelvic pain** — Raises concern for pelvic inflammatory disease, tubo-ovarian abscess, or complicated appendicitis; requires blood cultures, inflammatory markers, and specialist review.

- **Pain out of proportion to the clinical picture, or rapidly escalating pain** — In the context of this station, if initial pain is worsening rather than improving, the diagnosis of mittelschmerz requires reassessment and exclusion of torsion or complicated cyst.

- **Vaginal bleeding in a sexually active woman with a positive pregnancy test** — Threatened abortion versus ectopic pregnancy; requires urgent quantitative beta-hCG, pelvic ultrasound, and obstetric or gynaecological consultation.

- **Absent bowel sounds with peritonism** — Clinical peritonitis warrants immediate surgical review regardless of the provisional diagnosis.

## Investigations

**Bedside:**
- Urine pregnancy test (beta-hCG) — mandatory in all women of reproductive age with pelvic or lower abdominal pain
- Urine dipstick and microscopy — screen for urinary tract infection and haematuria (ureteric calculus)
- Vital signs including temperature, heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation
- Blood glucose level

**Bloods:**
- Full blood count (FBC) — leukocytosis suggests infection or appendicitis; low haemoglobin may indicate bleeding
- C-reactive protein (CRP) — elevated in inflammatory and infective conditions
- Serum beta-hCG — quantitative if urine test equivocal or ectopic pregnancy suspected
- Urea, creatinine and electrolytes — renal function baseline; relevant if pyelonephritis or hydronephrosis identified
- Liver function tests — if hepatobiliary pathology clinically suspected
- Serum lipase — if upper abdominal component present

**Imaging:**
- Pelvic and abdominal ultrasound — first-line imaging in women of reproductive age; assesses ovaries, uterus, free fluid, and gallbladder; high false-negative rate for appendix visualisation (50–85% of normal appendices not visualised)
- CT abdomen and pelvis with contrast — second-line if ultrasound is indeterminate and appendicitis or other significant intra-abdominal pathology cannot be excluded clinically; preferred for definitive appendiceal assessment
- Transvaginal ultrasound — superior resolution for adnexal pathology when ectopic pregnancy or ovarian torsion is suspected

**Specialist:**
- Gynaecological consultation — if ectopic pregnancy, torsion, or haemoperitoneum cannot be excluded
- Surgical consultation — if appendicitis or peritonitis is clinically suspected
- Diagnostic laparoscopy — definitive investigation for endometriosis; also used therapeutically for ovarian torsion and ectopic pregnancy

## Management

**Acute Episode of Mittelschmerz — Conservative Management:**

The management of an acute episode of mittelschmerz is conservative, as this is a physiological process that resolves spontaneously within hours.

- **Simple analgesia:** Paracetamol 500–1000 mg orally up to four times daily is first-line. NSAIDs such as ibuprofen 400 mg orally with food are effective and may be preferred given the prostaglandin-mediated component of ovulation pain. Candidates should confirm the absence of NSAID contraindications (peptic ulcer disease, renal impairment, asthma with aspirin sensitivity) before recommending NSAIDs.
- **Rest:** Patients should be advised to rest until the pain subsides. Physical exertion may exacerbate discomfort due to increased intraperitoneal movement of released fluid.
- **Local heat application:** Application of a heat pack or warm compress to the lower abdomen is commonly recommended and provides symptomatic relief in many patients. Heat promotes local muscle relaxation and may reduce cramping.
- **Reassurance:** The patient should be clearly reassured that mittelschmerz is a normal physiological process associated with ovulation, is not indicative of disease, and resolves without intervention. This is particularly important when the patient has presented with significant anxiety regarding a specific diagnosis (gallbladder disease or appendicitis).

**Longer-Term Management for Recurrent Mittelschmerz:**

In women who experience significant recurrent mid-cycle pain affecting quality of life, the combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP) may be considered. By suppressing ovulation and regulating the hormonal cycle, the COCP attenuates the follicular rupture event and may reduce the frequency and severity of mittelschmerz. This option is appropriate to mention briefly in the counselling task, particularly when the patient describes a pattern of recurrent episodes. However, prescribing the COCP requires a full contraceptive history, blood pressure measurement, thrombotic risk assessment, and discussion of risks and benefits — this extended consultation falls outside the scope of an acute mittelschmerz station but can be offered as a future option.

**Incidental Asymptomatic Cholelithiasis:**

The ultrasound in this station reveals gallstones with no features of acute cholecystitis (normal gallbladder wall thickness, no pericholecystic fluid, no bile duct dilatation, no biliary colic). Asymptomatic cholelithiasis in a young woman does not require acute intervention. The patient should be counselled that the stones were an incidental finding, that they are not causing her current symptoms, and that they will be monitored. She should be advised to return if she develops right upper quadrant pain, particularly after fatty meals, or if she develops jaundice, fever, or vomiting — symptoms consistent with symptomatic cholelithiasis or acute cholecystitis. Elective cholecystectomy is typically considered only when gallstones become symptomatic.

## Communication and Patient Education

**Structuring the Consultation:**

The information-giving sequence in this station follows a logical narrative arc: establish the diagnosis, explain the physiological mechanism in lay terms, address the patient's specific concern directly, explain incidental findings, provide management advice, and close with a safety net. Candidates who deliver diagnosis and management without addressing the patient's stated concern will score poorly on the communication domain regardless of clinical accuracy.

**Explaining the Diagnosis in Plain Language:**

Avoid the term "mittelschmerz" as an opener. Instead, establish the physiological concept first — the egg grows in a follicle in the ovary, the follicle ruptures at mid-cycle, fluid is released, and this irritates the abdominal lining causing transient pain. Only after this scaffolding is in place should the medical term be introduced: *"We call this mid-cycle pain, or by its medical name, mittelschmerz."* Supporting the diagnosis with three anchoring reasons — the timing relative to the menstrual cycle, the recurrent pattern in previous cycles, and the mild self-limiting character of the pain — reinforces the patient's understanding and confidence in the diagnosis.

**Addressing the Patient's Specific Concern:**

Do not gloss over the concern at the end of the consultation. Address it as a discrete component:
- *For gallbladder concern:* "I understand your worry given your mother's experience. I want to reassure you clearly — the pain you have today is not coming from your gallbladder. The scan does show some stones, but they are not inflamed, not blocked, and not responsible for this episode of pain."
- *For appendicitis concern:* "I understand why you thought of appendicitis — the pain is on the right side, and I know that can be worrying. I want to explain why I am confident this is not appendicitis..."  followed by the specific clinical reasons outlined above.

**Communicating Incidental Findings:**

Incidental findings require careful communication. Stating *"We found something else on your scan"* before providing context may create unnecessary alarm. A more structured approach is: establish what the scan was looking for, confirm the primary diagnosis, and then introduce the incidental finding with immediate contextual reassurance — *"The scan also happened to show some small stones in your gallbladder. I want to be clear straight away — these are not causing your pain today and they are not causing you any problems at the moment."*

**Written Information and Follow-Up:**

Where available, provide written patient information about mittelschmerz. Advise the patient to follow up with her general practitioner if episodes become more frequent, more severe, or are associated with new symptoms. For the incidental gallstones, a follow-up appointment to discuss long-term monitoring is appropriate.

## AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

- **Do not anchor prematurely on the stated side of pain.** The examiner's use of "right-sided" rather than "right lower quadrant" is deliberate. Commence with an open question and allow the patient to localise. Candidates who assume right lower quadrant and skip hepatobiliary questions may miss a gallbladder concern that the patient raises spontaneously — and with it, the entire communication task.

- **Always exclude pregnancy before diagnosing mittelschmerz.** A urine beta-hCG is mandatory in any woman of reproductive age with pelvic or lower abdominal pain. Ectopic pregnancy is a must-not-miss diagnosis that can mimic mittelschmerz clinically; failure to mention beta-hCG testing will be noted by the examiner.

- **Non-visualisation of the appendix on ultrasound is not a positive finding for appendicitis.** Up to 50–85% of normal appendices are not seen on ultrasound. Candidates must be prepared to explain this to a patient who is concerned about appendicitis and has an "inconclusive" appendix on imaging.

- **Address the ICE concern before closing.** The patient's concern — whether about gallbladder disease or appendicitis — must be explicitly acknowledged and addressed with specific clinical reasons, not reassured away with vague statements such as "I don't think it's that." Provide named features from the history and imaging that support your reassurance.

- **Build physiological explanation before introducing medical terminology.** Explaining ovulation physiology in lay terms before using the word "mittelschmerz" significantly improves patient comprehension and examiner scores. The sequence is: egg → follicle → rupture → fluid → pain → mittelschmerz.

- **Do not over-investigate mittelschmerz in the acute setting.** The station rewards appropriate investigation (ultrasound, beta-hCG, FBC, CRP) without escalating to CT scanning in a haemodynamically stable patient with a clear history and characteristic ultrasound findings. Proposing CT as a first-line investigation reflects poor clinical reasoning.

- **Differentiate the acute management task from the longer-term management task.** When asked about management of the current episode, focus on simple analgesia, rest, and heat packs. Oral contraceptive pill is a long-term option for recurrent cases — introduce it only when the station task specifically invites discussion of recurrence management, and flag that it requires a separate, fuller consultation.

- **Explain all four ultrasound findings, not just the primary one.** Candidates who explain the ruptured follicle but fail to address the fluid in the pouch of Douglas, the incidental gallstones, and the non-visualised appendix will leave the patient with unanswered questions — and will lose marks for incomplete information-giving.

- **Do not dismiss appendicitis without providing clinical reasons.** Saying "it's not appendicitis" without explaining the absence of migratory pain, anorexia, fever, and nausea is insufficient. The examiner is assessing whether the candidate can provide evidence-based reassurance, not just a confident assertion.

- **Counsel the patient about recurrence.** Mittelschmerz is a recurrent condition in many women. Informing the patient that she may experience similar pain in future mid-cycles, and providing a framework for self-management at home, demonstrates comprehensive counselling and will distinguish a high-scoring candidate from an average one.

- **Use the diagnosis to address all three ICE elements explicitly.** The diagnosis of mittelschmerz satisfies the patient's expectation (an explanation for her pain), addresses her concern (it is not the serious condition she feared), and builds on her idea (or corrects it). Structured acknowledgment of all three ICE elements before closing is a hallmark of a high-scoring communication station.

## Safety Net

**When to Seek Immediate Care (Emergency Department):**

The patient must be given clear, specific instructions on when to seek urgent medical attention, rather than generic advice to "come back if worried." Provide verbal and, where possible, written safety net instructions:

- **Worsening or severe pain** — If the pain becomes significantly worse, constant rather than intermittent, or reaches a severity of seven or more out of ten, the patient should present to the emergency department rather than waiting for a GP appointment. Escalating pain may indicate ovarian torsion, haemoperitoneum, or a complication of the incidental gallstones.
- **Unusual vaginal bleeding** — Any intermenstrual bleeding, particularly in conjunction with pelvic pain, warrants urgent assessment to exclude ectopic pregnancy or other pelvic pathology.
- **Fever or rigors** — Temperature above 38°C with pelvic pain raises concern for pelvic inflammatory disease, tubo-ovarian abscess, or other infective complication; requires same-day assessment.
- **Dizziness, fainting, or feeling of collapse** — Haemodynamic compromise from haemoperitoneum or ruptured ectopic pregnancy; the patient should call 000 and not drive herself to hospital.
- **Shoulder tip pain** — Diaphragmatic irritation from significant intraperitoneal haemorrhage; requires emergency assessment.
- **Right upper quadrant pain after eating, particularly fatty foods** — Suggests symptomatic activation of the incidental gallstones; requires GP assessment and possible surgical referral.
- **Jaundice, dark urine, or pale stools** — Features of biliary obstruction from gallstone migration; requires urgent hepatobiliary assessment.

**Follow-Up Plan:**

- Review with general practitioner in five to seven days if pain has not fully resolved.
- Discuss pattern of mid-cycle symptoms at the follow-up appointment to assess whether recurrent mittelschmerz warrants consideration of the combined oral contraceptive pill.
- Arrange monitoring of the incidental asymptomatic gallstones with the general practitioner, with elective surgical referral only if gallstones become symptomatic.
- Advise the patient to maintain a menstrual symptom diary documenting the timing, severity, and character of future mid-cycle pain episodes, which will assist in confirming the diagnosis of mittelschmerz if it recurs and in assessing the response to any future management.

Reassurance: The patient should be clearly reassured that mittelschmerz is a normal physiological process associated with ovulation, is not indicative of disease, and resolves without intervention. This is particularly important when the patient has presented with significant anxiety regarding a specific diagnosis (gallbladder disease or appendicitis).
