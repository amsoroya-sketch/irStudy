# Acute Epigastric Pain — Acute Pancreatitis: AMC Clinical Examination History Taking Station

---

## Clinical Presentation

A 25-year-old man presents to the emergency department with acute epigastric pain. His vital signs are provided in the station stem and demonstrate haemodynamic stability. He appears distressed, is leaning forward in his chair, and is intermittently retching into a bag — a constellation of non-verbal cues that, when recognised early, substantially narrows the differential diagnosis before a single history question is asked. This station represents one of the highest-yield acute abdominal presentations in the AMC Clinical Examination, and candidates who fail to integrate clinical observation with systematic history taking will miss the diagnostic thread running through the encounter.

Acute pancreatitis is the most common cause of acute abdomen requiring emergency admission in young adult males presenting with epigastric pain. It is a diagnosis anchored in clinical history, supported by biochemistry, and confirmed on imaging. The AMC examiner expects candidates to demonstrate not only systematic questioning but also the ability to adjust their differential weighting in real time as history is gathered — beginning with broad upper abdominal differentials and converging toward pancreatitis based on site, radiation, relieving positions, and risk factor elicitation.

This station tests three overlapping domains: structured history taking using SOCRATES, clinical reasoning with differential prioritisation, and patient-centred communication including explanation of diagnosis and investigation rationale. Candidates who approach this as a rote exercise rather than a dynamic clinical encounter will score in the lower band; those who demonstrate adaptive reasoning, non-verbal observation, and purposeful question selection will score in the upper band.

---

## Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

The AMC examiner assesses this history taking station across three domains: history taking (weighted heavily), communication skills, and clinical reasoning evidenced through differential diagnosis and investigation selection.

**Opening:** Introduce yourself by name and role. Acknowledge the patient's distress with a brief, genuine empathic statement — not formulaic language, but an authentic acknowledgement of what you observe: *"You look quite uncomfortable. I'm going to ask you some questions to understand what's happening."* Do not immediately launch into closed questions. If the patient appears to be vomiting or in significant distress, pause and attend to this before proceeding.

**Vital signs:** When haemodynamic parameters are provided in the station stem, do not ask the patient for them. Candidates who ask *"Is your heart racing? Are you dizzy?"* when stable vitals are printed on the card waste time and signal poor clinical integration. Accept the provided data and proceed.

**History Structure:** The examiner expects a systematic SOCRATES-based pain history, followed by targeted systemic questions (nausea, vomiting, bowel habit, fever, jaundice), red flag cancer screening, organ-specific history for each differential group (pancreatitis, hepatobiliary, peptic ulcer disease, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, acute coronary syndrome, lower lobe pneumonia), and a structured past, drug, social, and family history.

**ICE Integration:** Ideas, concerns, and expectations should be woven naturally into the consultation — not asked as a block at the end. Responding to the patient's distress with *"What's worrying you most about this pain?"* mid-consultation demonstrates patient-centredness and will be noted by the examiner.

**Closing:** Summarise findings back to the patient, offer a working diagnosis with a plain-language explanation, outline your differential, describe your investigation plan, and provide a clear safety net.

## Systematic Approach

### Non-Verbal Clinical Observation — The First 30 Seconds

Before speaking, train your eye on the patient. The AMC examiner deliberately stages patients with diagnostic non-verbal cues. For this pancreatitis station, observe:

- **Forward-leaning posture:** The patient sits bent forward, head down, perhaps bracing against the chair or bed. This posture reflexively reduces pancreatic ductal pressure and is a classical, well-documented feature of acute pancreatitis. It distinguishes pancreatitis from most other causes of epigastric pain, in which position provides no relief.
- **Active nausea or vomiting:** The patient may be holding a bag, retching, or clearly suppressing the urge to vomit. Pancreatitis produces profound, often intractable nausea and vomiting that fails to relieve the pain — unlike the nausea of gastroenteritis, which is partially alleviated by vomiting.
- **Diaphoresis and pallor:** A visibly sweating, pale patient with epigastric pain in this posture has pancreatitis until proven otherwise.

Acknowledging what you see in your history is both clinically appropriate and demonstrates competence: *"I notice you seem more comfortable leaning forward — has the pain been better in that position?"* This integrates observation into structured questioning and will score well.

---

### SOCRATES Assessment — Pain History

**Site:**
Ask the patient to point to the area of maximal pain. In acute pancreatitis, the patient will reliably point to the epigastric region, often with a flat hand gesture across the upper abdomen. Document this as epigastric.

*"Can you point to where the pain is worst for you?"*

**Onset:**
Establish the time course precisely. Acute pancreatitis typically has a rapid onset over hours, often with a clear precipitant (a large meal, an episode of heavy alcohol consumption, or both). Ask whether this is the first ever episode or whether there have been prior similar episodes — recurrent attacks raise the possibility of chronic pancreatitis or a recurring precipitant such as gallstones.

*"When did this pain start? Did it come on suddenly or gradually? Have you had anything like this before?"*

**Character:**
The quality of pain in acute pancreatitis is typically described as severe, constant, and deep — a boring, gnawing, or stabbing sensation rather than colicky. Biliary colic is colicky (waxing and waning). Peptic ulcer pain is burning. Pancreatitis pain is unrelenting and severe.

*"Can you describe what the pain feels like? Is it sharp, stabbing, dull, or a deep ache? Does it come and go, or has it been constant since it started?"*

**Radiation:**
Radiation is a critical discriminating feature. Pancreatitis classically radiates to the back — specifically to the central or left upper back — as the inflamed pancreas abuts the retroperitoneal structures. Some candidates receive a scenario where the patient reports radiation to both the back and the chest; in this setting, acute coronary syndrome must be actively excluded rather than assumed to be excluded.

*"Does the pain travel or spread anywhere — into your back, your chest, or down into your belly?"*

**Associations:**
Probe systematically:
- **Nausea and vomiting:** Almost universal. Establish frequency, volume, and crucially the colour of vomit. Green (bilious) vomiting suggests upper intestinal obstruction and is a specific alarm finding. Faeculent vomiting is a surgical emergency.
- **Anorexia:** Common in acute pancreatitis.
- **Fever and rigors:** Fever suggests either an infectious precipitant (e.g. viral pancreatitis) or a complication such as infected pancreatic necrosis or cholangitis.
- **Jaundice:** Yellowish discolouration of the skin or sclera, dark urine, and pale stools suggest biliary obstruction — either from gallstone pancreatitis with common bile duct obstruction or from pancreatic head pathology (including malignancy). This must be specifically asked.

*"Have you noticed any yellowing of your skin or eyes? Any dark urine, like dark tea or cola? Any very pale or whitish stools?"*

**Timing:**
Duration since onset, whether the pain is static, worsening, or fluctuating. Acute pancreatitis pain is typically maximal at onset and may worsen progressively over the first 24–48 hours if complicated.

**Exacerbating and Relieving Factors:**
Two specific lines of enquiry are essential:

1. **Positional relief:** *"Is there any position that makes the pain better or worse? Have you noticed it's better when you lean forward?"* A positive response is pathognomonic of pancreatitis in the right clinical context. Lying flat worsens the pain.
2. **Relationship to food:** *"Is the pain related to eating or drinking? Does it come on after meals, or is it unrelated to food?"* In acute pancreatitis, the pain is typically not directly triggered by eating, though a precipitating large fatty meal may have occurred hours before. This distinguishes it from biliary colic (precipitated by fatty meals within 30–60 minutes) and peptic ulcer disease (relieved or worsened by food depending on the type).

**Severity:**
Use a validated numeric scale. *"On a scale of 0 to 10, where 0 is no pain and 10 is the worst pain you can imagine, how would you rate this pain right now?"* Acute pancreatitis is typically rated 8–10.

### Differential-Directed History — Organ System Approach

Once SOCRATES is complete and pancreatitis is the leading diagnosis, expand your history to systematically address all differential groups. This demonstrates that the candidate has not anchored prematurely and has excluded alternative or co-existing pathology.

#### Group 1: Pancreatitis — Aetiology-Specific Enquiry

This is the highest-yield additional history block for this station. Having established the presentation is consistent with pancreatitis, you must now identify the cause — because aetiology drives management.

**Alcohol:**
Do not ask only *"Do you drink alcohol?"* The AMC examiner uses a nuanced scenario in which the patient may minimise regular consumption but has had a recent episode of heavy or binge drinking.

*"Do you drink alcohol at all? ... How much would you typically drink in a week? ... Even if you don't drink regularly, have you had a particularly heavy night of drinking recently — a binge-drinking episode in the last day or two?"*

A typical response: *"I don't drink that much normally, but last night at a party I had five or six shots."* Five to six standard drinks in one sitting constitutes a binge-drinking episode sufficient to precipitate acute pancreatitis in a susceptible individual.

**Gallstones:**
Gallstone pancreatitis occurs when a gallstone migrates through the common bile duct and transiently or persistently obstructs the ampulla of Vater, causing retrograde activation of pancreatic enzymes.

*"Have you ever been told you have gallstones? Have you had any previous episodes of pain, particularly in the right upper part of your abdomen, especially after a fatty meal — maybe a few hours after eating?"*

Eliciting a prior history of biliary colic establishes gallstones as the likely precipitant and shifts management planning toward cholecystectomy.

**Hypertriglyceridaemia:**
Severe hypertriglyceridaemia (serum triglycerides >10 mmol/L) is an underappreciated but important cause of acute pancreatitis, increasingly prominent in current AMC examination scenarios.

*"Have you ever been told your blood fat levels — triglycerides or cholesterol — are high? Has any doctor ever mentioned hyperlipidaemia or high lipids?"*

A positive answer here is diagnostically significant and mandates a fasting lipid profile as part of the investigation panel.

**Medications:**
A number of medications are established causes of drug-induced pancreatitis. Important examples include:
- Azathioprine and 6-mercaptopurine
- Sodium valproate
- Tetracyclines
- Thiazide diuretics
- Didanosine (antiretrovirals)
- Statins (less commonly)
- Corticosteroids (at high doses)

*"Are you taking any prescription medications, supplements, or over-the-counter medicines regularly?"*

**Trauma:**
Blunt abdominal trauma — for example, a motor vehicle collision, handlebar injury, or assault — can cause traumatic pancreatitis by direct pancreatic injury.

*"Have you had any injury or trauma to your abdomen recently — any accident, fall, or blow to the stomach?"*

**Infection:**
Viral pancreatitis can occur with mumps, coxsackievirus B, cytomegalovirus, and HIV. Enquire about recent viral illness, fever before the onset of pain, and immunocompromised status.

*"Have you had any viral illnesses recently — fever, sore throat, swollen glands?"*

**Hypercalcaemia:**
Primary hyperparathyroidism causing hypercalcaemia is a recognised precipitant. Ask about symptoms of hypercalcaemia: polyuria, polydipsia, constipation, bone pain, or renal colic.

---

#### Group 2: Hepatobiliary — Hepatitis and Biliary Pathology

**Jaundice screen:** Already covered under SOCRATES associations. Reinforce if not yet asked.

**Hepatitis risk factors:** Recent overseas travel (particularly Southeast Asia, South Asia, or sub-Saharan Africa), unprotected sexual intercourse, intravenous drug use, recent tattoos or body piercing with non-sterile equipment, blood transfusion, or close contact with a jaundiced individual.

*"Have you travelled overseas recently? Have you had any contact with someone who was jaundiced?"*

**Biliary colic:** Prior episodes of right upper quadrant or epigastric pain following fatty meals, lasting 30 minutes to several hours, possibly with radiation to the right shoulder tip.

**Cholangitis triad (Charcot's):** Fever, jaundice, and right upper quadrant pain — if present together, ascending cholangitis is a surgical emergency.

---

#### Group 3: Peptic Ulcer Disease and Gastro-Oesophageal Reflux Disease

**GORD screening:**
The most efficient screening question is heartburn. If asking for a more specific feature:

*"Have you noticed a bitter or acidic taste in the back of your throat? Any sudden flooding of your mouth with saliva — particularly when not eating?"*

The latter phenomenon — waterbrash — is the reflexive hypersalivation triggered by oesophageal acid exposure. It is highly specific for GORD when present and can be efficiently elicited with a targeted question.

**Peptic ulcer disease:**
- Epigastric burning or gnawing pain
- Relationship to food: duodenal ulcer pain is often relieved by eating and returns 2–3 hours post-meal; gastric ulcer pain may worsen with food
- Risk factors: Helicobacter pylori infection (ask about prior testing or treatment), NSAID or aspirin use (regular or high-dose), alcohol and caffeine consumption, smoking

*"Do you use anti-inflammatory tablets like ibuprofen or naproxen — either prescribed or bought over the counter?"*

---

#### Group 4: Cardiac — Acute Coronary Syndrome

Epigastric pain or pain felt in the upper abdomen is a well-documented atypical presentation of inferior myocardial infarction. This is a must-not-miss diagnosis. Even in a 25-year-old, ACS must be considered — particularly with associated risk factors or radiation to the chest.

*"Have you had any chest pain, tightness, pressure, or discomfort? Any pain in your left arm, jaw, or neck? Any shortness of breath? Any palpitations or feeling that your heart is racing?"*

If the patient reports radiation to the chest in addition to the back, ACS must be actively excluded with ECG and troponin as priority investigations.

---

#### Group 5: Respiratory — Lower Lobe Pneumonia

Lower lobe pneumonia — particularly right lower lobe — can refer pain to the right upper quadrant or epigastric region via diaphragmatic irritation. This must not be overlooked, particularly in febrile patients.

*"Have you had any cough? Any shortness of breath? Any fever or chills before the pain started?"*

---

#### Group 6: General Gastrointestinal — Red Flag Cancer Screen

A structured red flag screen must be performed regardless of the suspected diagnosis. In a 25-year-old, primary pancreatic or gastric malignancy is uncommon but not impossible; more importantly, this screen demonstrates systematic clinical practice to the examiner.

Ask specifically about:
- **Involuntary weight loss:** *"Have you lost any weight recently without trying? Over what time period?"*
- **Appetite change:** *"Has your appetite changed — are you eating less than usual?"*
- **Haematemesis or melaena:** *"Have you vomited any blood or coffee-ground material? Have your bowel motions been unusually dark or tarry?"*
- **Change in bowel habit:** *"Have you noticed any change in your normal bowel pattern — constipation, diarrhoea, or alternating?"*
- **Dysphagia:** *"Any difficulty or pain swallowing?"*
- **Fatigue and constitutional symptoms:** *"Have you been unusually tired or run down?"*
- **Family history of gastrointestinal malignancy:** *"Has anyone in your immediate family — parents, siblings — had bowel, stomach, or pancreatic cancer?"*
- **Lumps:** *"Have you noticed any new lumps or swellings anywhere on your body?"*

### Structured Past, Drug, Social, and Family History

**Past medical history:**
- Prior episodes of pancreatitis
- Known gallstones or biliary disease
- Peptic ulcer disease or Helicobacter pylori
- Dyslipidaemia, diabetes mellitus
- Inflammatory bowel disease
- Prior abdominal surgery

**Drug history:**
- Prescription medications (with specific focus on pancreatitis-associated drugs listed above)
- NSAIDs, aspirin, over-the-counter analgesics
- Oral contraceptive pill or hormone replacement therapy
- Recreational drug use

**Social history:**
- Alcohol: detailed quantification using standard drinks per week; specific probe for recent binge drinking episode
- Smoking
- Recreational drug use (cocaine causes vasospasm and can precipitate pancreatitis)
- Occupational history
- Sexual history if relevant to hepatitis risk

**Family history:**
- Gastrointestinal malignancy
- Familial hyperlipidaemia or pancreatitis

## Differential Diagnosis

| Category | Diagnosis | Key Distinguishing Features |
|----------|-----------|----------------------------|
| Common | Acute pancreatitis | Epigastric pain radiating to back, relieved by leaning forward, intractable vomiting, alcohol or gallstone precipitant, elevated lipase |
| Common | Peptic ulcer disease | Epigastric burning, relationship to food, NSAID or H. pylori risk factors, may be relieved by antacids |
| Common | Biliary colic | Colicky right upper quadrant pain after fatty meals, radiation to right shoulder tip, no fever |
| Common | Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease | Heartburn, waterbrash, regurgitation, worse on lying flat, unrelated to single acute episode |
| Important | Acute cholecystitis | Right upper quadrant pain, fever, Murphy's sign positive, may follow biliary colic episodes |
| Important | Cholangitis | Charcot's triad: fever, jaundice, right upper quadrant pain — surgical emergency |
| Important | Hepatitis (viral or alcoholic) | Jaundice, right upper quadrant discomfort, dark urine, pale stools, elevated transaminases |
| Important | Gastroparesis | Nausea, vomiting, bloating, prior diabetes or vagotomy |
| Must Not Miss | Acute coronary syndrome (inferior MI) | Epigastric or chest pain, diaphoresis, ECG changes — particularly with radiation to chest or jaw |
| Must Not Miss | Perforated peptic ulcer | Sudden-onset severe epigastric pain, peritonism, absent bowel sounds, free air under diaphragm |
| Must Not Miss | Pancreatic malignancy | Weight loss, anorexia, obstructive jaundice, Courvoisier's sign, painless jaundice in older patients |
| Must Not Miss | Lower lobe pneumonia | Pleuritic pain referred to upper abdomen, fever, cough, reduced air entry, dullness to percussion |
| Must Not Miss | Mesenteric ischaemia | Severe pain out of proportion to examination, atrial fibrillation, post-prandial pain, elderly |

## Red Flags — Must Not Miss

- **Haematemesis or melaena** — Suggests bleeding peptic ulcer, Mallory-Weiss tear (from repeated vomiting), or oesophageal varices (in alcohol-dependent patients); requires urgent upper GI endoscopy and resuscitation.

- **Fever with jaundice and right upper quadrant pain (Charcot's triad)** — Ascending cholangitis is a life-threatening biliary infection requiring emergency biliary decompression. Pentad of Reynolds adds hypotension and altered consciousness — septic shock from cholangitis.

- **Signs of peritonism** — Generalised guarding, rigidity, or rebound tenderness suggest perforation (peptic ulcer, colonic) or haemorrhage; requires immediate surgical review and imaging.

- **Haemodynamic instability (tachycardia, hypotension, decreased urine output)** — Even when initial vitals are stable, rapid clinical deterioration in pancreatitis can indicate haemorrhagic pancreatitis (Cullen's sign: periumbilical bruising; Grey Turner's sign: flank bruising), requiring ICU-level care.

- **Radiation of epigastric pain to the chest, jaw, or left arm** — Must prompt immediate ECG and troponin to exclude acute coronary syndrome before attributing pain to a GI cause.

- **Green or faeculent vomiting** — Suggests high intestinal obstruction or gastric outlet obstruction; a surgical emergency.

- **Unexplained weight loss with anorexia in the context of epigastric pain** — Raises concern for pancreatic, gastric, or oesophageal malignancy regardless of age. Warrants CT abdomen and upper GI endoscopy.

- **New-onset diabetes or unexplained hyperglycaemia** — Can be the presenting feature of pancreatic adenocarcinoma causing endocrine insufficiency; do not assume type 2 diabetes without appropriate work-up.

- **Cullen's or Grey Turner's signs on examination** — Periumbilical or flank ecchymosis indicates retroperitoneal haemorrhage from haemorrhagic pancreatitis — a severe, life-threatening complication with high mortality.

- **Failure to improve or clinical deterioration at 48–72 hours** — Suggests development of infected pancreatic necrosis, pseudocyst, or systemic complications; requires escalation to specialist gastroenterology or surgical review and contrast-enhanced CT.

## Investigations

**Bedside:**
- Vital signs monitoring (heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, temperature)
- Capillary blood glucose
- Urine dipstick (glycosuria, bilirubinuria)
- 12-lead ECG — to exclude inferior MI as a cause of epigastric pain
- Urine or serum pregnancy test in women of reproductive age (to exclude ectopic pregnancy)

**Bloods:**
- **Serum lipase** — First and most important test; more sensitive and specific than amylase for acute pancreatitis; elevated >3× upper limit of normal is diagnostic in the right clinical context
- Serum amylase — Less specific than lipase; rises earlier and falls faster; still included in standard panels
- Full blood count — Leukocytosis supports acute inflammation or infection; haematocrit elevation may indicate haemoconcentration
- Urea, electrolytes, and creatinine (UEC) — Assess renal function and hydration status; acute kidney injury is a serious complication
- C-reactive protein (CRP) — Marker of systemic inflammation; CRP >150 mg/L at 48 hours correlates with severe pancreatitis (Glasgow scoring system)
- Liver function tests (LFTs) — ALT elevation >3× upper limit of normal is suggestive of gallstone pancreatitis; bilirubin elevation suggests biliary obstruction; AST may be mildly elevated in alcoholic pancreatitis
- Blood glucose level — Hyperglycaemia occurs in pancreatitis due to both glucagon release and insulin deficiency; a marker of severity
- Serum calcium — Hypocalcaemia is a recognised complication of severe pancreatitis (saponification of peripancreatic fat); hypercalcaemia can precipitate pancreatitis
- Serum triglycerides and fasting lipid profile — To confirm hypertriglyceridaemia as a precipitant
- Venous blood gas (VBG) / Arterial blood gas (ABG) — Arterial lactate, pH, and base excess assess metabolic status and oxygenation; required in suspected severe pancreatitis
- Coagulation studies — If disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) is suspected in haemorrhagic pancreatitis
- Blood cultures — If fever present and cholangitis or infected necrosis is suspected
- Hepatitis serology (A, B, C, E) — If viral hepatitis is a differential or there are jaundice features

**Imaging:**
- **Abdominal ultrasound** — First-line imaging; identifies gallstones, biliary dilatation, cholecystitis, and peripancreatic fluid; the pancreas itself is often poorly visualised on ultrasound due to overlying gas
- **Contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen and pelvis (CECT)** — Gold standard for staging severity, identifying pancreatic necrosis, peripancreatic collections, and complications; indicated at 48–72 hours if no clinical improvement, or earlier if diagnosis is uncertain
- **Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP)** — Non-invasive imaging of the biliary and pancreatic ductal anatomy; preferred over ERCP for diagnosis of choledocholithiasis
- **Chest X-ray** — To exclude lower lobe pneumonia, assess for pleural effusion (a complication of pancreatitis), and free intraperitoneal gas (perforation)
- **Plain abdominal X-ray** — Limited diagnostic value; may show a sentinel loop (isolated ileus of adjacent small bowel) or colon cut-off sign in pancreatitis; primarily used to exclude perforation

**Specialist:**
- **Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)** — Therapeutic, not diagnostic; indicated for gallstone pancreatitis with persistent biliary obstruction or cholangitis; performed by specialist gastroenterologist or interventional endoscopist
- **Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)** — High sensitivity for microlithiasis and early pancreatic neoplasia

## Management

**Immediate and Acute Management (Emergency Department):**

1. **Resuscitation:** Establish intravenous access; commence aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation — crystalloid (Hartmann's solution preferred over normal saline based on evidence of lower systemic inflammation); target urine output ≥0.5 mL/kg/hour.

2. **Analgesia:** Adequate pain control is a priority. Titrated intravenous opioid analgesia (morphine or fentanyl) is the standard of care — the historical contraindication to morphine in pancreatitis (concern about sphincter of Oddi spasm) is not supported by current evidence. Add regular paracetamol. Consider PCA (patient-controlled analgesia) for severe or ongoing pain.

3. **Nil by mouth (NBM):** Traditionally patients are kept NBM during the acute phase. However, current evidence supports early enteral feeding (within 24–48 hours) via nasogastric or nasojejunal tube in severe pancreatitis, as it preserves gut mucosal integrity and reduces infectious complications. In mild pancreatitis, resumption of oral fluids and soft diet as tolerated is appropriate once nausea is controlled.

4. **Antiemetics:** Intravenous ondansetron or metoclopramide for intractable nausea and vomiting.

5. **Monitoring:** Continuous cardiac monitoring, hourly urine output, frequent reassessment of vital signs, and serial abdominal examination.

**Severity Assessment:**
Apply validated scoring systems to risk-stratify:
- **Glasgow-Imrie Score** (most commonly used in Australian and UK practice): scored at 48 hours using eight parameters (PaO₂, age, white cell count, LDH, AST, glucose, serum albumin, serum calcium). Score ≥3 predicts severe pancreatitis.
- **APACHE II score**
- **Bedside Index of Severity in Acute Pancreatitis (BISAP)**
- **CRP >150 mg/L at 48 hours** — single most clinically useful bedside marker of severity

**Aetiology-Specific Management:**

- **Gallstone pancreatitis:** Laparoscopic cholecystectomy should be performed during the same admission (or within 2 weeks) for mild pancreatitis; delayed for severe pancreatitis until clinical resolution. ERCP indicated for concomitant cholangitis or persisting ductal obstruction.
- **Alcohol-induced pancreatitis:** Alcohol cessation counselling; referral to alcohol and other drug (AOD) services; thiamine supplementation if alcohol dependence suspected.
- **Hypertriglyceridaemia-induced pancreatitis:** Insulin infusion (promotes lipoprotein lipase activity and triglyceride clearance); plasmapheresis in severe refractory cases; long-term management with fibrates.
- **Medication-induced pancreatitis:** Cessation of the causative agent.

**Complications Monitoring:**
- **Local:** Pancreatic necrosis, pseudocyst formation, walled-off necrosis, pancreatic fistula
- **Systemic:** Acute kidney injury, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), sepsis, multi-organ failure, DIC, hyperglycaemia

**Severe Pancreatitis:**
ICU admission for organ support; specialist gastroenterology and surgery input; consideration of percutaneous drainage or surgical necrosectomy for infected necrosis (not performed until at least 4 weeks after onset when necrosis is demarcated).

**Long-Term:**
- Exocrine insufficiency: pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy
- Endocrine insufficiency: diabetes management
- Dietary modification: low-fat diet, alcohol abstinence
- Outpatient follow-up with gastroenterology

## Communication and Patient Education

**Explaining the Diagnosis:**
Use plain, accessible language without medical jargon. A reliable framework:

*"You have a condition called acute pancreatitis. The pancreas is an organ in your upper abdomen — it sits behind your stomach — and its job is to produce enzymes that help your body digest food. In your case, the pancreas has become inflamed — which is what we call pancreatitis. This inflammation is causing your pain, your nausea, and the vomiting you've been having."*

**Explaining the Cause:**
Tailor to the identified precipitant:

*"Based on what you've told me — particularly the heavy drinking episode last night — we believe the alcohol has triggered this episode of inflammation. Alcohol is one of the most common causes of pancreatitis."*

If hypertriglyceridaemia is also a contributing factor: *"You also mentioned being told in the past that your blood fats were elevated. High blood fat levels — what we call triglycerides — can also make the pancreas more susceptible to this kind of inflammation, and we want to check those levels today."*

**Explaining the Differential:**
Briefly acknowledge the diagnostic reasoning process without overwhelming the patient:

*"Before settling on pancreatitis, I was also considering other possibilities — problems with the gallbladder or liver, irritation in the stomach lining, and I wanted to make sure this wasn't anything to do with your heart or lungs. Based on your symptoms and the examination findings, I believe pancreatitis is the most likely explanation, and our blood tests and ultrasound will confirm this."*

**Explaining Investigations:**
If the station task includes explaining results, structure this systematically:

- White blood cells: *"Your white blood cell count is elevated, which tells us your body is fighting inflammation."*
- Lipase/amylase: *"The most important result is your lipase level — this is an enzyme made by the pancreas, and yours is significantly elevated, which confirms the pancreas is inflamed."*
- Liver enzymes (mildly elevated): *"Your liver enzymes are slightly raised — this can happen with pancreatitis because the pancreas and liver work closely together. It's a mild elevation and doesn't mean your liver is damaged."*

**Addressing Concerns:**
Common patient concerns in this scenario:
- *"Is this serious? Am I going to be okay?"* — Reassure appropriately: most episodes of pancreatitis resolve with supportive treatment, but explain that the team will monitor carefully.
- *"Do I need surgery?"* — Reassure that the majority of cases do not require surgery, but explain that the treatment plan will depend on how things progress.
- *"Is it because of my drinking?"* — Be honest and non-judgmental: *"Alcohol does appear to have played a role here. This is something we'll want to talk more about to help prevent this from happening again."*

## AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

- **Read the vital signs in the stem and act on them.** If the patient is haemodynamically stable, proceed without asking about dizziness or palpitations. If the stem shows tachycardia or hypotension, address haemodynamic instability as your opening priority. Failing to use provided information signals poor clinical integration.

- **Look at the patient before you speak.** In the AMC Clinical Examination, non-verbal cues are deliberately staged. A patient leaning forward, retching, or diaphoretic is giving you a pre-formed diagnosis. Naming what you observe (*"I can see you're leaning forward — does that help the pain?"*) demonstrates clinical acuity and almost certainly scores communication marks.

- **Do not ask only "Do you drink alcohol?"** The current examination uses a nuanced alcohol scenario in which the patient minimises regular use but has had a recent binge. A two-stage question — establishing baseline use, then specifically probing for recent heavy or binge drinking — is the only way to elicit the correct answer. Candidates who accept "I don't drink much" and move on will miss the aetiology.

- **Lipase before amylase, and lipase before all other bloods.** In the investigation list, lead with serum lipase. It is more sensitive and specific than amylase for acute pancreatitis, and experienced examiners expect it to appear first. Candidates who omit lipase entirely and list amylase alone are demonstrating outdated knowledge.

- **Ask about hypertriglyceridaemia by name.** The examiner rewards candidates who ask specifically about elevated blood fats or triglycerides and who identify this as a precipitant of pancreatitis. The question *"Has anyone ever told you your blood fat levels were high?"* is concise, patient-accessible, and will yield the relevant history when positive.

- **Actively exclude ACS.** Radiation of epigastric pain to the chest demands an ECG and troponin, irrespective of the patient's age. Mentioning this explicitly in your investigation plan demonstrates systematic thinking and prevents anchoring bias.

- **Structure your differential verbally and by anatomical group.** In the explanation-of-diagnosis component of the station, present your differential by anatomical region (pancreas, hepatobiliary, stomach/oesophagus, cardiac, respiratory). This structured verbal reasoning is assessed under clinical reasoning and scores marks.

- **Mention gallstones as a differential cause even in a young male.** Gallstone pancreatitis can occur at any age and is the second most common cause after alcohol. Failing to elicit a history of prior biliary colic or to include gallstone pancreatitis in your differential list is a systematic omission.

- **Time-manage the SOCRATES block.** Candidates frequently spend their entire six minutes on pain history and never reach the hepatobiliary, cardiac, or respiratory differentials. Practice a compact but complete SOCRATES (under 2.5 minutes) to allow time for the full systematic review and ICE.

- **Ask about waterbrash specifically for GORD.** Rather than asking *"Do you have reflux?"* — which many patients will not understand — ask *"Have you ever had a sudden flood of saliva in your mouth for no reason, when you're not eating?"* This is both more diagnostically specific and demonstrates a depth of knowledge that distinguishes upper-band candidates.

- **Do not diagnose "pancreatitis" without explaining what it means.** In the diagnosis explanation task, always follow the diagnosis with a brief anatomical and functional explanation. *"The pancreas is an organ that digests food, and it is inflamed"* is the minimum scaffold. Patients who leave without understanding what is wrong with them represent a communication failure, which will cost marks.

- **Acknowledge mild LFT elevation in context.** When presenting investigations, explain that ALT and AST elevation of 2–3 times the upper limit of normal is expected and does not indicate liver disease — it reflects the shared ductal anatomy of the pancreato-biliary system. Candidates who misinterpret mild transaminase elevation as hepatitis will misdirect their management plan.

## Safety Net

**Before discharge or at completion of the AMC station explanation, the following safety net must be communicated clearly:**

*"You will be admitted to hospital for treatment and monitoring while we manage this. I will be reviewing your blood results and ultrasound results as they come back. The team will be checking on you regularly."*

For community follow-up scenarios (after resolution of acute episode):

**Return immediately to the emergency department if you experience:**
- Return of severe abdominal pain
- Vomiting blood or passing black, tarry stools
- Fever and chills
- Yellow discolouration of skin or eyes
- Inability to tolerate any fluids by mouth
- Feeling faint, dizzy, or unusually unwell

**Short-term follow-up:**
- Outpatient review with gastroenterology within 4–6 weeks of discharge
- Repeat fasting lipid profile if hypertriglyceridaemia was identified
- Liver function tests to confirm normalisation
- Ultrasound follow-up to confirm gallstone status — if gallstones were identified, referral for elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy
- Dietary advice: strict alcohol abstinence; low-fat diet during recovery phase; gradual reintroduction of normal diet as tolerated

**Alcohol cessation:**
If alcohol was the identified precipitant, reinforce that any further alcohol consumption significantly increases the risk of recurrent pancreatitis, which carries a higher complication rate with each episode. Provide referral to local alcohol and other drug support services (e.g. DirectLine, local AOD teams, GP-managed pharmacotherapy). Document this discussion in the patient's notes.

**Written resources:**
Provide written discharge instructions including the diagnosis, key warning symptoms, dietary restrictions, alcohol abstinence advice, and follow-up appointment details. In Australian hospitals, this is typically managed via structured discharge summary to both the patient and their general practitioner.
