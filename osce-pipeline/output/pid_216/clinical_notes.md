# Abdominal Pain in a Young Woman — Pelvic Inflammatory Disease: AMC Clinical Examination History Taking Station

---

## Clinical Presentation

A 19-year-old woman presents to the clinic with lower abdominal pain of several days' duration, localised to the right lower quadrant. The pain is constant, progressive in severity, and rated 5 out of 10 in intensity. Associated features include low-grade fever, nausea, and a yellow, malodorous, watery vaginal discharge. She is sexually active, does not use barrier contraception, and has had multiple sexual partners. There is no history of prior surgery, regular medications, or significant past medical or family history.

This case exemplifies a common AMC Clinical Examination presentation — the young woman with lower abdominal pain — which demands a structured, systematic history encompassing gastrointestinal, renal/urological, and pelvic differentials. The clinical challenge lies in recognising that the presenting complaint of abdominal pain is the entry point into a much broader differential, and that elicitation of a positive finding — in this case vaginal discharge with associated risk factors — must trigger targeted, in-depth probing to reach a working diagnosis.

The predominant assessment domain in this station is history taking. Examiners evaluate not only whether the candidate collects information systematically, but whether they demonstrate clinical reasoning by responding to positive findings, probing further with relevant follow-on questions, and constructing a coherent diagnosis and differential list. The ability to explain the diagnosis clearly, in patient-friendly language, is an equally critical component of the station.

---

## Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

This is a six-minute history taking station in which the candidate must complete a structured clinical history, identify positive findings, probe them appropriately, and conclude by explaining the diagnosis and relevant differentials to the patient in plain language.

**Opening:** Introduce yourself by name and role. Confirm the patient's name. Confirm haemodynamic stability with the examiner before commencing. Sanitise hands. Sit at eye level and adopt an open, empathic posture.

**Respond to the Opening Statement:** Allow the patient to give their narrative uninterrupted. Acknowledge the emotional content explicitly — validate distress before pivoting to questioning. Confirm whether the patient is in pain at that moment and offer analgesia if required.

**Systematic History:** Proceed through three structured differential groups — gastrointestinal, renal/urological (KUB), and pelvic/gynaecological. Within each group, screen broadly, then probe positives deeply.

**ICE Elicitation (Ideas, Concerns, Expectations):** Address concerns as they arise organically. The patient's opening statement contains her concern — worsening pain causing worry. This must be acknowledged explicitly. Expectations may emerge around investigation and treatment planning.

**Red Flag Screen:** Integrate red flag questions for malignancy, sepsis, ectopic pregnancy, and surgical emergencies throughout the history.

**Closing:** Obtain social history including smoking, alcohol, recreational drug use. Review medications, allergies, and family history. Provide a brief summary to the patient confirming what was gathered.

**Diagnosis and Differential Explanation:** After completing the history, the candidate must explain the working diagnosis in layperson's terms, justify the reasoning based on the patient's own history findings, and briefly outline relevant differential diagnoses with explanations for why they are considered less likely.

## Systematic Approach

### SOCRATES Pain Assessment

Pain assessment must be completed early and efficiently. In a six-minute station, SOCRATES should take no more than two minutes, leaving adequate time for differential-driven questioning.

**Site:** Right lower quadrant. This is confirmed by asking the patient to point to the pain.

**Onset:** When did the pain start? Has it been present for several days, or did it come on suddenly? In this case — progressive over three to five days.

**Character:** Sharp, stabbing, cramping, or dull aching? In PID, pain is typically dull and constant rather than colicky. In this case — dull, constant.

**Radiation:** Does the pain travel anywhere — to the back, shoulder tip, groin, or inner thigh?

**Associations:** Nausea, vomiting, fever, change in bowel habit, urinary symptoms, vaginal discharge, vaginal bleeding.

**Time course:** Onset and offset pattern — constant versus episodic. Worsening trajectory is a clinically important feature.

**Exacerbating and relieving factors:** Movement, food intake, defaecation, micturition, sexual intercourse (dyspareunia is a feature of PID). Nothing relieves the pain in this case — a useful clinical finding.

**Severity:** Numerical pain scale (0–10). This case: 5/10.

**Key discriminating question — pain migration:** "Where did the pain start — was it in the same place from the beginning, or did it start elsewhere and move here?" The absence of periumbilical migration to the right lower quadrant makes appendicitis less likely. In classic appendicitis, pain migrates from the periumbilical region to McBurney's point as the peritoneum becomes involved.

---

### Differential-Driven History: Three-Group Framework

Following the SOCRATES assessment, the candidate proceeds through three systematic differential groups. This structured approach ensures no major category is omitted.

#### Group 1 — Gastrointestinal Differentials

**General GI symptoms (screen broadly):**
- Nausea, vomiting (positive in this case)
- Bloating, flatulence
- Bowel habit changes — constipation, diarrhoea, alternating pattern
- Rectal bleeding, melaena, mucus per rectum
- Fever (positive in this case)

**Colorectal cancer red flag screen:**
- Unintentional weight loss
- Anorexia
- Palpable abdominal or rectal mass
- Rectal bleeding or change in bowel habit persisting more than four weeks
- Family history of colorectal cancer or inflammatory bowel disease
- Fatigue or pallor suggesting anaemia

**Surgical history:** Prior abdominal surgery (relevant to adhesions, post-operative complications).

**Medications:** Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, opioids, antibiotics — all may affect bowel function and GI symptoms.

In this case, all red flag gastrointestinal questions are negative. The positive findings — nausea and fever — are consistent with an infective or inflammatory process rather than a structural GI cause.

---

#### Group 2 — Renal and Urological Differentials (KUB)

Screen with three targeted questions:

- "Do you have any pain or burning when you pass urine?" — screens for urinary tract infection, urethritis
- "Are you passing urine more frequently than usual?" — screens for UTI, urethral irritation
- "Have you noticed any blood in your urine?" — screens for urolithiasis, bladder pathology, renal pathology

All three are negative in this case. This makes a urological cause less probable but does not exclude it entirely. Upper UTI (pyelonephritis) can present without lower urinary tract symptoms and would require further consideration if the overall picture were consistent.

---

#### Group 3 — Pelvic and Gynaecological Differentials

This is the highest-yield group for this patient. Approach systematically:

**Hernia screen (before proceeding to gynaecological questions):**
- "Have you noticed any lump or swelling in your groin?" — a right inguinal or femoral hernia can present with right lower quadrant pain, particularly if incarcerated.

**Gynaecological symptom screen:**

*Vaginal bleeding:*
- "Have you had any vaginal bleeding outside of your normal period?" — screens for ectopic pregnancy, cervical or uterine pathology, STI-related cervicitis.

*Vaginal discharge:*
- "Have you noticed any vaginal discharge?" — **Positive in this case.** This triggers mandatory in-depth probing.

**Probing the vaginal discharge — CCVO Framework:**

Once a positive discharge is identified, the candidate must characterise it efficiently using four key features:

1. **Colour:** "Can you describe the colour of the discharge?" — Yellow, green, white, clear, or blood-stained. Yellow-green suggests gonorrhoea or bacterial infection. Clear or white may be physiological or candidal.

2. **Consistency:** "Is it watery and runny, or thick and sticky?" — Thick, curd-like discharge suggests candidiasis. Watery-to-mucopurulent suggests bacterial STI.

3. **Odour:** "Does it have any unusual smell?" — Malodour is characteristic of bacterial vaginosis and polymicrobial pelvic infection. In PID, the discharge is often yellow, mucopurulent, and malodorous.

4. **Volume:** (If time permits) — Increased volume may indicate cervicitis or ascending infection.

In this case: yellow, watery, malodorous discharge — strongly suggestive of STI-associated PID.

**Menstrual history:**
- "When was your last menstrual period?" — Critical for two reasons: (1) to assess pregnancy probability; (2) to safely prescribe antibiotics. A period two weeks prior significantly reduces the probability of pregnancy and makes ectopic pregnancy extremely unlikely at this gestational age.
- Menstrual irregularities: Heavy periods (menorrhagia), intermenstrual bleeding, post-coital bleeding — all may indicate cervicitis or endometritis.

**Sexual history — elicited respectfully and non-judgementally:**
- "Are you currently sexually active?"
- "Do you use condoms or other barrier contraception consistently?" — Barrier method use significantly reduces STI risk.
- "Have you had more than one sexual partner?" — Multiple partners is a recognised independent risk factor for PID.
- "Have you ever been diagnosed with a sexually transmitted infection in the past?" — Prior STI is a significant risk factor for ascending infection and recurrence.

The sexual history in this case yields: sexually active, no condom use, multiple partners, possible prior STI diagnosis. This constellation constitutes a complete STI risk factor profile and strongly supports the working diagnosis of pelvic inflammatory disease.

---

### Remaining History — Social, Medications, Family

**Smoking:** Non-smoker in this case. Smoking increases susceptibility to cervical infection and impairs immune response.

**Alcohol:** Occasional (a glass of wine every few weeks) — clinically insignificant in this presentation, but must be documented.

**Recreational drugs:** Denies use. Important to ask in young patients presenting with pelvic pain, as intravenous drug use increases risk of disseminated gonococcal infection and hepatitis B/C co-infection.

**Medications:** None. If oral contraceptive pill were in use, this would not prevent STI acquisition.

**Allergies:** None documented.

**Past medical history:** Nil significant. Generally well.

**Family history:** Non-contributory in this case. Relevant in presentations where inflammatory bowel disease or hereditary cancer syndromes are being considered.

## Differential Diagnosis

The structured differential for right lower quadrant pain in a young woman spans four major anatomical categories. Candidates are expected to demonstrate breadth of differential thinking, then use positive and negative findings to systematically narrow the list.

| Category | Diagnosis | Key Distinguishing Features |
|----------|-----------|----------------------------|
| **Primary Diagnosis** | Pelvic Inflammatory Disease | Yellow malodorous discharge, fever, nausea, RLQ pain, no contraception, multiple partners, prior STI risk |
| **Common** | Appendicitis | Periumbilical-to-RLQ pain migration, anorexia, RLQ guarding, positive Rovsing/McBurney signs, no vaginal discharge |
| **Common** | Urinary Tract Infection / Pyelonephritis | Dysuria, frequency, haematuria, loin pain, costovertebral angle tenderness; absent vaginal discharge |
| **Common** | Gastroenteritis | Diarrhoea, vomiting, crampy diffuse pain, recent dietary exposure, contact history; typically no vaginal findings |
| **Important** | Ectopic Pregnancy | Missed period, positive bHCG, adnexal mass, shoulder tip pain; LMP two weeks ago makes this extremely unlikely currently |
| **Important** | Ovarian Cyst / Torsion | Sudden severe unilateral pain, nausea and vomiting, adnexal mass on examination; torsion is a surgical emergency |
| **Important** | Inflammatory Bowel Disease | Recurrent bloody diarrhoea, weight loss, extra-intestinal manifestations; chronic course with systemic features |
| **Important** | Inguinal / Femoral Hernia | Groin lump, pain worsening with Valsalva, absent vaginal symptoms; more common in older patients |
| **Must Not Miss** | Ectopic Pregnancy with Rupture | Haemodynamic instability, peritonism, positive bHCG, shoulder tip pain — immediate surgical emergency |
| **Must Not Miss** | Appendicitis with Perforation | Sudden relief then recurrence of pain, peritonism, sepsis features — surgical emergency requiring urgent review |
| **Must Not Miss** | Tubo-ovarian Abscess | Severe PID complication — high fever, peritoneal signs, palpable adnexal mass, sepsis; requires hospital admission and IV antibiotics |
| **Must Not Miss** | Bowel Obstruction | Absolute constipation, absent flatus, vomiting, distension, visible peristalsis; acute surgical emergency |

## Red Flags — Must Not Miss

- **Haemodynamic instability (hypotension, tachycardia, reduced consciousness)** — suggests sepsis, ruptured ectopic pregnancy, or ruptured tubo-ovarian abscess. Requires immediate resuscitation and surgical review. Do not proceed with routine history; activate emergency response.

- **Sudden severe onset of abdominal pain reaching maximum intensity within seconds to minutes** — suggests ovarian torsion, ruptured ectopic pregnancy, or ruptured ovarian cyst. Surgical emergency.

- **Positive pregnancy test with pelvic pain and vaginal bleeding** — ectopic pregnancy must be excluded immediately with serum bHCG and pelvic ultrasound regardless of last menstrual period date.

- **Pain migration from periumbilical region to RLQ with anorexia, guarding, and rebound tenderness** — classic appendicitis presentation; surgical consultation required urgently.

- **High fever (>38.5°C), rigors, tachycardia, and peritonism** — features of sepsis or tubo-ovarian abscess; requires hospital admission, IV antibiotics, surgical review, and blood cultures.

- **Severe PID symptoms unresponsive to oral antibiotics** — requires admission for IV cefotetan or cefoxitin-based regimens per therapeutic guidelines.

- **Unintentional weight loss, anorexia, rectal bleeding, or palpable abdominal mass in a young woman** — must prompt urgent investigation to exclude malignancy, including colorectal and ovarian cancer, even at this age.

- **Post-coital bleeding, intermenstrual bleeding, or contact bleeding** — cervical pathology including cervical cancer must be considered and appropriate referral made; do not dismiss in a young woman.

- **Shoulder tip pain (referred diaphragmatic irritation)** — suggests haemoperitoneum from ruptured ectopic pregnancy or ruptured ovarian cyst; requires urgent imaging.

## Investigations

### Bedside
- Urine dipstick — nitrites, leucocytes, blood, glucose, protein; excludes UTI and glycosuria
- Urine pregnancy test (bHCG) — if there is any clinical uncertainty about pregnancy status, regardless of stated LMP; if LMP is reliably two weeks ago and patient is not pregnant, this may be deferred but should not be omitted in clinical practice
- Blood glucose level — if diabetic ketoacidosis is in the differential
- Temperature, heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, respiratory rate — vital signs to assess for sepsis

### Bloods
- Full blood count — leucocytosis with left shift supports infective/inflammatory aetiology; anaemia raises concern for bleeding
- C-reactive protein — elevated in PID; supports inflammatory diagnosis
- Erythrocyte sedimentation rate — non-specific but elevated in systemic inflammation
- Serum bHCG — quantitative if ectopic pregnancy is being considered seriously
- Liver function tests and hepatitis serology — Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome (perihepatitis) is a rare complication of PID caused by ascent of gonococci or chlamydia to the hepatic capsule; hepatitis B and C screening as part of full STI screen
- Blood cultures — if sepsis is suspected

### Microbiology / Swabs
- **High vaginal swab for microscopy, culture, and sensitivity (MCS)** — identifies polymicrobial flora, bacterial vaginosis organisms
- **Endocervical or vulvovaginal swab for chlamydia and gonorrhoea nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT/PCR)** — most sensitive and specific test for these organisms; this is the single most important investigation in suspected PID
- Full STI screen including syphilis serology, HIV serology, hepatitis B surface antigen and surface antibody, hepatitis C antibody — all recommended at time of index STI presentation

### Imaging
- Transvaginal or transabdominal pelvic ultrasound — identifies tubo-ovarian abscess, free fluid in the pouch of Douglas, ovarian pathology (torsion, cyst), and excludes ectopic pregnancy
- CT abdomen and pelvis with contrast — if appendicitis, bowel pathology, or complex pelvic pathology is a significant differential and ultrasound is non-diagnostic

### Specialist Referral
- Gynaecology — if tubo-ovarian abscess is suspected, or if patient fails to respond to outpatient antibiotics within 72 hours
- General surgery — if appendicitis or other surgical emergency cannot be excluded
- Infectious diseases — if HIV is newly diagnosed or if complex STI management is required

## Management

### Diagnostic Principle

The diagnosis of pelvic inflammatory disease is **clinical**. Therapeutic guidelines nationally recognise that the clinical threshold for treatment must be low, as delaying treatment while awaiting investigation results increases the risk of tubal damage, infertility, ectopic pregnancy, and chronic pelvic pain. The presence of lower abdominal pain with cervical motion tenderness, uterine tenderness, or adnexal tenderness in a sexually active woman is sufficient to initiate empirical treatment.

### Severity Classification

**Non-severe PID** (suitable for outpatient management):
- Patient is systemically well
- Temperature less than 38.5°C
- No features of sepsis
- No evidence of tubo-ovarian abscess on imaging or examination
- Able to tolerate oral antibiotics

**Severe PID** (requires inpatient management):
- High fever, rigors, or sepsis criteria
- Tubo-ovarian abscess confirmed or suspected
- Unable to tolerate oral medication
- Failure to respond to outpatient antibiotics within 72 hours
- Pregnancy
- Diagnostic uncertainty requiring surgical review

### Outpatient Antibiotic Regimen (Non-Severe PID — Australian Therapeutic Guidelines)

The treatment regimen targets gonorrhoea, chlamydia, and polymicrobial vaginal flora simultaneously:

1. **Ceftriaxone 500 mg intramuscular injection, single dose (stat)** — covers gonorrhoea
2. **Doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily for 14 days** — covers chlamydia (note: chlamydia cervicitis alone is treated for seven days; PID extends to 14 days due to ascending infection)
3. **Metronidazole 400 mg orally twice daily for 14 days** — covers anaerobes and bacterial vaginosis organisms

Treatment is commenced empirically without awaiting swab results.

### Inpatient Regimen (Severe PID)

- Cefoxitin or ceftriaxone IV plus doxycycline IV/oral and metronidazole — as per current Therapeutic Guidelines Antibiotic edition (Australia); liaise with infectious diseases or gynaecology.

### Partner Notification and Treatment

- All recent sexual partners (within the preceding 60 days) must be notified, tested, and treated empirically. This is both a clinical and legal obligation in Australia under public health legislation.
- Partner treatment covers gonorrhoea and chlamydia regardless of their own test results.
- Contact tracing is facilitated through local sexual health services.

### Follow-up

- Clinical review at 72 hours — if no improvement, reassess diagnosis, consider imaging, and consider admission for IV therapy.
- Review swab results when available; escalate or modify antibiotic regimen if resistant organism identified.
- Repeat STI testing in three months is recommended to detect reinfection.
- Discuss contraception and long-term sexual health at follow-up.

## Communication and Patient Education

### Explaining the Diagnosis

Avoid using the abbreviation "PID" without explanation. Use plain language:

*"Based on everything you've told me and my examination findings, I believe you have a condition called pelvic inflammatory disease. This is an infection that has spread into your womb and fallopian tubes — the tubes that connect your ovaries to your womb. This type of infection is usually caused by a sexually transmitted infection, though not always. The reasons I'm making this diagnosis are the pain you've been having in your lower abdomen, the yellow discharge that has an unpleasant smell, and some risk factors I found when we were talking — including not using condoms regularly. On examination, your womb and tubes were tender when I examined you, which confirms there is inflammation in that area."*

### Explaining the Differentials

Briefly explain why alternative diagnoses are less likely, using the patient's own negative findings as justification:

- *"I did think about appendicitis — inflammation of a small organ in your bowel. I think this is less likely because typically with appendicitis, the pain starts around the belly button and then moves to the right side. Yours started in the right side from the beginning."*
- *"I also thought about an ectopic pregnancy — where a pregnancy forms in the tube rather than the womb. This seems very unlikely because your last period was only two weeks ago."*
- *"I considered a urine infection. This is less likely because you have no pain when passing urine and no increased frequency."*

### Addressing Concerns

Many patients will have concerns about the implications of an STI diagnosis for their relationships and future fertility. Address these sensitively:

- Acknowledge the emotional weight of the diagnosis without judgement
- Reassure that PID is treatable with antibiotics when caught early
- Explain that early treatment significantly reduces the risk of complications including infertility and chronic pain
- Inform the patient that partner notification is necessary and provide information about how this can be managed confidentially through sexual health services

### Written Information and Resources

- Provide written information about PID, STIs, and safe sexual practices
- Provide details of the local sexual health clinic for ongoing management and partner notification support
- Recommend the SHINE SA, Family Planning Australia, or local sexual health service resources as appropriate to the patient's state of residence

## AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

- **Acknowledge the opening statement before asking any questions.** Examiners specifically observe whether candidates respond empathically to the patient's initial narrative. Proceed with history only after explicitly validating the patient's distress — this takes fewer than fifteen seconds and is heavily weighted.

- **Always ask about pain migration in RLQ pain.** The single most discriminating feature between PID and appendicitis in the history is whether the pain started centrally and migrated, or was localised to the RLQ from onset. Do not omit this question.

- **When you find a positive finding, probe it before moving on.** The examiner is specifically watching for this. If a patient mentions vaginal discharge, immediately characterise it (colour, consistency, odour) before proceeding to the next differential group.

- **Use the CCVO framework for vaginal discharge.** Colour, Consistency, (Volume), Odour. Four questions, thirty seconds. This demonstrates systematic clinical reasoning.

- **Never skip the sexual history in a young woman with pelvic pain.** Risk factors for STI — number of partners, condom use, prior STI diagnosis, recent partner change — are diagnostic criteria for PID and must be elicited.

- **Do not explain the diagnosis using clinical abbreviations.** "PID", "STI", "LMP" are not patient-friendly language. Explain each concept in plain terms: "an infection in your womb and tubes", "a sexually transmitted infection — one that can pass between sexual partners", "your last period".

- **The LMP is not just an obstetric question — it is a safety question.** Asking the LMP excludes pregnancy before prescribing potentially teratogenic antibiotics (doxycycline is contraindicated in pregnancy) and stratifies the risk of ectopic pregnancy.

- **In a six-minute station, a structured three-group differential approach (GI / KUB / Pelvic) prevents both omission and time wastage.** Complete each group efficiently before moving to the next; do not return to a group once you have progressed unless a positive finding demands it.

- **Empirical treatment is the correct approach in PID — do not wait for swab results.** In the AMC examination, candidates who state they will "wait for results before treating" will lose marks. State clearly that diagnosis is clinical and treatment should commence immediately.

- **Ceftriaxone + doxycycline + metronidazole is the standard outpatient triple therapy.** Know the doses and duration: ceftriaxone 500 mg IM stat; doxycycline 100 mg BD for 14 days; metronidazole 400 mg BD for 14 days. These differ from the shorter courses used for uncomplicated chlamydia.

- **Always mention full STI screening alongside the targeted swabs.** HIV, syphilis, hepatitis B and C are part of a complete STI screen at any index presentation and demonstrate comprehensive clinical management.

- **In a three-minute history taking station, prioritise pelvic and STI history over comprehensive GI red flag screening.** When time is severely limited and the clinical picture already points strongly toward a pelvic cause, asking the five highest-yield questions — LMP, discharge characterisation, sexual activity, condom use, prior STI — is more valuable than completing the entire GI cancer red flag screen.

## Safety Net

Provide clear, specific safety net instructions before concluding the consultation:

**Return to the emergency department immediately if any of the following develop:**

- Pain becomes significantly worse or spreads across the entire abdomen
- Fever rises above 38.5°C or you experience rigors (uncontrollable shaking)
- You feel faint, dizzy, or collapse
- You begin vomiting and cannot keep the antibiotics down
- You develop shoulder tip pain
- You feel generally unwell and deteriorating

**Follow-up plan:**
- Review appointment in 72 hours to assess clinical response to antibiotics
- If no improvement at 72 hours, attend the emergency department or be reviewed by a gynaecologist
- Return for swab results review when available
- Repeat STI testing at three months

**Ongoing sexual health:**
- Complete the full 14-day antibiotic course even if symptoms resolve early — incomplete courses contribute to treatment failure and antibiotic resistance
- Abstain from sexual intercourse until you and your partner(s) have both completed treatment and been reviewed
- All recent sexual partners must be notified and tested — your local sexual health clinic can assist with this confidentially
- Use condoms consistently and correctly with all partners to reduce the risk of reinfection

**Additional resources:**
- Local sexual health clinic contact details (state-specific — SHINE SA, Melbourne Sexual Health Centre, Sydney Sexual Health Centre, Family Planning Queensland, etc.)
- Provide written information in an appropriate health literacy format
