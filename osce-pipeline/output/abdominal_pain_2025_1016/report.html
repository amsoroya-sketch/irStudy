Acute Pancreatitis — Upper Abdominal Pain History Taking Station

Clinical Presentation

A 40-year-old male presents with severe upper abdominal pain following a large meal accompanied by alcohol intake. The pain is epigastric, radiates to the back, and is partially relieved by leaning forward. This constellation of features, combined with recent use of a GLP-1 receptor agonist (semaglutide/Ozempic) and a history of prior cholecystectomy, creates a diagnostically rich scenario that requires systematic, structured history taking to correctly identify acute pancreatitis as the primary diagnosis.

This station is clinically important because acute pancreatitis represents one of the most common causes of acute upper abdominal pain requiring hospital admission in Australia, with a rising incidence attributable to alcohol use, gallstone disease, and increasingly, GLP-1 receptor agonist pharmacotherapy. The AMC Clinical Examination has featured pancreatitis cases with notable frequency in recent examination cycles, often with deliberate diagnostic distractors embedded in the history — such as concurrent alcohol exposure, prior cholecystectomy, weight loss, and comorbid metabolic conditions — to differentiate candidates who take a systematic approach from those who anchor prematurely on a single finding.

The clinical challenge in this station is threefold: first, the candidate must apply a disciplined SOCRATES framework to characterise the pain precisely; second, they must systematically exclude competing diagnoses including biliary colic, cholecystitis, and peptic ulcer disease; and third, they must integrate medication history — specifically GLP-1 receptor agonist use — as a causative factor, a clinically contemporary and examinationally high-yield association that distinguishes well-prepared candidates.

Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

This is a history taking station with scores weighted predominantly toward structured history taking (8 marks), physical examination counselling (4 marks), and communication skills (2 marks). The examiner assesses whether the candidate can:

  1. Open the consultation professionally — introduce themselves, confirm patient identity, establish rapport, and clarify the presenting complaint without bias or premature hypothesis anchoring.

  2. Apply a systematic pain history — use SOCRATES comprehensively, with deliberate emphasis on the features that discriminate between the principal differentials in upper abdominal pain.

  3. Explore the differentials methodically — screen positively and negatively for biliary, pancreatic, gastroduodenal, and vascular pathology through targeted questioning.

  4. Elicit ICE — determine the patient's Ideas (what they think is causing the pain), Concerns (what they are worried about), and Expectations (what they hope to achieve from the consultation), as this informs the communication component of the station.

  5. Screen for red flags — ask specifically about features suggesting malignancy, vascular catastrophe, or systemic illness that require immediate escalation.

  6. Close the consultation professionally — summarise findings, give the patient an opportunity to ask questions, explain next steps, and provide a safety net.

The structured four-stage approach expected for this station is:

  • Stage 1 — Introduction: Professional greeting, consent, and one critical clarifying question before proceeding.
  • Stage 2 — Pain exploration: Full SOCRATES with targeted aggravating factor emphasis (food, posture, alcohol).
  • Stage 3 — Differential screening: Systematic questioning across biliary, pancreatic, peptic ulcer, and general gastrointestinal domains, followed by red flag screening.
  • Stage 4 — Closing: ICE, summarisation, explanation of next steps, and safety netting.

Systematic Approach

Stage 1 — Introduction and Clarification

Before entering the structured pain history, the candidate must attend to one non-negotiable preliminary step: establishing the severity and acuity of the presentation to determine whether immediate resuscitation is required before the full history can proceed. In this station, the patient is haemodynamically stable, but the examiner expects the candidate to demonstrate awareness that acute severe abdominal pain may represent a surgical or vascular emergency.

Opening template:

"Good morning, I'm Dr [Name], one of the doctors here today. I understand you've been experiencing some abdominal pain — before I ask you some detailed questions, I'd just like to check: are you in severe pain right now, and are you feeling faint or short of breath?"

This brief screen for haemodynamic instability (AVPU, pallor, diaphoresis) is appropriate at the outset even in a history-only station, as it reflects safe clinical practice and scores highly on communication marks.

One clinically critical question that must not be omitted at the introduction stage in this scenario is: "Are you currently taking any regular medications, particularly any new medications prescribed in the last six to twelve months?" Obtaining medication history early is essential here because GLP-1 receptor agonist use (semaglutide, liraglutide, dulaglutide) is a causative factor for acute pancreatitis and must be considered before the differential is narrowed.

Stage 2 — SOCRATES Pain Assessment

The SOCRATES framework must be applied systematically, with particular clinical emphasis on site and radiation, aggravating factors (specifically food, posture, and alcohol), and associated features.

Site
- "Can you point to exactly where the pain is located?"
- In acute pancreatitis: epigastric, often with a band-like quality extending across the upper abdomen.
- Distinguish from right upper quadrant predominance (biliary) or right iliac fossa radiation.
- This patient localises to the epigastrium — consistent with pancreatic or gastroduodenal pathology.

Onset
- "Did the pain come on suddenly or gradually? What were you doing when it started?"
- Pancreatitis: typically builds over 30–60 minutes following a trigger (large fatty meal, alcohol).
- Biliary colic: sudden, colicky, often nocturnal.
- Perforated peptic ulcer: instantaneous, knife-like onset.
- This patient: pain onset following a large meal with wine — consistent with pancreatitis or biliary pathology.

Character
- "Can you describe the pain — is it sharp, dull, cramping, burning, or a pressure sensation?"
- Pancreatitis: constant, severe, boring or deep aching quality.
- Biliary colic: cramping, waxing and waning.
- Peptic ulcer disease: gnawing, burning.
- This patient: severe, constant pain — favours pancreatitis over biliary colic.

Radiation
- "Does the pain spread anywhere — to your back, shoulder, or jaw?"
- Pancreatitis: posterior radiation to the back is the hallmark — caused by retroperitoneal inflammation of the pancreatic body and tail.
- Biliary colic/cholecystitis: right shoulder tip radiation (diaphragmatic irritation via phrenic nerve).
- Aortic aneurysm: tearing pain radiating to the back or flanks.
- This patient: epigastric pain radiating to the back — strongly supports pancreatitis.

Alleviating Factors — CRITICAL
- "Does anything make the pain better — changing position, eating, drinking, leaning forward, or taking antacids?"
- Pancreatitis: classically relieved or partially ameliorated by leaning forward (reduces pressure on the retroperitoneum); worsened by lying supine.
- Peptic ulcer disease: antacids may relieve; food may worsen (duodenal ulcer improves with food; gastric ulcer worsens).
- Biliary colic: typically no positional relief.
- This patient: found sitting and leaning forward — a non-verbal cue with high specificity for pancreatitis.

Timing
- "Is the pain constant, or does it come and go? How long has it been present?"
- Pancreatitis: constant, persistent — hours to days.
- Biliary colic: episodic, lasting 30 minutes to 6 hours, then resolving.
- This patient: constant pain since large meal — pancreatitis pattern.

Exacerbating Factors — CRITICAL
- "Does eating or drinking make it worse? Does movement worsen it?"
- In pancreatitis: food and alcohol are the principal triggers and exacerbating factors.
- Specifically enquire about alcohol volume, frequency, and the pattern of intake at the precipitating meal.
- "How much alcohol did you have with your meal today?" — quantify in standard drinks.
- Ask about background alcohol use: "Do you drink alcohol regularly? On average, how many drinks per week?"

Severity
- "On a scale of 0 to 10, with 0 being no pain and 10 being the worst pain imaginable, how would you rate this pain right now?"
- Pancreatitis pain is typically rated 7–10/10, often prompting emergency presentation.
- Use pain score to guide urgency of investigation and management.

Stage 3 — Systematic Differential Screening

After completing SOCRATES, the candidate should systematically screen for each differential diagnosis group using targeted questions.

Group 1 — Biliary Pathology (Biliary Colic / Acute Cholecystitis)

Prior cholecystectomy 10 years ago substantially reduces — but does not eliminate — biliary pathology as a cause (retained stones, common bile duct stones, post-cholecystectomy syndrome, or sphincter of Oddi dysfunction remain possible).

  • "Have you had problems with your gallbladder in the past? I understand you had your gallbladder removed — was there any explanation given for why?"
  • "Have you had similar pain in the past that was related to eating fatty foods?"
  • "Have you noticed yellow discolouration of your eyes or skin (jaundice), dark urine, or pale stools?" — suggests common bile duct obstruction (choledocholithiasis).
  • "Have you had any fevers or chills?" — cholangitis triad: fever, jaundice, right upper quadrant pain (Charcot's triad).

Group 2 — Pancreatic Pathology (Acute Pancreatitis / Chronic Pancreatitis / Pancreatic Malignancy)

  • "Has this ever happened before? Have you ever been told you had inflammation of the pancreas?"
  • "Do you use alcohol regularly? Was the amount you had today typical for you?"
  • "Are you taking any new medications — particularly any injections for diabetes or weight management?" — GLP-1 receptor agonist (semaglutide/Ozempic) is a causative factor for pancreatitis.
  • "Have you had any blood tests showing high triglyceride or calcium levels?" — hypertriglyceridaemia and hypercalcaemia are metabolic causes of pancreatitis.
  • "Have you noticed any changes in your bowel motions — particularly pale, greasy stools that are difficult to flush?" — steatorrhoea suggests exocrine pancreatic insufficiency; may indicate chronic pancreatitis.

Group 3 — Peptic Ulcer Disease (Gastric Ulcer / Duodenal Ulcer / GORD)

  • "Do you experience a burning or gnawing sensation in the upper abdomen between meals or at night, and does eating or drinking milk make it better?" — duodenal ulcer pattern.
  • "Do you use anti-inflammatory medications regularly — ibuprofen, naproxen, aspirin, or similar?" — NSAID use is the most common cause of peptic ulceration in Australia after H. pylori.
  • "Have you taken any antacids or acid-reducing medications? Have they helped?"
  • "Have you had any vomiting of blood, or noticed black or tarry stools?" — upper gastrointestinal haemorrhage from peptic ulceration.

Medication History — Essential in This Case

  • "Can you tell me all the medications you are currently taking, including any injections?"
  • This patient is on: thyroxine (for hypothyroidism — not hyperthyroidism; the transcript appears to have a transcription error given the context), and semaglutide (Ozempic) — a GLP-1 receptor agonist prescribed for type 2 diabetes or weight management.
  • GLP-1 receptor agonists and pancreatitis: The TGA-approved product information for semaglutide and related agents lists acute pancreatitis as a known adverse effect. The mechanism involves increased pancreatic ductal pressure and direct stimulation of acinar cells via GLP-1 receptor pathways. Candidates should be aware that this association has become examinationally relevant as prescription rates of GLP-1 receptor agonists have risen substantially in Australian clinical practice.
  • "How long have you been on Ozempic? Have you had any problems with it in the past?"
  • "Has anyone warned you about the risk of pancreatitis with this medication?"

General Gastrointestinal Screen

  • Nausea and vomiting: "Have you felt sick or vomited?" — vomiting is near-universal in acute pancreatitis and does not relieve the pain (distinguishing feature).
  • Bowel habit: "Have your bowels been normal?" — constipation and ileus can accompany severe pancreatitis.
  • Appetite: "How has your appetite been?"

Systemic Screen and Red Flag Enquiry

  • Fever: "Have you had any fevers?" — suggests infected pancreatic necrosis, cholangitis, or abscess.
  • Weight loss: "Have you lost any weight recently — was it intentional?"
  • This case: 3 kg over one month, intentional — in the context of semaglutide use and active weight management, this is clinically expected and not a red flag for malignancy.
  • However, the candidate must ask the follow-up question: "Was the weight loss planned? Has your appetite changed?" to correctly contextualise the finding.
  • Unintentional weight loss of >5% body weight in 6 months in a patient with upper abdominal pain and new-onset diabetes would warrant investigation for pancreatic malignancy.
  • Jaundice: "Have you noticed yellowing of your eyes or skin?"
  • Family history: "Has anyone in your family had pancreatic problems, pancreatitis, or pancreatic cancer?"
  • Social history: "Do you smoke? How much alcohol do you drink per week?"

Stage 4 — ICE and Closing

Ideas
- "What do you think might be causing this pain?"
- Many patients presenting with upper abdominal pain following alcohol and a large meal will attribute symptoms to "indigestion" or "too much food." The candidate should explore this without dismissing the patient's perspective.

Concerns
- "Is there anything in particular you are worried about?"
- Patients on Ozempic may be concerned about medication side effects or whether to continue the drug. Others may be concerned about the possibility of something serious such as cancer, particularly if weight loss has occurred.

Expectations
- "What were you hoping we could do for you today?"
- Most patients will expect pain relief and an explanation of the cause. Some may request that the pain medication be their primary concern.

Closing Summary
- Summarise the key findings back to the patient in plain language.
- Explain that further tests are required to confirm the diagnosis.
- Provide a clear safety net regarding what to do if symptoms worsen.
- Thank the patient and confirm that a doctor will return to discuss results.

Redflags Panel
Clinically Enriched

Red Flags — Must Not Miss

  • Haemodynamic instability (tachycardia, hypotension, diaphoresis) — indicates severe acute pancreatitis with systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), haemorrhagic pancreatitis, or ruptured vascular catastrophe. Requires immediate IV access, fluid resuscitation, and urgent surgical review.

  • Fever above 38.5°C with upper abdominal pain and jaundice — raises the diagnosis of ascending cholangitis (Charcot's triad), a life-threatening biliary emergency. Requires urgent ERCP and intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics.

  • Tearing or ripping pain radiating to the back in a patient with known or suspected aortic disease — ruptured or leaking abdominal aortic aneurysm is a surgical emergency with mortality approaching 90% without immediate intervention. Do not delay for investigations; urgent vascular surgery referral.

  • Peritonitic abdomen (board-like rigidity, guarding, rebound tenderness) — indicates visceral perforation or haemorrhagic pancreatitis with peritoneal involvement. Requires nil by mouth, IV antibiotics, and surgical consultation.

  • Pallor, jaundice, or scleral icterus — suggests haemolysis, hepatic pathology, or biliary obstruction. Urgent LFTs, haemolysis screen, and imaging required.

  • Unintentional weight loss exceeding 5% body weight in 6 months combined with upper abdominal pain — raises the possibility of pancreatic adenocarcinoma. CT abdomen with contrast (pancreas protocol) and serum CA 19-9 are required. Note: In this case, the weight loss was intentional in the context of semaglutide therapy — this must be confirmed explicitly by the candidate.

  • New-onset diabetes mellitus in a patient with upper abdominal pain — may represent endocrine pancreatic destruction from chronic pancreatitis or pancreatic malignancy. Fasting BSL and HbA1c required.

  • Grey Turner's sign (flank bruising) or Cullen's sign (periumbilical bruising) — indicates retroperitoneal haemorrhage from haemorrhagic pancreatitis; associated with high mortality and ICU-level care.

  • ECG changes consistent with inferior MI — 10–15% of inferior myocardial infarctions present with epigastric pain and vomiting without chest pain. A 12-lead ECG is mandatory in all adult presentations of upper abdominal pain.

  • Altered mental status or confusion — in the context of acute pancreatitis, may indicate Reynold's pentad (ascending cholangitis with septic encephalopathy), severe electrolyte disturbance (hypocalcaemia in pancreatitis), or alcohol intoxication/withdrawal.

Management Flow
Clinically Enriched

Investigations

Bedside:
- 12-lead ECG — exclude inferior myocardial infarction
- Urinalysis — glucosuria (new-onset diabetes in chronic pancreatitis), haematuria (renal calculi)
- Capillary blood glucose — hypoglycaemia or hyperglycaemia
- Urine beta-hCG (females of reproductive age) — exclude ectopic pregnancy
- Oxygen saturation and vital signs — haemodynamic assessment

Bloods:
- Full blood count (FBC) — leucocytosis (infection/inflammation), haematocrit (haemoconcentration in severe pancreatitis)
- Urea, electrolytes, creatinine (UEC) — renal function, guide fluid management
- Liver function tests (LFTs) — elevated ALT raises suspicion of gallstone pancreatitis (ALT >150 IU/L has >95% positive predictive value for biliary aetiology); elevated bilirubin suggests choledocholithiasis
- Serum lipase — the investigation of choice for acute pancreatitis in Australian practice (preferred over amylase due to superior sensitivity and specificity); a level >3× upper limit of normal is diagnostic
- Serum amylase — elevated but less specific than lipase; normalises earlier in the disease course
- C-reactive protein (CRP) — elevation to >150 mg/L at 48 hours correlates with severe pancreatitis and pancreatic necrosis
- Serum calcium — hypocalcaemia is a severity marker in acute pancreatitis (fat saponification); hypercalcaemia may be causal
- Fasting triglycerides — hypertriglyceridaemia (>11 mmol/L) is a recognised cause of acute pancreatitis
- Blood glucose — assess for pancreatic endocrine dysfunction
- Blood cultures — if fever present, prior to antibiotics
- Coagulation studies (PT/INR, APTT) — if haemorrhagic pancreatitis or hepatic pathology suspected
- Group and hold — if haemorrhagic pancreatitis or surgical intervention anticipated

Imaging:
- Ultrasound abdomen — first-line imaging; identifies gallstones, common bile duct dilatation, and free fluid; pancreas may be poorly visualised due to bowel gas
- CT abdomen and pelvis with IV contrast (pancreas protocol) — gold standard for assessing pancreatic necrosis, peripancreatic collections, and vascular complications; indicated when diagnosis is uncertain or if clinical deterioration occurs at 48–72 hours; the Modified CT Severity Index (MCTSI) guides prognostication
- MRCP (Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography) — superior to CT for visualising the biliary tree, pancreatic duct strictures, and small stones; no radiation; preferred in younger patients and those with renal impairment
- Chest X-ray — identify pleural effusion (left-sided is a recognised complication of acute pancreatitis), basal atelectasis, or free subdiaphragmatic air (perforation)

Specialist:
- Gastroenterology/Hepatology consultation — management of severe or recurrent pancreatitis, consideration of ERCP
- ERCP — urgent (within 24 hours) if ascending cholangitis or persistent biliary obstruction is confirmed; therapeutic stone extraction and sphincterotomy
- General surgery — severe pancreatitis with necrosis, suspected perforation, vascular complications
- Endocrinology — if new-onset diabetes or significant glycaemic dysregulation
- Dietitian — nutritional support; enteral nutrition (nasojejunal) is preferred over parenteral nutrition in severe pancreatitis
- Review and cessation of semaglutide — discuss with prescribing physician; the TGA recommends discontinuation of GLP-1 receptor agonists if acute pancreatitis is confirmed

Management

Acute Management (Emergency Department / Hospital Admission)

Nil by mouth initially — bowel rest reduces pancreatic stimulation; however, early enteral feeding via nasojejunal tube is preferred in severe pancreatitis once haemodynamically stable, as it maintains gut integrity and reduces infective complications.

Intravenous fluid resuscitation — aggressive early fluid resuscitation is the cornerstone of acute pancreatitis management:
- Hartmann's solution (Lactated Ringer's) is preferred over normal saline; evidence supports lower rates of SIRS with balanced crystalloid
- Target urine output ≥0.5 mL/kg/hour
- Reassess every 1–2 hours in the acute phase; avoid over-resuscitation in patients with cardiac or renal comorbidities

Analgesia:
- IV opioid analgesia (morphine or fentanyl) for severe pain — do not withhold adequate analgesia
- Regular paracetamol as adjunct
- Consider patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) for ongoing severe pain
- NSAIDs should be avoided given the risk of renal impairment and gastrointestinal complications

Antiemetics — ondansetron or metoclopramide for nausea and vomiting

Monitoring:
- Regular vital signs — every 1–4 hours depending on severity
- Fluid balance chart
- Daily bloods including CRP at 48 hours (prognostic)
- Monitor for complications: hypocalcaemia, hyperglycaemia, acute kidney injury, respiratory failure (ARDS)

Severity Assessment

Use validated scoring systems to guide the level of care:
- Ranson's criteria (on admission and at 48 hours) — score ≥3 indicates severe pancreatitis
- BISAP score (BUN >25, Impaired mental status, SIRS, Age >60, Pleural effusion) — score ≥3 correlates with increased mortality
- APACHE II — comprehensive; used in ICU settings
- Revised Atlanta Classification — mild (no organ failure, no local complications), moderately severe (transient organ failure <48 hours or local complications), severe (persistent organ failure >48 hours)

Patients with severe pancreatitis should be managed in a high-dependency or intensive care unit.

Treatment of Underlying Cause

  • Alcohol-related pancreatitis: brief intervention, alcohol cessation counselling, referral to addiction medicine or alcohol and other drugs (AOD) services; Australian guidelines support motivational interviewing
  • GLP-1 receptor agonist-induced pancreatitis: cease semaglutide immediately; notify prescribing physician; document as an adverse drug reaction and report to the TGA Adverse Drug Reaction System (if appropriate); alternative medications for glycaemic management or weight loss should be reviewed with endocrinology
  • Gallstone pancreatitis (if confirmed): cholecystectomy during the same hospital admission (once the acute episode has resolved) is the standard of care in Australia to prevent recurrence; ERCP if common bile duct stones are confirmed

Ongoing Management

  • Nutritional support: early enteral nutrition (nasojejunal feeds) reduces infective complications and mortality; total parenteral nutrition is reserved for when enteral routes are not tolerated
  • Antibiotics: not routinely indicated in sterile pancreatitis; reserved for confirmed infected pancreatic necrosis (CT-guided fine needle aspiration to confirm infection); imipenem or meropenem for confirmed infected necrosis
  • Interventional radiology or surgical debridement: for walled-off pancreatic necrosis or abscess unresponsive to conservative management (step-up approach: endoscopic or percutaneous drainage before surgical necrosectomy)
  • Pancreatic enzyme supplementation (creon): if exocrine pancreatic insufficiency develops, particularly with recurrent or chronic pancreatitis
  • Insulin therapy: if endocrine pancreatic function is significantly compromised
  • Alcohol cessation: mandatory; recurrence rates approach 50% with continued alcohol use

Communication and Patient Education

Explaining the Diagnosis:
Explain to the patient in clear, non-technical language that the pancreas — a gland behind the stomach — has become inflamed, likely triggered by the combination of a large fatty meal, alcohol, and potentially a medication side effect from Ozempic.

"Your pancreas — a gland that sits behind your stomach and helps with digestion — has become inflamed. This is called acute pancreatitis. It can be caused by alcohol, large meals, and sometimes by certain medications, including the Ozempic injection you've been using. We need to do some blood tests and a scan to confirm this and check how severe it is."

Addressing Medication Concerns:
Patients on semaglutide who develop pancreatitis may be alarmed, particularly if they have been using it for weight management or glycaemic control. The candidate should:
- Acknowledge the association between GLP-1 receptor agonists and pancreatitis in accessible language
- Explain that the medication will need to be stopped immediately and their prescriber will discuss alternative treatments
- Reassure that alternative medications for their underlying condition exist
- Advise them not to restart Ozempic without specialist review

Addressing Alcohol Use:
Communicate the association between alcohol and pancreatitis without blame, using a motivational, non-judgmental approach consistent with Australian brief intervention standards:

"Alcohol can trigger pancreatitis and make it more severe. I want to make sure you have all the information you need to make decisions about your alcohol use going forward. Would you be open to having a conversation about that with one of our specialists?"

Weight Loss Contextualisation:
Clarify to the patient that the weight loss identified in the history appears to be intentional (related to semaglutide therapy) and is not, in this context, alarming for malignancy. However, advise that if weight loss continues after the medication is ceased, further investigation would be warranted.

Written Information:
- Pancreatitis patient information leaflet (Gastroenterological Society of Australia [GESA] or hospital-approved resource)
- Information on safe alcohol limits (Australian Alcohol Guidelines: no more than 10 standard drinks per week, no more than 4 on any single occasion)
- Information on what to expect during the hospital admission
- Follow-up plan with GP and/or gastroenterology

AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

  • Ask about GLP-1 receptor agonist use explicitly. In 2025 Australian clinical practice, semaglutide (Ozempic), liraglutide, and dulaglutide are increasingly prescribed. Pancreatitis is a documented adverse effect listed in the TGA product information, and examiners will specifically reward candidates who identify this medication as a contributory cause. Never omit a thorough medication history in any abdominal pain station.

  • Do not anchor prematurely on the most obvious trigger. The presence of alcohol and a fatty meal is a powerful diagnostic cue, but it is also a deliberate distractor designed to make candidates bypass the medication history. The examiner will penalise candidates who miss semaglutide as a causative factor by failing to ask about injectable medications.

  • Clarify the nature of weight loss before treating it as a red flag. Three kilograms over one month is clinically significant only if unintentional. In this case, intentional weight loss on a GLP-1 receptor agonist is expected and does not warrant malignancy workup as a primary concern. Failing to ask "Was this intentional?" and then escalating unnecessarily to cancer investigations reflects poor clinical reasoning.

  • Distinguish pancreatitis from biliary colic through postural relief questioning. The single most discriminating question in this differential is whether leaning forward relieves the pain. This positional finding is pathognomonic for pancreatitis caused by retroperitoneal inflammation. Always ask: "Does any position make the pain better or worse?"

  • A prior cholecystectomy does not exclude biliary pathology. Retained common bile duct stones, recurrent stones, sphincter of Oddi dysfunction, and post-cholecystectomy syndrome all remain in the differential after cholecystectomy. Ask about jaundice, pale stools, and dark urine to screen for biliary obstruction.

  • Order serum lipase, not amylase, as your primary pancreatic investigation. Australian practice guidelines and the GESA position lipase as the preferred pancreatic enzyme test due to its superior sensitivity and specificity, and its longer elevation window (persists 7–14 days versus 3–5 days for amylase). Specifying lipase in the investigations section demonstrates contemporary clinical knowledge.

  • Always include a 12-lead ECG in the investigation plan for upper abdominal pain. Up to 15% of inferior myocardial infarctions present with epigastric pain as the predominant symptom, with or without nausea and vomiting. Failing to include ECG in your investigation plan for upper abdominal pain is a systematic error that examiners note.

  • Use the Revised Atlanta Classification when discussing severity. Stating that you would classify severity using validated scoring tools (Ranson's, BISAP, Modified CT Severity Index) demonstrates a systematic, evidence-based approach and impresses examiners in the closing or management discussion component of the station.

  • Demonstrate awareness of the alcohol brief intervention framework. Australian clinical practice standards require non-judgmental, motivational counselling for alcohol-related presentations. Avoid phrasing such as "You need to stop drinking" — instead, use motivational interviewing principles: "How do you feel about your alcohol use?" and "Would you be open to some support?"

  • Report the adverse drug reaction. Demonstrating knowledge that semaglutide-induced pancreatitis should be documented as an ADR and reported to the TGA Adverse Drug Reaction Reporting System (or via the prescribing specialist) reflects comprehensive patient safety awareness and scores communication marks.

  • Do not omit ICE. Even when the clinical history is diagnostically clear, the examiner will observe whether ICE is systematically elicited. Many candidates rush through the closing in complex histories and sacrifice ICE marks. Allocate 60–90 seconds explicitly for Ideas, Concerns, and Expectations before closing the case.

  • Vomiting in pancreatitis does not relieve the pain. This distinguishing feature differentiates pancreatitis from gastroenteritis and bowel obstruction, where vomiting may transiently relieve discomfort. If the examiner presents vomiting as a symptom, ask explicitly: "Did vomiting make the pain better, or did it stay just as bad?"

Safety Net

Before concluding the consultation, the candidate must provide a clear and specific safety net. This is a mandatory component of the AMC Clinical Examination station and is assessed under both the history taking and communication domains.

Immediate Return to Emergency Department / Call Ambulance (000) If:
- Pain becomes unbearable and unresponsive to prescribed analgesia
- New onset of vomiting blood (haematemesis) or passage of black, tarry, or bloody stools (melaena / haematochezia)
- Sudden worsening of abdominal pain suggesting perforation or haemorrhagic pancreatitis
- Development of high fever (>38.5°C) with rigors — may indicate infected pancreatic necrosis or cholangitis
- Signs of haemodynamic compromise: extreme dizziness, fainting, rapid weak pulse, profuse sweating
- Confusion, altered consciousness, or extreme agitation
- Difficulty breathing or reduced urine output (oliguria)
- Yellow discolouration of eyes or skin developing suddenly

Medication Instructions:
- Cease semaglutide (Ozempic) immediately and do not restart without specialist review
- Continue thyroxine as prescribed; this medication is unrelated to the current presentation
- Do not take NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) while pancreatitis is active — they may worsen renal function and gastrointestinal integrity

Follow-Up:
- Inpatient review by gastroenterology or general surgery during hospital admission
- GP follow-up within one week of discharge
- Fasting lipid profile (including triglycerides) and calcium levels to be checked after acute episode resolves, to screen for metabolic causes
- Repeat liver function tests and ultrasound in 4–6 weeks if gallstone aetiology is under investigation
- Endocrinology or GP review to determine safe alternative therapy for weight management or glycaemic control in place of semaglutide
- GESA patient fact sheet on pancreatitis to be provided on discharge
- Contact number for the hospital's gastroenterology outpatient clinic for urgent concerns post-discharge

Key Facts & Clinical Priorities

Red Flags — Must Not Miss

  • Haemodynamic instability (tachycardia, hypotension, diaphoresis)
  • Fever above 38.5°C with upper abdominal pain and jaundice
  • Tearing or ripping pain radiating to the back in a patient with known or suspected aortic disease
  • Peritonitic abdomen (board-like rigidity, guarding, rebound tenderness)
  • Pallor, jaundice, or scleral icterus
  • Unintentional weight loss exceeding 5% body weight in 6 months combined with upper abdominal pain
  • New-onset diabetes mellitus in a patient with upper abdominal pain
  • Grey Turner's sign (flank bruising) or Cullen's sign (periumbilical bruising)

Differential Diagnoses

  • Acute pancreatitis
  • Biliary colic
  • Acute cholecystitis
  • Peptic ulcer disease (gastric or duodenal)
  • Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease
  • Choledocholithiasis
  • Ascending cholangitis
  • Chronic pancreatitis with acute exacerbation

AMC Exam Key Points

  • Ask about GLP-1 receptor agonist use explicitly. In 2025 Australian clinical practice, semaglutide (Ozempic), liraglutide, and dulaglutide are increasingly prescribed. Pancreatitis is a documented adverse effect listed in the TGA product information, and examiners will specifically reward candidates who identify this medication as a contributory cause. Never omit a thorough medication history in any abdominal pain station.
  • Do not anchor prematurely on the most obvious trigger. The presence of alcohol and a fatty meal is a powerful diagnostic cue, but it is also a deliberate distractor designed to make candidates bypass the medication history. The examiner will penalise candidates who miss semaglutide as a causative factor by failing to ask about injectable medications.
  • Clarify the nature of weight loss before treating it as a red flag. Three kilograms over one month is clinically significant only if unintentional. In this case, intentional weight loss on a GLP-1 receptor agonist is expected and does not warrant malignancy workup as a primary concern. Failing to ask *"Was this intentional?"* and then escalating unnecessarily to cancer investigations reflects poor clinical reasoning.
  • Distinguish pancreatitis from biliary colic through postural relief questioning. The single most discriminating question in this differential is whether leaning forward relieves the pain. This positional finding is pathognomonic for pancreatitis caused by retroperitoneal inflammation. Always ask: *"Does any position make the pain better or worse?"*
  • A prior cholecystectomy does not exclude biliary pathology. Retained common bile duct stones, recurrent stones, sphincter of Oddi dysfunction, and post-cholecystectomy syndrome all remain in the differential after cholecystectomy. Ask about jaundice, pale stools, and dark urine to screen for biliary obstruction.
  • Order serum lipase, not amylase, as your primary pancreatic investigation. Australian practice guidelines and the GESA position lipase as the preferred pancreatic enzyme test due to its superior sensitivity and specificity, and its longer elevation window (persists 7–14 days versus 3–5 days for amylase). Specifying lipase in the investigations section demonstrates contemporary clinical knowledge.
  • Always include a 12-lead ECG in the investigation plan for upper abdominal pain. Up to 15% of inferior myocardial infarctions present with epigastric pain as the predominant symptom, with or without nausea and vomiting. Failing to include ECG in your investigation plan for upper abdominal pain is a systematic error that examiners note.
  • Use the Revised Atlanta Classification when discussing severity. Stating that you would classify severity using validated scoring tools (Ranson's, BISAP, Modified CT Severity Index) demonstrates a systematic, evidence-based approach and impresses examiners in the closing or management discussion component of the station.
