# Codeine-Induced Constipation — Abdominal Pain History Taking Station

## Clinical Presentation

A 20-year-old woman presents to the general practice or emergency department with a one-week history of lower abdominal pain. Critically, the clinical stem discloses that she had a separate episode of back pain ten days prior, which has since resolved. This lead point — the resolved back pain — is not incidental; it is the diagnostic pivot of the entire station. The examining candidate must recognise that the treatment received for the back pain, specifically a combination opioid analgesic such as codeine with paracetamol (Panadeine Forte®), is the causative agent driving the current presentation.

The abdominal pain is diffuse across the lower abdomen, rated approximately 4 out of 10 in severity, and has been present for approximately one week. The patient may report that pain is aggravated by eating, which in the context of constipation reflects a heightened gastrocolic reflex. There are no gynaecological symptoms, no urinary symptoms, and no systemic red flags in the uncomplicated presentation. Physical examination reveals faecal loading in the left lower quadrant on palpation and hard stool on digital rectal examination.

This station is clinically important for AMC candidates because it tests the ability to synthesise a medication history with a presenting complaint — a higher-order clinical reasoning skill. Codeine-induced constipation is a genuine and common clinical problem in Australian primary care and emergency medicine. Candidates who approach abdominal pain systematically, explore medication history meticulously, and correctly incorporate the examiner-provided lead point will reach the correct diagnosis efficiently and demonstrate the standard of care expected of an Australian medical graduate.

## Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

This is a combined history taking and physical examination station with a diagnostic explanation component. The examiner expects the candidate to demonstrate four competencies in four minutes:

**1. Structured opening:** Introduce yourself, confirm patient identity, establish rapport with an open-ended question ("Can you tell me what's been bothering you?"), and allow the patient to narrate.

**2. Systematic pain history:** Apply the SOCRATES framework to the presenting abdominal pain, with particular attention to associated features in the gastrointestinal, urological, and gynaecological systems, consistent with the anatomical location of lower abdominal pain.

**3. Lead point exploration:** The station stem discloses a resolved back pain episode and treatment with codeine-containing analgesia. The examiner expects the candidate to actively inquire about this — specifically what medication was prescribed, the duration of use, and whether it correlates temporally with the onset of abdominal symptoms. This is not a passive historical detail; it is the diagnostic key.

**4. Physical examination request and interpretation:** When directed to examine, the candidate should request a full abdominal examination including inspection, auscultation, palpation (with specific inquiry for masses or tenderness), percussion, and digital rectal examination. The examiner will provide findings consistent with faecal loading. The candidate must interpret these findings in real time and synthesise them with the history.

**5. Diagnosis and differential explanation:** Deliver the diagnosis to the patient in clear, non-technical language. Explain the mechanism of codeine-induced constipation. Offer a structured differential diagnosis to the examiner, and provide a safety net.

## Systematic Approach

### Opening and SOCRATES Assessment of Abdominal Pain

Begin with a professional introduction and a single open-ended question. Allow the patient to speak without interruption for 30–60 seconds. Then systematically clarify using SOCRATES:

**Site:**
- "Where exactly is the pain?" — Lower abdomen, diffuse across the lower quadrants. Not localised to a single fossa, which reduces the likelihood of acute appendicitis (right iliac fossa) or ovarian pathology (lateralised).

**Onset:**
- "When did it start?" — Gradual onset over the past week. Absence of sudden severe onset reduces the probability of visceral perforation or torsion.

**Character:**
- "Can you describe what the pain feels like?" — Typically described as a dull ache or cramping discomfort. Colicky character may be present due to intestinal peristalsis against a constipated colon.

**Radiation:**
- "Does the pain move anywhere?" — Usually does not radiate. Radiation to the back or groin would raise concern for renal colic or aortic pathology.

**Associated symptoms:**
- Gastrointestinal: Bowel habit change — specifically constipation, reduced frequency, hard stools, straining, incomplete evacuation. The patient has not opened bowels normally since commencing codeine.
- Nausea and vomiting: Absent or minimal in constipation. The presence of persistent vomiting should redirect the diagnosis toward bowel obstruction (see Red Flags).
- Flatus: Patient should still be passing wind. Inability to pass wind is a red flag for complete bowel obstruction.
- Appetite: May be reduced. Worsening of pain with eating reflects the gastrocolic reflex acting on a loaded colon.
- Rectal bleeding: Absent. Its presence would necessitate further investigation (colorectal pathology, haemorrhoids secondary to straining).

**Timing:**
- "Has this been constant or does it come and go?" — Intermittent cramping is consistent with constipation. A pain-free interval between cramps supports this.

**Exacerbating and relieving factors:**
- Worsened by eating (gastrocolic reflex). Not relieved by position change or antacids. May be partially relieved after bowel movement.

**Severity:**
- 4 out of 10. This moderate severity is consistent with an uncomplicated clinical course. Escalating or severe pain warrants reassessment of the differential.

### Lead Point Exploration — The Medication History

The stem explicitly states that the patient suffered back pain ten days prior, which has since resolved. This is a deliberate clinical cue provided by the AMC. The candidate who ignores this information will miss the diagnosis.

**Explore the back pain episode:**
- "I understand you had some back pain about ten days ago. Can you tell me more about that?"
- "Did you see a doctor for it?"
- "What did they tell you was causing it?"
- "What treatment did they prescribe?"

The patient will disclose that she was prescribed paracetamol with codeine — in the Australian context, this is Panadeine Forte® (500 mg paracetamol / 30 mg codeine per tablet). The candidate should clarify:
- Duration of use: How many days was she taking it?
- Dose and frequency: How many tablets per day?
- Last dose: When did she stop?
- Temporal correlation: "When did you start having the abdominal pain — was it around the same time you started the codeine?"

This temporal correlation is the diagnostic linchpin. Codeine acts on μ-opioid receptors in the enteric nervous system, inhibiting propulsive peristalsis, increasing colonic transit time, and elevating resting anal sphincter tone. The result is constipation, which in this case has progressed to faecal loading.

**Complete medication review:**
- Any other regular medications (oral contraceptive pill, iron supplements, calcium channel blockers — all potential contributors to constipation)?
- Allergies?
- Over-the-counter preparations (laxatives already attempted)?

### Systematic Symptom Review — GI, Urological, and Gynaecological Systems

Given the anatomical location of lower abdominal pain in a young woman, a structured differential requires review of three organ systems:

**Gastrointestinal system:**
- Bowel frequency, stool consistency (Bristol Stool Scale types 1–2 in constipation), blood or mucus per rectum, tenesmus.
- Symptoms of irritable bowel syndrome: bloating, alternating bowel habit, relationship to menstrual cycle or stress.
- Constitutional symptoms: fever, weight loss, night sweats (red flags for inflammatory bowel disease or malignancy — unlikely at age 20 but must be screened).

**Urological/Renal system (KUB):**
- Dysuria, frequency, urgency, haematuria — screen for urinary tract infection and renal calculi.
- Loin-to-groin radiation would suggest ureteric colic.
- Note: In this case, the resolved back pain could theoretically represent a previously passed calculus, though the medication history makes codeine-induced constipation significantly more probable.

**Gynaecological system (in a woman of reproductive age):**
- Last menstrual period — is she pregnant? A pregnancy test is mandatory in any woman of childbearing age with abdominal pain.
- Menstrual regularity, dysmenorrhoea, intermenstrual bleeding.
- Vaginal discharge — character, colour, odour (screen for pelvic inflammatory disease).
- Dyspareunia (superficial or deep).
- Previous gynaecological history: endometriosis, ovarian cysts, sexually transmitted infections.
- In this case, gynaecological history is normal. The examiner will confirm there are no pelvic symptoms.

**Hernias:**
- Any groin swelling or pain, particularly with exertion or Valsalva manoeuvre.

### Physical Examination Findings Interpretation

The candidate should request and interpret the following:

**General appearance:** Well, no acute distress. Afebrile.

**Vital signs:** Within normal limits. Tachycardia or hypotension would suggest a more serious pathology.

**Abdominal examination:**
- *Inspection:* Mildly distended lower abdomen possible. No visible peristalsis.
- *Auscultation:* Bowel sounds present but may be reduced in frequency.
- *Palpation:* The examiner reports faecal loading — a palpable, non-tender, doughy, indentable mass in the left lower quadrant along the line of the descending and sigmoid colon. This is pathognomonic of significant constipation or faecal impaction. It does not shift with position (unlike ascites) and does not pulsate (unlike vascular pathology).
- *Percussion:* Dull over loaded colon; tympanic elsewhere.
- *Digital rectal examination (DRE):* Hard, impacted stool palpable in the rectal vault. This confirms faecal loading at the most distal segment. In the absence of DRE, faecal impaction in the rectum will be missed. This examination is essential and must be requested explicitly.

**Pelvic examination:** In a young woman with lower abdominal pain, a speculum and bimanual examination should be offered to exclude cervicitis, adnexal tenderness (PID), or adnexal masses (ovarian cyst or torsion). In this station, the examiner confirms normal pelvic findings.

## Red Flags — Must Not Miss

- **Inability to pass wind** — Suggests complete bowel obstruction rather than constipation. A patient who cannot pass flatus at all requires urgent surgical assessment. Do not discharge with laxatives alone; obtain erect chest X-ray and abdominal series.

- **Persistent or projectile vomiting** — In the context of a constipated patient, vomiting indicates either complete obstruction or faecal impaction with retrograde gut dysfunction. This changes the diagnosis from constipation to obstruction and mandates urgent investigation.

- **Absolute constipation (no stool or flatus for >72 hours)** — Combined with abdominal distension, this is large bowel obstruction until proven otherwise. Requires urgent plain abdominal film, CT abdomen/pelvis, and surgical consultation.

- **Haemodynamic instability** — Tachycardia, hypotension, or pallor in a young woman with lower abdominal pain must immediately raise the possibility of ectopic pregnancy, ruptured ovarian cyst with haemoperitoneum, or sepsis from PID. A positive β-hCG in this context is a surgical emergency.

- **Positive pregnancy test** — Mandatory to perform in any woman of reproductive age with lower abdominal pain. An ectopic pregnancy mimics constipation-related pain and can be rapidly fatal if missed.

- **Fever and cervical motion tenderness** — Suggest pelvic inflammatory disease. Empirical antibiotic therapy should not be delayed pending culture results if clinical suspicion is high.

- **Weight loss, night sweats, or rectal bleeding** — In a young patient, these red flags raise concern for inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis) or, less commonly, colorectal malignancy. Warrant colonoscopy and specialist referral.

- **Escalating pain severity despite laxatives** — Failure to improve with treatment for constipation, or worsening pain, should prompt reassessment. A missed diagnosis (ovarian torsion, appendicitis) may initially mimic constipation.

## Investigations

**Bedside:**
- Urine pregnancy test (β-hCG) — mandatory in all women of reproductive age
- Urinalysis and urine microscopy/culture/sensitivities — exclude UTI or renal calculus
- Vital signs including temperature, pulse rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation

**Bloods:**
- Full blood count — leucocytosis in infection or inflammation; anaemia in bleeding or IBD
- C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate — inflammatory markers
- Electrolytes, urea, creatinine — hypokalaemia and hypercalcaemia can contribute to constipation; assess renal function
- Serum calcium — hypercalcaemia causes constipation (malignancy, hyperparathyroidism)
- Thyroid-stimulating hormone — hypothyroidism is a reversible cause of constipation
- Liver function tests — if concern for intra-abdominal pathology

**Imaging:**
- Plain abdominal X-ray (supine ± erect) — confirms faecal loading pattern; identifies bowel obstruction (dilated loops, air-fluid levels, absence of gas in rectum)
- Pelvic ultrasound — first-line imaging in women of reproductive age; assesses ovaries, uterus, free fluid
- CT abdomen and pelvis with contrast — if bowel obstruction is suspected or diagnosis is uncertain; highest sensitivity and specificity for intra-abdominal pathology

**Specialist:**
- Gynaecology review — if PID, ectopic pregnancy, or ovarian pathology is suspected
- Colorectal surgery — if bowel obstruction, faecal impaction requiring manual disimpaction, or surgical pathology is identified
- Colonoscopy (non-urgent, via gastroenterology) — if IBD or colorectal neoplasia is a concern based on red flag symptoms

## Management

**Immediate management — codeine-induced constipation:**

1. **Cease codeine immediately** — The causative agent must be stopped. Advise the patient to discontinue Panadeine Forte® now that the back pain has resolved. Provide an alternative non-opioid analgesic for residual back discomfort (e.g., ibuprofen 400 mg TDS with food if not contraindicated, or regular paracetamol 1 g QID).

2. **Osmotic laxatives** — Polyethylene glycol (PEG, e.g., Movicol®) is first-line in Australia for opioid-induced constipation. One to two sachets dissolved in water, once to twice daily, until bowels open normally. It works by drawing water into the colon, softening stool.

3. **Stimulant laxatives** — Senna (Senokot®) or docusate sodium (Coloxyl®) can be added if osmotic laxatives alone are insufficient. Bisacodyl (Dulcolax®) suppositories are useful for rectal loading.

4. **Adequate oral hydration** — Minimum 2 litres of water daily. Dehydration worsens constipation.

5. **Dietary advice** — Increase dietary fibre (fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes). Prune juice has natural laxative properties and is well-tolerated.

6. **Mobilisation** — Physical activity promotes gastrointestinal motility. Encourage regular walking.

**If faecal impaction is confirmed or bowel obstruction is suspected:**
- Hospital admission for IV fluids, nasogastric tube, nil by mouth
- Manual disimpaction under appropriate analgesia/sedation if rectal vault is loaded and patient cannot pass stool independently
- Enemas (e.g., phosphate enemas) under medical supervision
- Urgent surgical consultation if complete obstruction

**Ongoing management:**
- If opioid analgesia cannot be avoided in future (e.g., post-operative pain), prescribe prophylactic laxatives concurrently from day one
- Educate regarding the class effect of all opioid medications on bowel motility
- Review bowel habit at follow-up in one to two weeks
- If constipation persists beyond two weeks after ceasing codeine, reassess for an alternative underlying cause

## Communication and Patient Education

**Explaining the diagnosis:**

Use the following structure when speaking to the patient:

*"From the history you've given me and what I found on examination, I believe I know what's causing your abdominal pain. When I examined your tummy, I could feel that your bowel is quite loaded with stool — what we call constipation. On the internal examination, I also felt hard stool that has been sitting there for some time. I believe this is directly related to the painkiller you were taking for your back pain. Codeine — the ingredient in Panadeine Forte® — is a type of opioid painkiller, and one of its most common side effects is that it slows down the movement of your bowel. This means stools don't pass through as easily, they become harder and more difficult to pass, and they can build up — which is what has been causing your discomfort."*

**Addressing likely patient concerns:**

- *"Is this serious?"* — Reassure that in its current form it is not dangerous, but explain that if left untreated it could worsen. Now that the cause (codeine) has been removed, the bowel will recover with appropriate treatment.
- *"Will I need an operation?"* — Not at this stage. The management is medical and dietary. Explain what signs would prompt urgent review.
- *"Can I take codeine again in the future?"* — Not without medical supervision, and only if a laxative is prescribed concurrently.

**Written resources:**
- Provide written information about constipation management, dietary fibre, and fluid intake
- Provide a medication fact sheet for prescribed laxative (Movicol® or equivalent)

**Follow-up:**
- Review in one to two weeks to confirm resolution of constipation and abdominal pain
- Earlier review if symptoms worsen or new red flag symptoms develop

## AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

- **Always explore the clinical lead point provided in the station stem.** The AMC deliberately embeds this information in the written brief because it will not arise spontaneously in the history without direct questioning. If the stem mentions a prior illness or treatment, that detail is diagnostic — ask about it early and systematically.

- **The medication history is not optional — it is diagnostic.** In opioid-induced constipation, the diagnosis cannot be made without knowing that codeine was taken. Ask every patient with a change in bowel habit about all recent and current medications, including prescription, over-the-counter, and herbal preparations.

- **Know the Australian brand name:** Panadeine Forte® is the brand name for paracetamol 500 mg / codeine phosphate 30 mg. AMC patients may present with this brand name rather than the generic. Recognise it immediately.

- **Digital rectal examination is mandatory and must be explicitly requested.** Faecal impaction in the rectal vault will not be detected by abdominal palpation alone. Omitting the DRE is a critical examination error that will cost marks.

- **Distinguish constipation from complete bowel obstruction before closing the station.** The key differentiating question is: "Are you still passing wind?" If yes — constipation. If no, with vomiting and distension — obstruction. These are different diagnoses requiring different management pathways.

- **Pregnancy must be excluded in every woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain.** A urine β-hCG is a mandatory bedside investigation. Failing to order this in the investigations discussion will be marked as a significant omission.

- **Structure your differential using anatomical compartments.** For lower abdominal pain in a woman, present differentials from three systems: gastrointestinal (constipation, IBD, IBS), urological (UTI, calculus), and gynaecological (PID, ectopic pregnancy, ovarian pathology). This demonstrates systematic clinical reasoning to the examiner.

- **Deliver the diagnosis to the patient, not just to the examiner.** This station requires the candidate to explain the diagnosis in patient-appropriate language. A jargon-heavy explanation addressed only to the examiner fails the communication component of the mark sheet.

- **Temporal correlation is your diagnostic argument.** When explaining the diagnosis, articulate why you believe codeine is the cause: the abdominal pain began after codeine was commenced, it correlates with reduced bowel frequency, and the physical examination confirms faecal loading. This structured reasoning demonstrates diagnostic competence.

- **Do not get anchored on a single diagnosis without completing the systematic history.** Even when codeine-induced constipation is the obvious answer, complete the GI, KUB, and gynaecological screens. Examiners assess completeness, not just accuracy of the primary diagnosis.

- **Safety netting must be specific and actionable.** Telling the patient to "come back if it gets worse" is insufficient. Name the specific symptoms that should prompt immediate return: inability to pass wind, persistent vomiting, significant worsening of pain, or feeling faint. This reflects real clinical practice.

- **If the examiner changes the clinical findings — respond.** The same case with vomiting and inability to pass flatus becomes bowel obstruction. Adapt your differential and management accordingly. The ability to update your diagnosis in response to new clinical information is a core AMC competency.

## Safety Net

Provide the patient with clear, specific instructions before she leaves the consultation:

**Return immediately to the emergency department if:**
- You develop complete inability to pass wind or have no bowel motion within 48 hours of starting laxative treatment
- Vomiting becomes persistent or you cannot keep fluids down
- The abdominal pain becomes severe, constant, or spreads across the entire abdomen
- You develop fever (temperature above 38°C)
- You feel dizzy, faint, or develop shortness of breath
- You notice blood in your stools

**Follow up with your general practitioner in 1–2 weeks if:**
- Symptoms have not fully resolved with laxative treatment
- You are unsure about ongoing analgesia for your back pain
- You have any further concerns about your bowel health

**General advice before leaving:**
- Do not restart codeine-containing medications without discussing with a doctor first
- If you ever need opioid analgesia again, ask your doctor to prescribe a laxative at the same time
- Drink at least 2 litres of water daily and increase fibre in your diet
- A written medication information sheet for Movicol® has been provided
- If you have any questions before your follow-up appointment, contact the practice by telephone during business hours
