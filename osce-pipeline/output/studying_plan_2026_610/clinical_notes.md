# Strategic Preparation for the AMC Clinical Examination

## Clinical Presentation

The AMC Clinical Examination is a structured objective assessment requiring candidates to demonstrate clinical competence across multiple domains within a timed, role-player–based format. Unlike written examinations, the clinical examination demands the integration of medical knowledge, clinical reasoning, communication skill, and performance under pressure — simultaneously and within strict time constraints. Candidates who approach this examination without a deliberate, strategic preparation framework consistently underperform relative to their actual clinical knowledge, a pattern that is well documented in pass rate data across examination cohorts.

The fundamental challenge of the AMC Clinical Examination lies not in the breadth of content alone, but in the candidate's ability to deploy structured clinical reasoning reliably under examination conditions. A candidate may possess sound theoretical knowledge yet fail to pass individual stations because they cannot organise that knowledge into a coherent, efficient, examiner-satisfying performance within eight minutes. This disconnect between knowledge and performance is the defining problem that strategic preparation is designed to address.

This reference addresses the principles of structured examination preparation, case prioritisation, time allocation, knowledge consolidation, review strategy, and role-play optimisation — all within the specific context of the AMC Clinical Examination and the Australian healthcare environment. Candidates who internalise these principles and apply them with discipline significantly improve their likelihood of passing on first attempt.

---

## Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

The AMC Clinical Examination is structured across five core domains: **Medicine and Surgery** (collectively the largest domain), **Physical Examination**, **Psychiatry**, **Obstetrics and Gynaecology**, and **Paediatrics**. Each station is assessed against a standardised mark sheet, and examiners assess whether the candidate demonstrates a structured, safe, and patient-centred approach to the clinical scenario.

The examination currently comprises approximately 16 stations per sitting. Based on examination data from 2025 cohorts, the average case distribution is:
- **Medicine and Surgery:** 5–6 stations
- **Physical Examination:** 3–4 stations
- **Psychiatry:** 2–3 stations (increasing trend, with video-based cases used frequently)
- **Obstetrics and Gynaecology:** 1–3 stations
- **Paediatrics:** 2–3 stations

Critically, **Medicine and Physical Examination combined account for more than 50% of all stations** — commonly nine out of sixteen. This has remained consistent across examination years dating back to 2017 and constitutes the single most important strategic consideration in preparation planning.

Examiners use a criterion-referenced marking system assessing specific behaviours: structured opening, systematic history or examination technique, elicitation of ICE (Ideas, Concerns, Expectations), identification of red flags and key clinical points, appropriate differential diagnosis generation, investigation and management planning, patient education, and safety netting. Candidates who fail to meet the criterion for key clinical points — regardless of communication quality — will not achieve a passing mark.

## Systematic Approach

### Domain-Weighted Time Allocation

The cornerstone of effective preparation is aligning study time with the **examination weight of each domain**. A common and costly error is allocating study time based on perceived ease, comfort, or topical interest rather than on statistical representation in the examination.

**Recommended minimum time allocation:**
- At least **50% of total preparation time** should be allocated to Medicine/Surgery and Physical Examination combined
- The remaining 50% should be distributed across Psychiatry, Obstetrics and Gynaecology, and Paediatrics

For a candidate with eight weeks of available preparation time (after reserving two weeks for the review phase):

| Domain | Recommended Allocation |
|--------|----------------------|
| Medicine/Surgery | 2.5 weeks |
| Physical Examination | 2.0 weeks |
| Psychiatry | 1.0 week |
| Obstetrics & Gynaecology | 1.5 weeks |
| Paediatrics | 1.0 week |

This allocation should be treated as a **minimum** for Medicine and Physical Examination. If circumstances interrupt preparation in lower-weighted domains, those shortfalls are acceptable; shortfalls in Medicine or Physical Examination carry a proportionally higher risk of examination failure.

**The core principle:** Passing the examination does not require perfection across all domains. A candidate who achieves six passes from nine Medicine and Physical Examination stations, then passes one each of Psychiatry, Paediatrics, and Obstetrics/Gynaecology has demonstrated sufficient competence for overall passage. Conversely, a candidate who achieves near-perfect results in Paediatrics, Psychiatry, and Obstetrics/Gynaecology but underperforms in Medicine and Physical Examination cannot compensate sufficiently to pass.

### Case Selection and Depth of Preparation

A second critical strategic principle is **prioritising preparation depth over breadth**. Candidates consistently make the error of attempting to prepare for the maximum number of possible cases, which necessarily reduces the quality of preparation for each individual case. This approach fails for a predictable reason: the AMC Clinical Examination is dominated by **recurring, well-established cases** that appear across multiple examination sittings.

**Principle of focused mastery:**
- A candidate who prepares 60 cases thoroughly is better positioned than one who reviews 120 cases superficially
- With a fixed preparation time, each additional case added to the study list receives proportionally less preparation
- The well-known, recurring cases (e.g., migraine, chest pain, headache, abdominal pain, cardiovascular physical examination) appear repeatedly across examination years and are the cases upon which pass/fail decisions are most commonly determined

**Approach to AMC recalls:**
- Recalls (brief candidate-reported summaries of examination cases) provide useful orientation to likely case topics but are inherently incomplete
- A recall summary of one to four sentences does not capture the full eight-minute station performance required
- Recalls should be used to identify the **case topic and relevant cluster**, not as a template for memorisation
- The correct use of recalls is: identify the topic → build a structured approach to that topic → apply that structure to the recalled case

**New and pilot cases:**
- Novel cases appearing for the first time in an examination sitting are typically pilot cases (unscored or carrying reduced weight)
- The probability that a new case will appear in a given sitting is low compared to the probability of encountering a well-established recurring case
- Excessive preparation time directed toward obscure or hypothetical cases at the expense of mastery of core cases is a consistently observed error pattern in unsuccessful candidates

### The Preparation Phase — Building Clinical Structures

Clinical structures are the organising frameworks through which candidates systematically approach any case regardless of the specific presenting complaint. A well-constructed clinical structure enables a candidate to perform competently in a case they have not seen before, by applying a principled approach rather than relying on memorisation of a specific scenario.

**Core elements of a clinical structure for a history-taking station:**
1. **Presenting complaint** — systematic symptom characterisation (onset, duration, character, severity, progression, associated symptoms)
2. **Key diagnostic questions** — condition-specific questions that distinguish between major differentials
3. **Red flag screen** — mandatory identification of features requiring urgent management
4. **ICE elicitation** — patient's ideas about the cause, concerns about impact or diagnosis, expectations from the consultation
5. **Relevant background** — past medical history, medications, allergies, social history, family history
6. **Closure** — summarising findings, explaining next steps, safety netting

For example, a candidate approaching a headache station requires: a structured headache assessment (SOCRATES plus neurological red flags), knowledge of the key diagnostic questions distinguishing migraine from tension-type headache from secondary causes, a prioritised differential diagnosis list, awareness of the ICE domain for a patient with recurrent headache, and the ability to deliver this within eight minutes.

**Own notes as preparation tools:**
Active note-making during the preparation phase serves two functions: it reinforces learning during acquisition and produces the review material required during the final preparation phase. Prepared notes from external sources (commercial tutors, printed handouts, online resources) are significantly less effective as review tools than notes generated by the candidate themselves, because self-generated notes encode the candidate's own reasoning pathways and are retrieved more efficiently under stress.

The preparation phase note should capture:
- The clinical structure for each case cluster
- The key diagnostic questions specific to that cluster
- The priority differential diagnosis list
- The critical investigations and management decision points
- The communication points specific to that patient group

### The Review Phase — Consolidation Under Pressure

The review phase encompasses approximately the final two weeks before the examination. This phase is qualitatively different from the preparation phase and requires a distinct approach. It is the phase during which the greatest proportion of candidates experience acute psychological distress — not because their preparation was inadequate, but because they attempt to apply preparation-phase strategies (slow, thorough, case-by-case review) to a phase that requires rapid, targeted consolidation.

**Characteristics of effective review:**
- Each case is reviewed in **7–10 minutes**, not the full depth required during initial learning
- Review focuses on: clinical structure, key diagnostic questions, differential diagnosis list, and must-not-miss points
- Routine elements (standard history-taking sequence, universal communication skills) do not require review time — these are retrieved automatically under examination conditions
- Review is driven by the candidate's **own notes** prepared during the preceding weeks

**Psychological preparation for the review phase:**
Candidates should anticipate that the review phase will produce a subjective sense of inadequacy, regardless of actual preparation quality. The volume of material to be reviewed relative to the available time creates an inevitable perception of incompleteness. This is a normal and universal experience, not an indicator of actual unpreparedness. Candidates who understand this in advance are better equipped to manage the anxiety response and maintain effective study behaviour during this period.

**The 70/30 principle of examination performance:**
Empirical observation of candidate performance suggests that approximately 70% of what a candidate recalls and deploys in the examination was reviewed in the final two weeks; approximately 30% derives from earlier preparation phases. This does not diminish the importance of the preparation phase — the depth of understanding built during preparation is what enables efficient and accurate review — but it does explain why the review phase has a disproportionate influence on examination day performance.

### Role-Play Practice — Purposeful Application

Role-play is a necessary but frequently misused component of AMC Clinical Examination preparation. Its value lies in translating acquired knowledge into time-pressured, stress-exposed performance — not in providing a substitute for knowledge acquisition.

**When role-play is effective:**
- After thorough preparation of the relevant case cluster
- When the candidate has specific, pre-defined goals for the session (e.g., practising adherence to clinical structure, improving timing, rehearsing ICE elicitation, consolidating key diagnostic question delivery)
- When conducted with a partner of equivalent preparation level and comparable commitment

**When role-play is ineffective:**
- When used as the primary mode of knowledge acquisition (reading notes aloud with a partner is not role-play practice; it is passive review)
- When the candidate lacks foundational knowledge of the case content
- When conducted without defined objectives
- When conducted with partners at significantly different levels of preparation, creating reinforcement of mixed-quality habits

**Common hazard of sustained study partnerships:**
Long-term, exclusive study partnerships carry a specific risk: bidirectional reinforcement of both productive and unproductive habits. Candidates who study exclusively with one partner over extended periods tend either to both pass or to both fail — reflecting the shared habit patterns that develop when the same performance errors are positively reinforced between two candidates. Exposure to multiple partners with different styles, strengths, and approaches mitigates this risk.

**Recommended role-play allocation:** Approximately one session per week, with a maximum of six cases per session (approximately 90 minutes), each session structured around a specific, pre-identified learning objective.

### The AMC Candidate Handbook

The AMC Candidate Handbook is a formal examination resource that predates current examination formats. Despite its age and the acknowledged limitations of its content, it remains the **only official AMC-published document** providing direct insight into the examination framework, marking priorities, and examiner perspective on candidate performance.

**Value of the handbook:**
- Reveals AMC's conceptualisation of critical errors and how these are assessed
- Identifies the specific clinical points that examiners are directed to assess in individual cases
- Provides the official AMC perspective on differential diagnosis generation, investigation selection, and management reasoning
- Clarifies the examiner's viewpoint on candidate behaviours that attract or reduce marks

**Limitations of the handbook:**
- The clinical content reflects management guidelines that have since been updated
- Not all case types in the current examination are represented
- The handbook should not be used to derive clinical structures or management protocols — current clinical guidelines take precedence

**Recommended approach:** Read the handbook in its entirety at least once, producing a personal summary of no more than 10 pages. Use the handbook specifically to understand the **examiner's evaluative framework**, not as a primary clinical reference.

## Differential Diagnosis

For the AMC Clinical Examination broadly, the "differential diagnosis" of candidate failure maps to preparation strategy failures rather than clinical knowledge deficits. The following framework identifies the categories:

| Category | Failure Mode | Key Distinguishing Features |
|----------|-------------|----------------------------|
| Common | Domain imbalance | Adequate preparation in minor domains; underprepared in Medicine and Physical Examination |
| Common | Breadth over depth | Large number of cases studied superficially; insufficient mastery of high-yield cases |
| Common | Role-play substituting for knowledge | Study time consumed by role-play before foundational knowledge established |
| Common | Recall memorisation | Reproducing recalled stems without underlying clinical structure |
| Important | Review phase collapse | Preparation phase complete; review phase disorganised or abandoned due to volume anxiety |
| Important | No written preparation notes | Unable to conduct efficient review; must re-study material from primary sources |
| Important | Partner habit reinforcement | Sustained exclusive partnership reinforcing shared performance errors |
| Must Not Miss | Handbook not read | Missing AMC examiner-perspective on critical errors; case types from handbook unprepared |
| Must Not Miss | Ignoring physical examination | Underestimating performance requirements; structural preparation inadequate |

## Red Flags — Must Not Miss

- **Allocating less than 50% of preparation time to Medicine and Physical Examination** — These two domains collectively constitute more than 50% of examination stations. Underpreparing them cannot be compensated by performance in minor domains; this is the most common single cause of examination failure.

- **Entering the review phase without personal written notes** — The review phase requires rapid case-by-case consolidation. Without pre-prepared personal notes, candidates are forced to return to primary preparation materials at review speed, which is neither efficient nor effective under the time pressures of the review phase.

- **Commencing role-play practice before establishing foundational knowledge** — Role-play conducted without underlying knowledge produces performance that rehearses errors rather than correct clinical behaviour. This is a time-negative activity that simultaneously wastes preparation time and entrenches incorrect habits.

- **Treating the review phase as a continuation of the preparation phase** — Attempting to study cases at preparation depth during the review phase creates the volume crisis that leads to candidate breakdown. The review phase requires a deliberately faster, more targeted approach.

- **Failing to read the AMC Candidate Handbook** — The handbook is the only official AMC publication providing insight into the examiner's evaluative framework. Candidates who omit this resource lack direct access to AMC's articulation of critical error criteria.

- **Allowing anxiety about the review phase to disrupt the preparation phase** — Candidates who begin worrying about the volume of review material during the preparation phase disrupt the quality of early preparation and arrive at the review phase with less thorough foundational knowledge, creating a self-fulfilling difficulty.

- **Assuming uniform preparation requirements across all candidates** — Preparation strategy must be individualised to available time, clinical experience, work and family commitments, and examination date. Generic preparation plans that fail to account for individual circumstances produce poor time utilisation.

## Investigations

In the context of AMC Clinical Examination preparation strategy, "investigations" refers to the diagnostic assessment a candidate should perform on their own readiness:

**Self-assessment (pre-preparation):**
- Current level of knowledge in each of the five examination domains
- Available weekly study hours, accounting for work, family, and other commitments
- Number of weeks until the examination date
- Prior exposure to AMC clinical station format (first attempt vs. repeat candidate)

**Ongoing assessment (during preparation):**
- Structured self-testing after completing each case cluster (can you produce the clinical structure, key diagnostic questions, and differential list without notes?)
- Simulated role-play performance at regular intervals to identify structure adherence under timed conditions
- Weekly review of written notes to identify gaps in key clinical points

**Pre-examination assessment (review phase):**
- Timed self-review of each domain: can the core structure, key points, and differentials be recalled within 8–10 minutes per case?
- Identification of domains or clusters requiring additional focused review
- Confirmation that physical examination performance meets the required standard of a smooth, structured, unhurried demonstration

## Management

**Phase 1 — Planning (immediate action)**

Write a preparation plan on paper before commencing study. The plan must specify:
- Total available weeks until examination
- Two weeks reserved for the review phase (non-negotiable)
- Weekly time allocation per domain, proportional to domain weight
- A fixed deadline for each domain — once a deadline is reached, move to the next domain regardless of perceived completeness, with the single exception that Medicine and Physical Examination may receive additional time if less than 80% of planned content has been covered

**Phase 2 — Preparation (main study period)**

Study each domain systematically, building clinical structures rather than memorising individual recalls. Produce personal written notes during every study session. Integrate role-play on a limited, goal-directed basis (once weekly). Read the AMC Candidate Handbook during this phase, producing a personal summary.

**Phase 3 — Review (final two weeks)**

Conduct rapid, systematic review of all domains using personal notes. Each case reviewed in 7–10 minutes. Prioritise Medicine, Physical Examination, and high-yield cases in lower-weighted domains (e.g., Mental State Examination in Psychiatry; common paediatric presentations in Paediatrics). Accept that full review of all material is not achievable and is not required for examination success.

**Phase 4 — Examination day**

Enter the examination with the understanding that absolute readiness is unattainable and unnecessary. Examination performance is determined by reliable deployment of structure, consistent identification of key clinical points, and composed professional communication — not by comprehensive recall of all possible content.

## Communication and Patient Education

In this context, communication applies to the candidate's interaction with the examiner and role player. The following principles are paramount:

**Structured, not memorised:** Examiners assess the candidate's ability to construct a clinical approach in the moment, not to reproduce a memorised script. Candidates who are excessively scripted perform poorly when cases deviate even slightly from the anticipated scenario.

**ICE is non-negotiable:** Eliciting the patient's Ideas, Concerns, and Expectations is an assessed criterion in virtually every station type. Failure to elicit ICE, or eliciting it mechanically without genuine engagement, consistently contributes to mark loss across multiple stations simultaneously.

**Key points must be verbalised:** Clinical knowledge not communicated during the station does not receive marks. Candidates must learn to verbalise their clinical reasoning, differential diagnosis, and management plan explicitly — even when this feels obvious or premature.

**Closing structure:** Every station should conclude with a brief synthesis (summarising findings or the information provided), an explanation of next steps, and a safety net (specific instructions for when and how to seek further care).

## AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

- **Allocate preparation time in proportion to domain weight, not comfort.** The two most challenging domains — Medicine and Physical Examination — are also the two most heavily weighted. Prioritising easier domains is a statistically reliable path to failure.

- **Master the recurring high-yield cases before attempting rare or novel ones.** Migraine, chest pain, abdominal pain, headache, cardiovascular physical examination, and respiratory physical examination appear across multiple cohorts. A candidate who can pass these reliably is substantially protected against failure.

- **Build clinical structures; do not memorise recalls.** A well-constructed structure for headache will pass a migraine station, a tension headache station, a subarachnoid haemorrhage station, and a temporal arteritis station. A memorised migraine recall will fail in any other scenario.

- **Begin producing personal review notes from the first day of preparation.** These notes are the single most important tool in the review phase. Notes written during active learning are significantly more useful than notes written retrospectively or sourced externally.

- **Do not commence role-play before acquiring foundational knowledge of the case content.** Role-play is performance rehearsal; it requires knowledge to rehearse. Without knowledge, it rehearses error.

- **Read the AMC Candidate Handbook at least once.** It is the only official AMC document providing the examiner's evaluative framework. Case types drawn from the handbook appear regularly in current examination sittings and are typically among the most difficult to pass without prior familiarity.

- **Establish a deadline-driven study schedule on paper and adhere to it.** Flexibility within domains should not extend the schedule beyond the allocated phase. Moving to the next domain at the deadline — even with perceived gaps in the preceding domain — forces prioritisation of high-yield content and prevents the common pattern of perpetual extension that leaves later domains unprepared.

- **Prepare specifically for the review phase.** Anticipate that the volume of material requiring review will appear overwhelming. This is a universal experience, not an indicator of inadequate preparation. Having personal notes prepared and a structured rapid-review protocol in place transforms the review phase from a crisis into a systematic process.

- **Avoid single-partner, long-term role-play dependency.** Exclusive study partnerships tend to produce shared habit patterns — both productive and unproductive — that become entrenched through mutual reinforcement. Deliberate exposure to multiple partners with varying styles improves adaptability under examination conditions.

- **Do not seek 100% readiness before sitting the examination.** No candidate — regardless of preparation duration — enters the AMC Clinical Examination feeling fully prepared. Waiting for a subjective sense of complete readiness leads to unnecessary delay. The goal is adequate mastery of high-yield content delivered reliably through a structured approach.

- **Psychiatry preparation should prioritise Mental State Examination above all other content.** The Mental State Examination appears in virtually every psychiatry allocation and is highly learnable. Allocating a disproportionate share of psychiatry preparation time to this single case type is a high-return strategy.

- **Physical Examination stations require performance, not only knowledge.** Unlike history-taking or communication stations, Physical Examination requires demonstrable technical skill. Preparing for these stations by reading alone is insufficient; practice of the physical examination sequence on a partner or model is mandatory.

## Safety Net

**For the candidate:**

The appropriate safety net for AMC Clinical Examination preparation is a structured contingency plan for disruptions:

- **If a preparation phase deadline is reached with less than 80% coverage of Medicine or Physical Examination:** Extend the allocation for that domain by no more than one week; compress a lower-weighted domain proportionally
- **If a preparation phase deadline is reached with less than 80% coverage of Psychiatry, Obstetrics/Gynaecology, or Paediatrics:** Move to the next domain at the deadline; focus review-phase time on the highest-yield cases within the missed content (e.g., Mental State Examination for Psychiatry, normal obstetric milestones and common presentations for Obstetrics/Gynaecology, acute paediatric presentations for Paediatrics)
- **If illness or personal circumstances significantly reduce preparation time in the final weeks:** Prioritise Medicine and Physical Examination above all other domains; these two domains provide sufficient station coverage for examination passage if performed well
- **If entering the review phase without personal notes:** Produce abbreviated cluster summaries (key structure, three to five key clinical points, five differential diagnoses) rather than attempting comprehensive re-study
- **If psychological distress during the review phase is impairing study:** Recognise this as a near-universal experience; restrict study sessions to manageable durations (two to three hours); use structured rapid-review protocols to maintain momentum; seek peer support from candidates at an equivalent stage of preparation

**Return criteria:** A candidate who has not covered Medicine and Physical Examination to at least 80% should seriously evaluate whether rescheduling is preferable to sitting underprepared — noting that a subsequent sitting with adequate preparation is more efficient than a failed sitting followed by emergency preparation. This decision should be made early, not in the final 48 hours.
