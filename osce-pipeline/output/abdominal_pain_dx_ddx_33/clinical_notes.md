# Abdominal Pain: Diagnosis Delivery, Differential Diagnosis Formulation, and Clinical Reasoning in the AMC Clinical Examination

## Clinical Presentation

A 30-year-old woman presents to the general practice or emergency department with lower abdominal pain. The clinical encounter requires the candidate to demonstrate not only a comprehensive diagnostic formulation but, critically, to communicate that reasoning transparently to the patient using plain language. This station tests the capacity to move from systematic history taking to a coherent, evidence-based diagnostic conclusion — and to articulate that conclusion to a patient in a manner that reflects genuine clinical understanding rather than pattern recognition from rote memorisation.

The clinical importance of this station extends beyond the immediate presentation. Lower abdominal pain in a woman of reproductive age carries a broad and clinically consequential differential diagnosis, including time-critical emergencies such as ectopic pregnancy, ruptured ovarian cyst, and appendicitis. The AMC Clinical Examination uses this presentation precisely because it demands rapid diagnostic prioritisation, structured clinical reasoning, and the communication skills to deliver a nuanced explanation to a potentially anxious patient.

Candidates should understand that the examiner is assessing not merely whether the correct diagnosis has been reached, but whether the candidate can demonstrate the cognitive process that led to that conclusion. This is a deliberate design feature of the contemporary AMC Clinical Examination: diagnostic reasoning must be made explicit. A candidate who arrives at the correct diagnosis without articulating the clinical basis for that conclusion will not satisfy the marking criteria.

---

## Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

The AMC Clinical Examination communication station centred on diagnosis delivery follows a structured sequence. After completing history taking (typically within the first four to five minutes), the candidate is expected to transition smoothly into the diagnostic explanation phase. The examiner assesses this phase across several domains: clarity of the provisional diagnosis, quality of clinical reasoning linking history findings to the diagnostic conclusion, breadth and specificity of the differential diagnosis list, appropriate use of plain language with technical terms explained, and the overall quality of patient interaction including active listening and response to patient concerns.

The station is scored against a predetermined examiner checklist that typically includes between six and ten differential diagnoses drawn from a comprehensive list prepared by the AMC. A candidate who names only four or five differentials risks failing to tick sufficient items on the examiner's list, regardless of how confidently those few diagnoses are delivered. The examiner cannot award marks for diagnoses not stated.

Candidates must recognise that the task wording is diagnostically significant. A task instructing "explain the most likely diagnosis" requires delivery of a single provisional diagnosis with supporting reasons. A task instructing "explain your likely diagnoses" — note the plural — requires a prioritised differential list delivered to the patient. Misreading this distinction has caused candidates to omit differentials entirely, scoring zero on that domain.

The station structure proceeds as follows:
1. **Transition statement** — signal to the patient that the history-taking phase is complete and you are now sharing your clinical assessment
2. **Provisional diagnosis** — name the most likely condition using correct medical terminology, immediately followed by a brief, plain-language explanation of what that condition means anatomically and physiologically
3. **Clinical reasoning** — explicitly state the positive findings from the history and examination that support this diagnosis
4. **Differential diagnoses** — deliver a structured list of alternative conditions, each accompanied by at least one brief reason explaining why it is considered but ranked lower than the provisional diagnosis
5. **Invitation for questions and empathic closure** — check patient understanding and respond to concerns before the bell

## Systematic Approach

### Transitioning from History to Diagnosis Delivery

The transition from history taking to diagnosis delivery must be deliberate and clearly signalled. An abrupt shift from clinical questioning to diagnostic explanation is disorienting for the patient and signals poor communication structure to the examiner. A suitable transition statement positions the candidate as thoughtful and collaborative:

*"Thank you for telling me all of that. Based on everything you've shared with me today, I'd like to explain what I think may be going on and talk through some other possibilities as well."*

This statement accomplishes three objectives simultaneously: it acknowledges the patient's contribution, signals the shift in the encounter's purpose, and frames the ensuing explanation as a two-way dialogue rather than a pronouncement.

The candidate should be seated, make appropriate eye contact, and adopt a calm, unhurried manner even when time pressure is acute. The examiner is observing interpersonal conduct throughout.

---

### Delivering the Provisional Diagnosis with Clinical Reasoning

The provisional diagnosis must be stated using precise medical terminology, immediately followed by a brief, plain-language explanation. This dual-register approach — technical term plus lay explanation — satisfies two simultaneous marking criteria: specificity of diagnosis (technical language required) and patient-centred communication (plain language required). Vague category labels such as "an infection" or "a bowel problem" are insufficient; the examiner cannot award marks for sinusitis, appendicitis, or cystitis unless those specific terms are used.

**Structure of the diagnostic statement:**

1. **Name the condition precisely:** *"Based on our conversation, I believe you most likely have a condition called appendicitis."*

2. **Explain in plain language:** *"The appendix is a small, finger-shaped part of your large bowel, usually in the lower right part of your abdomen. In your case, I believe it has become inflamed."*

3. **State the reasons explicitly:** *"The reason I'm thinking this is that your pain began around your belly button and has moved to the lower right side of your abdomen. You've also lost your appetite and feel nauseated. When I examined your abdomen, pressing in that area caused significant pain, and releasing the pressure quickly — what we call rebound tenderness — also caused pain. Together, these features strongly suggest appendicitis."*

The clinical reasoning component is non-negotiable in the contemporary AMC Clinical Examination. The marking criteria explicitly require justification of the diagnostic formulation. Candidates who state a diagnosis without linking it to specific history and examination findings are identified by the examiner as having memorised a recall rather than having genuinely reasoned through the case. This is penalised.

Positive findings must be linked to the diagnosis. Negative findings that are clinically relevant — for example, the absence of vaginal discharge reducing the likelihood of pelvic inflammatory disease — may be incorporated into the reasoning for differential diagnoses, not the provisional diagnosis.

The explanation of the provisional diagnosis, including the plain-language description and clinical reasoning, should require approximately 45 to 50 seconds. Candidates who spend more than 60 seconds on this component risk insufficient time for the differential list, which carries equal or greater marking weight.

### Constructing and Delivering the Differential Diagnosis List

The differential diagnosis list in the AMC Clinical Examination is scored against an examiner's reference list that may contain six to ten or more specific diagnoses. The candidate must name as many clinically appropriate, specific diagnoses as possible within the remaining time. The following principles govern the construction and delivery of this list.

**Principle 1: Specificity over category**

Each differential must be named using a specific diagnostic term, not a categorical label. The following substitutions are mandatory:

| Unacceptable (vague) | Required (specific) |
|----------------------|---------------------|
| An infection | Cystitis / pelvic inflammatory disease / tubo-ovarian abscess |
| A bowel problem | Irritable bowel syndrome / inflammatory bowel disease / colorectal malignancy |
| A gynaecological condition | Ovarian torsion / ruptured ovarian cyst / endometriosis |
| Something from the urine | Urinary tract infection / ureteric calculus |

**Principle 2: Reasons for each differential (at least one per diagnosis)**

Each differential should be accompanied by one brief reason explaining why it is considered but ranked below the provisional diagnosis. In time-critical circumstances, a single clause suffices:

*"I was also considering ectopic pregnancy — a pregnancy that forms in the fallopian tube rather than the uterus — but this seems less likely because your last period was normal and arrived on time."*

*"Another possibility is cystitis, which is a bladder infection, but this seems less likely because you haven't noticed any pain or burning when passing urine."*

*"I also considered pelvic inflammatory disease, which is an infection of the reproductive organs, though this is less likely given there has been no vaginal discharge or recent change in sexual partners."*

**Principle 3: The list continues until the bell**

Candidates are advised not to set a predetermined number of differentials to deliver. The bell concludes the station; until it sounds, the candidate should continue presenting diagnoses. Stopping at five differentials because it "feels complete" is a strategic error. If the examiner's list contains eight diagnoses and the candidate names five, the candidate forfeits marks for the uncovered three.

For lower abdominal pain in a woman of reproductive age, the differential list should be drawn from the following conditions (presented here for candidate preparation, not necessarily in the order they will be delivered in the examination, which should reflect clinical prioritisation):

**Gynaecological causes:**
- Ectopic pregnancy
- Ovarian torsion
- Ruptured ovarian cyst (functional or pathological)
- Pelvic inflammatory disease
- Endometriosis
- Dysmenorrhoea (primary or secondary)
- Uterine fibroid with degeneration
- Ovarian cyst (unruptured, with torsion or stretch)
- Cervical pathology (less common)

**Gastrointestinal causes:**
- Appendicitis (provisional in this scenario)
- Mesenteric adenitis
- Irritable bowel syndrome
- Inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis)
- Colorectal malignancy
- Constipation with faecal loading
- Diverticular disease (less common in this age group but not excluded)
- Hernia (inguinal, femoral)

**Urological causes:**
- Urinary tract infection / cystitis
- Ureteric calculus (renal colic)
- Pyelonephritis

**Musculoskeletal:**
- Psoas abscess
- Rectus sheath haematoma

**Other:**
- Diabetic ketoacidosis (abdominal pain as a systemic manifestation)
- Herpes zoster (pre-eruptive phase with dermatomal pain)

**Principle 4: Prioritisation reflects clinical probability**

The most clinically likely and most dangerous diagnoses should be presented first, progressing to less likely alternatives. This reflects genuine clinical reasoning and demonstrates to the examiner that the candidate is not simply reciting a textbook index.

---

### Managing Patient Interaction During Diagnosis Delivery

A patient who asks questions during the differential list is a reality of the examination. When a patient asks for clarification of an unfamiliar term, the candidate must provide a brief, two-sentence plain-language explanation and resume the list immediately. The explanation should be the simplest anatomically accurate description possible:

- Ectopic pregnancy: *"That's when a pregnancy grows in the tube that carries the egg to the womb, rather than inside the womb itself."*
- Ovarian torsion: *"That's when the ovary, one of the organs that produces your eggs, twists on itself and cuts off its own blood supply."*
- Pelvic inflammatory disease: *"That's an infection that travels up from the vagina and affects the uterus and fallopian tubes."*

The candidate's objective during the differential list is maximum throughput of specific, reasoned diagnoses. Lengthy explanations at this stage are counter-productive. After providing the brief explanation, the candidate should return immediately to the list: *"Continuing with the other possibilities I wanted to mention..."*

Empathic acknowledgement of patient distress during this phase is appropriate and brief: *"I understand this is a lot of information — I want to make sure I give you the full picture before we talk about what we do next."* Extensive empathic exploration at this point is, however, an inappropriate use of limited station time if the differential list remains incomplete.

## Differential Diagnosis

| Category | Diagnosis | Key Distinguishing Features |
|----------|-----------|----------------------------|
| Common — Most Likely | Appendicitis | Periumbilical pain migrating to right iliac fossa, anorexia, nausea, rebound tenderness, low-grade fever, elevated WCC |
| Common | Ovarian cyst (ruptured or unruptured) | Sudden onset unilateral pain, mid-cycle or post-coital, may have haemoperitoneum if ruptured |
| Common | Urinary tract infection / cystitis | Dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic discomfort; urinalysis positive for nitrites and leucocytes |
| Common | Pelvic inflammatory disease | Bilateral lower abdominal pain, cervical motion tenderness, vaginal discharge, sexually active, often bilateral |
| Common | Primary dysmenorrhoea | Cyclical pain coinciding with menstruation, no fever, normal examination outside menstrual period |
| Common | Irritable bowel syndrome | Chronic relapsing abdominal pain, altered bowel habit, bloating, no systemic features |
| Important | Ectopic pregnancy | Amenorrhoea or irregular bleeding, unilateral pain, shoulder tip pain if ruptured, haemodynamic instability in severe cases |
| Important | Endometriosis | Cyclical pelvic pain, dyspareunia, dyschezia, infertility; often delayed diagnosis |
| Important | Ovarian torsion | Sudden severe unilateral pain, nausea and vomiting, absent ovarian blood flow on Doppler ultrasound |
| Important | Ureteric calculus | Colicky flank-to-groin pain, haematuria, patient unable to find comfortable position |
| Important | Inflammatory bowel disease | Chronic diarrhoea with blood and mucus, systemic features, perianal disease (Crohn's) |
| Must Not Miss | Ectopic pregnancy (ruptured) | Haemodynamic compromise, peritoneal signs, shoulder tip pain; immediate surgical emergency |
| Must Not Miss | Ovarian torsion | Surgical emergency; irreversible adnexal ischaemia without prompt detorsion |
| Must Not Miss | Appendicitis with perforation | Peritonitis, systemic sepsis; failure to diagnose risks perforation and death |
| Must Not Miss | Colorectal malignancy | Change in bowel habit, rectal bleeding, unexplained weight loss, anaemia; rare in this age group but must not be dismissed without adequate clinical reasoning |

## Red Flags — Must Not Miss

- **Haemodynamic instability (tachycardia, hypotension, pallor, diaphoresis)** — Raises immediate concern for ruptured ectopic pregnancy, ruptured ovarian cyst with significant haemoperitoneum, or perforated appendicitis with septicaemia. Requires immediate resuscitation and urgent surgical review; do not delay with further history.

- **Positive or uncertain urine beta-hCG in a woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain** — Ectopic pregnancy must be excluded before any other diagnostic pathway is pursued. Even in a woman who reports regular menstruation, ectopic pregnancy cannot be excluded without a pregnancy test.

- **Shoulder tip pain** — Referred pain from diaphragmatic irritation by intraperitoneal haemorrhage. This is a hallmark of ruptured ectopic pregnancy or significant haemoperitoneum from any cause and requires immediate surgical consultation.

- **Peritoneal signs (rigid abdomen, guarding, rebound tenderness beyond the right iliac fossa)** — Indicates peritonitis from perforated viscus or extensive intra-abdominal pathology. Requires immediate surgical assessment.

- **Cessation of bowel sounds with abdominal rigidity** — Paralytic ileus secondary to peritonitis; surgical emergency.

- **Fever above 38.5°C with lower abdominal tenderness and cervical motion tenderness** — Suggests tubo-ovarian abscess or severe pelvic inflammatory disease requiring hospitalisation and intravenous antibiotics.

- **Absent or markedly diminished ovarian blood flow on Doppler ultrasound** — Diagnostic of ovarian torsion; immediate gynaecological surgical consultation required to attempt detorsion and preserve adnexal function.

- **Unexplained significant weight loss with change in bowel habit** — In any patient with lower abdominal pain, these features mandate investigation to exclude colorectal malignancy, regardless of age.

- **Immunosuppression or pregnancy** — Attenuates the clinical signs of peritonitis and sepsis; a high index of suspicion must be maintained and thresholds for investigation and escalation lowered accordingly.

## Investigations

**Bedside:**
- Urine beta-hCG (mandatory in all women of reproductive age with abdominal pain)
- Urinalysis with microscopy (nitrites, leucocytes, blood, protein)
- Blood glucose (exclude diabetic ketoacidosis as a cause of abdominal pain)
- Observations including temperature, pulse, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, and respiratory rate

**Bloods:**
- Full blood count (leucocytosis in appendicitis, anaemia in haemorrhage or malignancy)
- C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (inflammatory markers)
- Urea, electrolytes, and creatinine (renal function, hydration status)
- Liver function tests (exclude hepatic and biliary pathology)
- Serum lipase (exclude pancreatitis if pain extends to epigastrium)
- Serum beta-hCG (quantitative, if urine test positive or equivocal)
- Blood cultures (if systemic sepsis suspected)
- Group and hold / crossmatch (if ruptured ectopic or significant haemorrhage suspected)
- Coagulation studies (if coagulopathy or haemorrhagic emergency)

**Imaging:**
- Pelvic and abdominal ultrasound (first-line imaging in women of reproductive age; evaluates ovaries, uterus, free fluid, appendix if visualised)
- Transvaginal ultrasound (superior sensitivity for adnexal pathology and intrauterine pregnancy; performed in conjunction with transabdominal)
- CT abdomen and pelvis with contrast (if ultrasound inconclusive and appendicitis or other surgical pathology strongly suspected; consider radiation exposure in women of reproductive age)
- MRI pelvis (preferred over CT in pregnancy; also useful for characterising complex pelvic masses and endometriosis)

**Specialist:**
- Diagnostic laparoscopy (gold standard for definitive evaluation of appendicitis, ovarian torsion, ectopic pregnancy, and endometriosis when imaging is inconclusive and clinical suspicion remains high)
- Gynaecological assessment with cervical swabs (if pelvic inflammatory disease suspected)
- Surgical referral (appendicitis, hernia, bowel pathology)

## Management

**Immediate priorities (apply to any patient with haemodynamic compromise):**
- Establish intravenous access with two large-bore cannulae
- Commence intravenous crystalloid resuscitation
- Continuous monitoring of vital signs
- Analgesia: intravenous morphine titrated to pain (does not mask peritoneal signs and should not be withheld)
- Nil by mouth if surgical intervention anticipated
- Urgent surgical and/or gynaecological consultation
- Crossmatch and activate massive transfusion protocol if ruptured ectopic or major haemorrhage confirmed

**Appendicitis (provisional diagnosis in this scenario):**
- Surgical referral for appendicectomy (laparoscopic approach preferred in women of reproductive age to simultaneously evaluate pelvic structures)
- Nil by mouth from time of diagnosis
- Intravenous antibiotics pre-operatively as per institutional protocol (typically cephalosporin plus metronidazole)
- Analgesia as above
- Consent for appendicectomy including discussion of laparotomy if laparoscopic approach not feasible
- Post-operative recovery, wound care, and return-to-work advice

**Ectopic pregnancy (if confirmed):**
- Haemodynamically stable with small ectopic: methotrexate (medical management) — requires strict follow-up of serial serum beta-hCG levels and absence of contraindications
- Haemodynamically unstable or failed medical management: emergency laparoscopic salpingotomy or salpingectomy
- Rhesus status and anti-D immunoglobulin if Rh-negative

**Pelvic inflammatory disease:**
- Outpatient: oral doxycycline plus metronidazole for 14 days (as per current Australian STI management guidelines)
- Inpatient (tubo-ovarian abscess, systemic sepsis, failed outpatient therapy): intravenous ceftriaxone plus doxycycline plus metronidazole
- Partner notification and treatment
- Follow-up in 72 hours to assess clinical response

**Ovarian torsion:**
- Emergency laparoscopic detorsion; oophorectomy if adnexa non-viable
- Cystectomy if underlying cyst identified

**Ongoing management (all presentations):**
- Clear discharge instructions with specific return-to-emergency criteria
- Written information in plain language
- General practitioner follow-up arranged prior to discharge
- Mental health and psychological support if diagnosis carries significant impact (e.g., ectopic pregnancy with loss of tube, malignancy)

## Communication and Patient Education

**Explaining the diagnosis:**
The explanation of appendicitis (or whichever provisional diagnosis applies) must move efficiently from technical accuracy to patient comprehension. The anatomical explanation should be simple: *"The appendix is a small, finger-shaped pouch attached to the large bowel, usually in the lower right part of your abdomen. In your case, this appendix has become inflamed and infected."* Avoid lengthy anatomical diagrams or complex physiological explanations; the patient requires a functional understanding sufficient to consent to treatment, not a medical education.

**Questions the patient is likely to ask:**
- *"How did this happen?"* — For appendicitis: *"We don't always know the exact cause, but it often happens when the opening of the appendix becomes blocked by stool or mucus, leading to inflammation."*
- *"Is it serious?"* — *"Yes, it does require treatment, and the good news is that treating it now gives you an excellent outcome. Without treatment, it can become more serious."*
- *"Will I need an operation?"* — *"Yes, the standard treatment is a surgical procedure to remove the appendix. We can discuss this in more detail when you meet the surgical team."*
- *"What happens if it's not treated?"* — *"If left untreated, the appendix can rupture, which can cause a serious infection throughout the abdomen. This is why we need to act today."*
- *"Will I be able to go home today?"* — *"You'll likely need to stay in hospital. The surgical team will talk you through the timeline."*

**Addressing concerns about the differential diagnoses:**
When delivering differentials, the candidate should frame each alternative as a responsible and thorough consideration, not as an expression of diagnostic uncertainty that undermines confidence. *"There are a few other conditions I want to make sure we rule out — not because I think they're more likely, but because being thorough is how we make sure we get this right for you."*

For ectopic pregnancy specifically, the explanation must be sensitive and clear: *"One condition I need to exclude is called an ectopic pregnancy. This is when a pregnancy develops outside the uterus, in the fallopian tube. I want to check for this with a simple urine test because it can occasionally be serious, and it's important we don't miss it."*

**Written resources:**
- Provide written information about the provisional diagnosis, ideally from a reputable Australian source (e.g., healthdirect.gov.au, Royal Australian College of General Practitioners patient information sheets)
- Ensure instructions are in plain English and, where applicable, translated resources are offered for patients with limited English proficiency
- Document that written information was provided in the clinical record

## AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

- **Read the task wording with surgical precision.** "Explain the most likely diagnosis" requires one provisional diagnosis with reasons. "Explain your likely diagnoses" requires a prioritised differential list. Missing the plural has caused candidates to score zero on differentials.

- **Always state the medical term and then immediately explain it in plain language.** Saying "an infection" without naming cystitis, pelvic inflammatory disease, or tubo-ovarian abscess will not score marks for those specific diagnoses on the examiner's checklist. Conversely, stating the medical term without explanation violates communication criteria.

- **Your clinical reasoning must be explicit and linked to specific findings.** "I think you have appendicitis because your pain shifted from around your belly button to the lower right, you lost your appetite, and I found rebound tenderness on examination" scores. "I think you have appendicitis because of your symptoms" does not.

- **Do not self-limit your differential list.** The examiner's reference list may contain eight to ten diagnoses. Stopping at five because the time feels short is a strategic error. Continue listing differentials until the bell sounds.

- **Each differential must include at least one reason, even a brief clause.** "I was also thinking about ectopic pregnancy, but it's less likely because your last period was normal" takes four seconds and satisfies the reasoning criterion for that differential.

- **Do not migrate into investigations and management unless that is your assigned task.** The psychological urge to discuss ultrasound and surgery after diagnosing appendicitis is well recognised but penalised. If the task does not include investigations and management, time spent there is time stolen from differentials.

- **Prepare your differentials before the examination by memorising condition-specific lists.** For lower abdominal pain in a woman, you should be able to name at least twelve specific diagnoses without pause: appendicitis, ectopic pregnancy, ovarian torsion, ruptured ovarian cyst, pelvic inflammatory disease, endometriosis, primary dysmenorrhoea, cystitis, pyelonephritis, ureteric calculus, irritable bowel syndrome, and inflammatory bowel disease — before considering less common diagnoses.

- **Empathy is assessed, but it is assessed on quality and timing, not quantity.** A single well-placed empathic statement at the appropriate moment — typically when the patient expresses fear or distress — scores better than repeated empathic statements that consume time without advancing the clinical encounter.

- **Active listening during history taking directly determines differential quality.** A candidate who cuts off the patient's spontaneous account to proceed to the next structured question misses qualifiers that distinguish diagnoses (e.g., the patient mentioning that the pain began around the navel, or that she has been feeling feverish — details that distinguish appendicitis from musculoskeletal pain).

- **In 2025 AMC Clinical Examinations, "approach to the patient" is scored holistically across the entire encounter.** This includes the diagnostic explanation phase — plain language used, technical terms explained, patient understanding checked, and concerns addressed — not only the opening empathic statement. Candidates should demonstrate consistent communication quality throughout.

- **Avoid mutually exclusive or overlapping diagnoses.** Stating "upper or lower respiratory tract infection" is not a specific diagnosis and will not be credited. Stating "pneumonia" and "tonsillitis" as separate differentials with separate reasons satisfies specificity criteria.

- **Never describe a serious diagnosis euphemistically to the point of obfuscation.** If malignancy is a differential, use the word "cancer" and explain it plainly. A patient who leaves the encounter without understanding that cancer was considered and requires exclusion has been inadequately informed, and this constitutes a communication failure.

## Safety Net

**Before the patient leaves the clinical encounter, the following safety net instructions must be provided — verbally and in writing where possible:**

**Return immediately to the emergency department if:**
- The pain becomes significantly worse or spreads to involve the whole abdomen
- Fever develops or worsens (temperature above 38.0°C)
- Vomiting prevents fluid intake
- The patient feels faint, dizzy, or collapses
- The abdomen becomes rigid or board-like
- There is shoulder tip pain (referred pain from diaphragmatic irritation)
- Vaginal bleeding occurs or worsens
- The patient feels her condition is deteriorating in any way

**Follow-up timeline:**
- If admitted for surgery (appendicitis): surgical team to arrange post-operative review at two to four weeks; general practitioner review within one week of discharge
- If managed conservatively and discharged: general practitioner review within 24 to 48 hours; strict return-to-emergency criteria as above
- If ectopic pregnancy treated medically with methotrexate: serial serum beta-hCG at days four and seven post-treatment; gynaecology outpatient review arranged prior to discharge
- If awaiting further investigations: results to be communicated within 24 hours; patient provided with direct contact number for the treating unit

**Written safety net:**
- Provide a printed or handwritten summary of the diagnosis, the plan, and the emergency return criteria
- Confirm the patient (or their nominated support person) has understood the information
- Document in the clinical record that safety netting advice was provided and comprehended

**Social and cultural considerations:**
- If the patient has limited English proficiency, arrange a professional interpreter for the safety net discussion; do not rely on family members to interpret medical information
- Explore whether the patient has transport and social support available, particularly if same-day surgery or hospital admission is required
- Acknowledge any cultural or religious considerations relevant to surgical consent, blood transfusion, or examination preferences, and document these in the clinical record
