# GORD Counselling — Explaining Diagnosis, Complications, and Management to the Patient

## Clinical Presentation

Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) is one of the most prevalent gastrointestinal conditions encountered in Australian general practice and emergency medicine, affecting approximately 10–15% of the adult population. In the AMC Clinical Examination, GORD counselling stations test the candidate's ability to translate complex pathophysiology into accessible language, deliver a confirmed endoscopic diagnosis with its implications, and co-develop a comprehensive management plan with the patient — all within a tightly structured eight-minute encounter.

This station presents a patient who has undergone upper gastrointestinal endoscopy following a history of reflux symptoms. The key clinical distinction here is that the endoscopy has demonstrated objective mucosal injury — oesophagitis — confirming not merely symptomatic reflux but established GORD with end-organ damage. This distinction is clinically important: erosive oesophagitis carries a defined complication profile, mandates pharmacological intervention, and requires endoscopic surveillance. The candidate must communicate this distinction clearly without using medical abbreviations or generating unnecessary alarm.

The station is weighted heavily toward communication skills (12 marks out of a possible 16), with a smaller component for history refinement (4 marks). Physical examination is not required. Candidates who succeed in this station demonstrate fluent patient-centred communication, a methodical management structure delivered as clear bullet points, culturally appropriate language, and a robust safety-netting conversation — not a comprehensive lecture on gastroenterology.

---

## Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

This is a diagnosis-delivery and counselling communication station. The examiner is assessing the candidate's ability to:

1. **Deliver a confirmed diagnosis** in plain language, without jargon, using a logical anatomical explanation
2. **Explain complications** accurately and proportionately — neither minimising risk nor catastrophising
3. **Present a structured management plan** covering pharmacological, dietary, lifestyle, and general advice components
4. **Address the patient's ICE** (Ideas, Concerns, and Expectations) before and after information delivery
5. **Safety-net effectively** with specific red flags, follow-up arrangements, and patient resources

The expected structure for this communication station is:

- **Opening:** Brief re-establishment of rapport; confirm the patient is ready to receive results
- **ICE elicitation (pre-disclosure):** Establish what the patient already understands, what they are worried about, and what they hope to learn today — before launching into explanation
- **Diagnosis delivery:** Structured, layered explanation using simple anatomical language; use visual aids (e.g., a diagram of the oesophagus and stomach) if available
- **Complication discussion:** Balanced, proportionate explanation of what may occur if the condition is left untreated
- **Management plan:** Delivered in clear bullet-point structure — medications, dietary modification, lifestyle changes, general advice
- **ICE revisitation:** Check understanding, address residual concerns, confirm expectations have been met
- **Safety netting and follow-up:** Clear instructions on red flag symptoms, review endoscopy timeline, and patient education resources
- **Closing:** Summarise, offer written information, confirm next appointment

## Systematic Approach

### ICE Framework — Eliciting the Patient's Perspective

Before delivering any information, the candidate must briefly establish the patient's existing understanding and concerns. In a results-delivery station, this serves two functions: it prevents the candidate from repeating information the patient already has, and it allows the management discussion to be tailored to what the patient values most.

**Ideas:**
- *"Before I go through everything, can you tell me what you've been thinking might be causing your symptoms?"*
- *"Has anyone discussed with you what we might find on the camera test?"*
- The patient may have self-diagnosed reflux, or may have significant anxiety about the possibility of cancer — the latter being a common undisclosed concern in patients presenting with chronic dyspeptic symptoms.

**Concerns:**
- *"Is there anything in particular you've been worried about?"*
- *"Some people find a camera test a little concerning — did you have any worries about what we might find?"*
- The most important concern to surface in this station is fear of oesophageal or gastric cancer. This must be addressed explicitly, empathetically, and directly.

**Expectations:**
- *"What were you hoping we could sort out for you today?"*
- *"What would be most helpful to know by the end of our conversation?"*
- Most patients expect a clear explanation of the diagnosis, a treatment plan, and reassurance about prognosis.

---

### Delivering the Diagnosis — Plain Language Explanation

The candidate should begin the explanation with a clear, non-jargon statement of the diagnosis, followed immediately by a lay explanation.

**Recommended opening statement:**
> *"The camera test — the endoscopy — has come back and it has shown us something important. You have a condition called gastro-oesophageal reflux disease. This has caused some inflammation in your food pipe, which we call oesophagitis."*

Avoid acronyms such as "GORD" without explanation. Do not use the term alone without the full anatomical context.

**Anatomical explanation using a simple model:**
Use a simple diagram or gesture to illustrate the following:

- *"You have a food pipe — which connects your mouth to your stomach — and a stomach, which produces acid to digest food."*
- *"Between the food pipe and the stomach, there is a muscular gateway — a valve — that is supposed to stay closed after food passes through, to keep the acid down in the stomach where it belongs."*
- *"In your case, this gateway is not working as it should. It has become a little loose or weakened, and some of the acid from your stomach is able to travel back up into your food pipe."*
- *"Acid is designed to be in the stomach — the stomach has a special protective lining. Your food pipe does not have the same protection, so when acid reaches it repeatedly, it causes irritation and inflammation. This is what we have found — oesophagitis — inflammation of the food pipe."*

This explanation should take no more than 60–90 seconds. The candidate should pause and check for understanding: *"Does that make sense so far? Do you have any questions about what I've just explained?"*

### Complications of Untreated GORD — Proportionate Disclosure

The candidate must explain potential complications if the condition is not managed. This conversation must be conducted with appropriate framing — these are risks of inadequate treatment, not inevitable outcomes. The purpose is to motivate engagement with management, not to alarm the patient.

**Complications to cover:**

- **Oesophageal ulcers:** *"If the inflammation continues long-term without treatment, it can cause ulcers — small sores — in the lining of the food pipe."*
- **Bleeding:** *"These ulcers can sometimes bleed, which is why we take this seriously."*
- **Stricture formation:** *"Over many years, the repeated damage and healing process can cause the food pipe to narrow — we call this a stricture — which can make swallowing difficult."*
- **Increased cancer risk:** *"The most important reason to treat this properly is that long-standing, untreated inflammation in the food pipe can, over time, increase the risk of developing a certain type of cancer of the food pipe. This is the most serious potential complication, and it is why we monitor and treat GORD carefully."*
  - This should be delivered calmly and directly: *"I want to be honest with you — if we treat this well and follow you up with regular checks, the risk of reaching that point is significantly reduced."*
- **Barrett's oesophagus** (for higher-level candidates): Chronic acid exposure can cause a change in the cell type lining the lower oesophagus — a condition called Barrett's oesophagus — which is a precancerous change requiring surveillance endoscopy.

**Framing statement after complications:**
> *"The good news is that all of these complications are preventable with the right treatment and follow-up. Let me take you through exactly what we are going to do."*

### Management Plan — Structured Bullet Points

The AMC Clinical Examination rewards candidates who deliver management in a clear, organised, patient-accessible structure. Avoid lengthy paragraphs. Present each component as a distinct, logical step.

#### 1. Pharmacological Management — Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs)

> *"The first and most important treatment is medication. I am going to start you on a type of medication called a proton pump inhibitor — or a PPI. These are anti-acid medications. Their job is to significantly reduce the amount of acid your stomach produces."*

- Examples include omeprazole, pantoprazole, lansoprazole, and esomeprazole — the candidate should be familiar with at least two.
- Standard dosing for erosive oesophagitis: typically **double-dose PPI for 8 weeks**, followed by reassessment.
- *"These medications are very effective and are among the most commonly used medicines in Australia for this condition."*
- Advise the patient to take the PPI **30 minutes before the first meal of the day** for optimal efficacy.

**Medication review — NSAIDs and aspirin:**
- If the patient is taking NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen, diclofenac for musculoskeletal pain): *"I will need you to stop this type of painkiller, as it can make your food pipe inflammation significantly worse. I will prescribe you paracetamol as a safer alternative for your pain."*
- If the patient requires low-dose aspirin for cardiovascular protection (e.g., post-myocardial infarction, stent): *"In your case, the benefit of continuing aspirin for your heart outweighs the risk to your food pipe. I will keep you on the aspirin but ensure your stomach acid is very well controlled with a high-dose PPI."*
- Anti-platelet agents (e.g., clopidogrel) in the setting of recent cardiac events should not be discontinued without specialist input.

#### 2. Dietary Modification

Specific dietary triggers to address:

- **Avoid:** Coffee, tea, caffeinated beverages, carbonated drinks (including cola), alcohol, chocolate, peppermint
- **Reduce:** Fatty foods, spicy foods, tomato-based products and sauces, citrus juices
- **Portion size:** *"I want you to move to smaller, lighter meals rather than large meals. A full stomach puts more pressure on that gateway valve and makes acid reflux more likely."*
- **Timing:** *"Please do not lie down within two to three hours of eating. Gravity helps keep the acid down — when you lie flat immediately after a meal, it is much easier for acid to travel back up."*

#### 3. Lifestyle Modification

- **Weight management:** *"Excess weight around the abdomen increases pressure on the stomach and on the gateway valve. Losing even a small amount of weight — five to ten percent of your body weight — can make a meaningful difference to your symptoms."*
- **Regular physical activity:** Moderate aerobic exercise supports weight management and has been shown to reduce reflux symptom frequency.
- **Smoking cessation:** Smoking relaxes the lower oesophageal sphincter and directly worsens reflux. This should be addressed explicitly, with referral to Quitline (13 7848) if appropriate.
- **Alcohol reduction:** Alcohol is a direct oesophageal irritant and relaxes the lower oesophageal sphincter.

#### 4. General and Positional Advice

- **Elevate the head of the bed:** *"Raising the head end of your bed by approximately 15–20 centimetres — either with bed risers or by placing firm pillows under the mattress — reduces night-time reflux significantly. This is different from simply adding extra pillows under your head, which can actually curve the spine and worsen symptoms."*
- **Avoid tight-fitting clothing:** Abdominal constriction increases intra-abdominal pressure.
- **Avoid eating within two to three hours of bedtime.**
- **Avoid bending or stooping after meals** where possible.

### If H. pylori Is Identified — Management Modification

If endoscopy or ancillary testing identifies *Helicobacter pylori* co-infection, the management plan expands to include eradication therapy:

- **Triple therapy** (standard first-line in Australia):
  - PPI (standard dose, twice daily) +
  - Amoxicillin 1g twice daily +
  - Clarithromycin 500mg twice daily
  - Duration: 7–14 days (14 days preferred for improved eradication rates)
- Following completion of eradication therapy, confirm eradication using the **urea breath test (UBT)**:
  - *"We will arrange a breath test approximately four weeks after you finish the antibiotics to confirm that the bacteria has been successfully eliminated."*
  - The UBT involves ingesting a small labelled urea compound; if *H. pylori* is present, it metabolises the compound and the labelled carbon dioxide is detected in exhaled breath.
  - PPIs must be withheld for two weeks and antibiotics for four weeks prior to the breath test to avoid false-negative results.

## Differential Diagnosis

While the endoscopic diagnosis of GORD with oesophagitis has been confirmed, the candidate should be aware of the broader differential context for this presentation, particularly conditions that may coexist or complicate the clinical picture.

| Category | Diagnosis | Key Distinguishing Features |
|---|---|---|
| Common | GORD (confirmed) | Endoscopic oesophagitis; lower oesophageal sphincter dysfunction; acid reflux symptoms |
| Common | Functional dyspepsia | Epigastric discomfort without structural abnormality on endoscopy; Rome IV criteria |
| Common | *H. pylori* gastritis | Urease-positive on biopsy or breath test; antral erythema on endoscopy; treatable |
| Important | Barrett's oesophagus | Columnar metaplasia of distal oesophagus on biopsy; long-segment vs short-segment; surveillance required |
| Important | Peptic ulcer disease | Endoscopic ulcer in stomach or duodenum; may coexist with GORD; *H. pylori* or NSAID-associated |
| Must Not Miss | Oesophageal carcinoma | Dysphagia, odynophagia, weight loss, anaemia; endoscopic mass or stricture; urgent specialist referral |
| Must Not Miss | Gastric carcinoma | Alarm symptoms — early satiety, weight loss, vomiting, palpable epigastric mass; biopsy mandatory |
| Must Not Miss | Eosinophilic oesophagitis | Young adults, atopic history, food bolus obstruction; endoscopic rings or furrows; dietary or steroid therapy |

## Red Flags — Must Not Miss

These symptoms require urgent review and must not be attributed to GORD without further investigation:

- **Progressive dysphagia (difficulty swallowing)** — May indicate oesophageal stricture, malignancy, or achalasia. Any patient reporting food sticking should be urgently re-referred for endoscopy and surgical assessment.
- **Odynophagia (pain on swallowing)** — Suggests severe mucosal injury, infective oesophagitis (immunocompromised patients), or malignancy.
- **Unintentional weight loss** — A cardinal symptom of gastrointestinal malignancy; mandates urgent investigation regardless of prior benign endoscopy findings.
- **Haematemesis (vomiting blood) or coffee-ground vomiting** — Indicates active upper gastrointestinal haemorrhage; requires emergency presentation and resuscitation.
- **Melaena (black, tarry stools)** — Suggests upper gastrointestinal bleeding; can indicate bleeding oesophageal or gastric ulcer. Requires same-day emergency assessment.
- **Persistent vomiting despite treatment** — May indicate gastroparesis, pyloric obstruction, or malignancy.
- **New or worsening anaemia** — Iron deficiency anaemia in the context of GORD should prompt investigation for occult gastrointestinal bleeding.
- **Onset after age 55, or new symptoms in an older patient** — Lower threshold for endoscopy and biopsy; increased background risk of malignancy.
- **Chest pain atypical for reflux** — Must exclude acute coronary syndrome before attributing chest pain to GORD. Cardiac causes take diagnostic precedence.

## Investigations

**Bedside:**
- Urine dipstick (if systemic illness suspected)
- Stool occult blood testing (if melaena or anaemia)
- Urea breath test (for *H. pylori* detection post-eradication, or non-invasive primary diagnosis)

**Bloods:**
- Full blood count — assess for iron deficiency anaemia secondary to occult blood loss
- Iron studies, serum ferritin — confirm iron deficiency
- Liver function tests, albumin — assess nutritional status and hepatic disease
- Coeliac serology (anti-tTG IgA) — if dyspepsia with weight loss or diarrhoea
- *H. pylori* serology (less preferred than breath test for active infection)

**Imaging / Endoscopy:**
- **Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (OGD):** Gold standard — already performed in this case; demonstrated oesophagitis. Repeat surveillance endoscopy recommended every 1–2 years in established erosive oesophagitis or Barrett's oesophagus.
- **Oesophageal manometry:** Indicated if atypical symptoms, dysphagia, or if surgical fundoplication is being considered — assesses lower oesophageal sphincter pressure and peristaltic function.
- **24-hour pH impedance monitoring:** Gold standard for quantifying acid and non-acid reflux episodes, particularly in refractory or atypical presentations.
- **Barium swallow:** Of limited diagnostic utility in GORD; occasionally used for structural assessment in dysphagia.

**Specialist Referral:**
- Gastroenterology referral for: Barrett's oesophagus surveillance, refractory GORD, consideration of anti-reflux surgery (Nissen fundoplication), or alarm features.
- Upper gastrointestinal surgery referral if surgical management is indicated (young patient, medication intolerance, hiatus hernia).

## Management

### Acute Management
For confirmed erosive oesophagitis, pharmacological therapy should commence immediately:

- **Proton pump inhibitor (PPI):** Full therapeutic dose (e.g., esomeprazole 40mg or pantoprazole 40mg orally, once daily, 30 minutes before breakfast) for a minimum of 8 weeks
- Review concomitant medications that may worsen GORD: NSAIDs, aspirin, bisphosphonates, calcium channel blockers, nitrates, tricyclic antidepressants, benzodiazepines
- Substitute or deprescribe offending agents where clinically safe
- If *H. pylori* is identified: initiate triple therapy eradication regimen as outlined above

### Ongoing Management
- **Step-down therapy:** After initial 8-week healing course, attempt to reduce PPI to maintenance dosing or on-demand therapy if symptoms resolve. Avoid indefinite full-dose PPI without clinical indication.
- **Dietary and lifestyle modification:** Ongoing, indefinitely. Reinforce at every review.
- **Weight management:** Structured program (dietitian referral, GP-managed weight loss program) if BMI >25 kg/m²
- **Endoscopic surveillance:** Repeat OGD every 1–2 years in patients with confirmed oesophagitis; annually or biannually if Barrett's oesophagus is identified, depending on grade of dysplasia.
- **Anti-reflux surgery (Nissen fundoplication):** Considered in young patients with confirmed GORD, significant hiatus hernia, medication intolerance, or patient preference for surgical over long-term pharmacological management. Requires gastroenterology and surgical review.

### Stepwise Escalation for Refractory Symptoms
1. Confirm medication compliance and correct timing (30 minutes before meals)
2. Increase PPI to twice-daily dosing
3. Add alginate-based agent (e.g., Gaviscon) as adjunct, particularly for postprandial and nocturnal symptoms
4. Refer to gastroenterology for pH impedance study and further management

## Communication and Patient Education

### Explaining the Diagnosis
The language used should be lay-accessible, anatomically grounded, and free of medical shorthand. The phrase *"your gateway valve between the food pipe and the stomach is not working properly, allowing acid to travel upwards and irritate the lining"* is both accurate and comprehensible to the majority of patients. Repeat the anatomical explanation using a diagram or hand gesture if available.

### Addressing Cancer Concerns
This is the most emotionally significant part of the consultation. Many patients presenting with chronic upper gastrointestinal symptoms carry an undisclosed fear of cancer. The candidate must:
- Actively surface this concern rather than waiting for the patient to raise it: *"Some people in your situation are worried about whether this could be cancer — is that something that has crossed your mind?"*
- Provide a balanced, honest response: *"The endoscopy has not shown any cancer. What it has shown is inflammation, which, if left untreated long-term, does increase the risk of cancer developing. That is exactly why we are treating it and monitoring you regularly."*
- Avoid false reassurance (*"There is absolutely nothing to worry about"*) and avoid unnecessary alarm (*"You could get cancer if you don't follow my advice"*).

### Checking Understanding — Teach-Back
After delivering key information, use teach-back to confirm comprehension:
- *"Just to make sure I've explained this clearly — can you tell me in your own words what you understand about why this is happening?"*
- *"What will you do differently with your diet from now on?"*

### Written Patient Education Resources
In the Australian context, reliable patient-facing resources include:
- **Better Health Channel** (Victoria): betterhealth.vic.gov.au — comprehensive, plain-language information on GORD, oesophagitis, and healthy eating
- **Healthdirect Australia**: healthdirect.gov.au — national resource with symptom checkers and condition summaries
- **The Royal Australian College of General Practitioners (RACGP) patient fact sheets** — when available
- Provide the patient with written information at the conclusion of the consultation.

## AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

- **Deliver the diagnosis in the first 60–90 seconds.** Candidates who spend excessive time on rapport-building before the diagnosis lose critical marks on structure and time management. The examiner is assessing whether you can open, explain, and manage — not whether you can socialise.

- **Never abbreviate without explanation.** Saying "you have GORD" without the full term is a communication failure. Say "gastro-oesophageal reflux disease" in full, then simplify: *"This means acid from your stomach is travelling back up into your food pipe."*

- **Always elicit ICE before launching into explanation.** Even 30 seconds spent asking *"What have you been worrying this might be?"* and *"What were you hoping to find out today?"* demonstrates patient-centred practice and often reveals cancer anxiety that must be addressed.

- **Structure your management as numbered bullet points, not a monologue.** Examiners reward clarity of structure. Say: *"There are four main things we are going to do — let me go through each one."* This signals organisation and ensures nothing is omitted.

- **The most commonly omitted management component is non-pharmacological advice.** Candidates reliably mention PPIs but forget to cover head-of-bed elevation, meal timing, small portions, and avoiding lying down post-meals. These are explicitly tested.

- **Address cancer risk proactively and proportionately.** Do not wait for the patient to ask. Introducing it yourself — *"I want to explain why treating this properly matters"* — demonstrates clinical confidence and prevents the patient leaving with unaddressed anxiety.

- **Know your red flags verbatim.** In the AMC Clinical Examination, stating *"If you develop difficulty swallowing, blood in your vomit, black tarry stools, or unexplained weight loss, please present to emergency or contact me immediately"* is required safety-netting language. Vague phrases like *"come back if you feel worse"* are inadequate.

- **If *H. pylori* is mentioned, name triple therapy components.** Candidates who say *"I'll treat the bacteria"* without specifying PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin, and who fail to mention the confirmatory urea breath test, score lower on management completeness.

- **Do not stop cardiovascular medications without justification.** If the examiner introduces aspirin or clopidogrel in the context of previous MI or stent, the correct answer is to continue antiplatelet therapy with high-dose PPI cover — not to cease the antiplatelet agent.

- **Close the consultation explicitly.** Summarise in two sentences what was discussed, confirm the patient's next appointment, provide written information, and invite final questions. Candidates who finish abruptly without a structured close lose marks on communication closure.

- **Time management is assessed.** Aiming to cover diagnosis, complications, and management within five minutes allows three minutes for ICE revisitation, safety netting, and closing. Practice this timing explicitly in mock stations.

- **Use the Better Health Channel as a universal patient resource reference** when no specific pamphlet is available. Naming a real, credible Australian resource demonstrates awareness of local health literacy infrastructure.

## Safety Net

The safety-netting conversation should be delivered as a clear, specific set of instructions — not a generalised reassurance. The following structure is recommended:

**When to seek emergency care immediately (present to the nearest Emergency Department or call 000):**
- Vomiting blood (haematemesis), including any fresh red blood or dark "coffee-ground" material
- Passage of black, tarry stools (melaena) — indicating upper gastrointestinal bleeding
- Severe, sudden, or worsening chest pain not relieved by antacids — to exclude acute coronary syndrome
- Sudden inability to swallow or a sensation of food being completely stuck

**When to contact the practice urgently (same-day or next available appointment):**
- New or worsening difficulty swallowing solid or liquid food
- Unexplained weight loss of more than 3–5 kilograms
- Persistent vomiting or inability to keep food down despite medication
- Symptoms that are significantly worse despite one week of PPI therapy

**Scheduled follow-up:**
- General practice review in **4–6 weeks** to assess symptom response to PPI therapy, review medication tolerance, and reinforce lifestyle modifications
- Repeat upper gastrointestinal endoscopy in **1–2 years** (or sooner if symptoms change) for surveillance of oesophagitis healing and Barrett's oesophagus screening
- If *H. pylori* eradication therapy was commenced: urea breath test **4 weeks after completion** of antibiotic course (with PPI withheld for 2 weeks prior)

**Patient resources:**
- Better Health Channel: betterhealth.vic.gov.au
- Healthdirect Australia: healthdirect.gov.au (24-hour nurse helpline: 1800 022 222)
- Provide written summary of today's discussion and the management plan

> *"Before you leave today, I want to make sure you know exactly when to seek help. If you notice any blood when you vomit, or your stools turn black and tarry, please go straight to the emergency department. If you develop difficulty swallowing, or you are losing weight without trying, I need to see you urgently. Otherwise, let's make an appointment for four to six weeks' time to check how you are going on the medication. Does that plan sound clear to you?"*
