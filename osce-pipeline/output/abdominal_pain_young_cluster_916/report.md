# Chronic Abdominal Pain in Young Patients — AMC Clinical Examination History Taking Station

## Clinical Presentation

Chronic abdominal pain in a young patient represents a distinct and clinically challenging presentation that frequently appears in the AMC Clinical Examination. Unlike the acute abdomen presentations that constitute the majority of abdominal pain stations, this cluster involves patients aged 15–25 years presenting with pain persisting over weeks to months, often with a non-specific or diffuse distribution. The clinical challenge lies in the fact that the history alone may yield few definitive localising features, requiring the candidate to systematically exclude a broad range of gastrointestinal, genitourinary, and systemic diagnoses before arriving at a working differential.

Two prototypical scenarios exemplify this cluster. In the first, a 16-year-old male presents to general practice with several months of generalised abdominal pain, mild nausea, and intermittently soft stools, with physical examination revealing erythema nodosum on both lower legs — a finding that redirects the diagnosis toward inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). In the second, a 22-year-old male presents with six months of abdominal pain triggered by pasta and bread, associated with bloating, loose and malodorous stools that are difficult to flush, and a background of iron deficiency anaemia managed with supplementation — a constellation pointing toward coeliac disease with resultant malabsorption.

For AMC candidates, this station type tests the ability to conduct a thorough systematic history in the context of diagnostic uncertainty, appropriately expand the differential diagnosis toward chronic gastrointestinal conditions common in young adults, and communicate a reasoned diagnosis with supporting clinical evidence. The examiner specifically assesses whether the candidate avoids premature diagnostic closure, addresses psychosocial factors relevant to the young patient, and asks targeted questions for IBD, coeliac disease, lactose intolerance, and functional gastrointestinal disorders — conditions the acute abdomen framework alone does not adequately cover.

---

## Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

This is a six-minute history taking station. The examiner expects a structured, systematic approach that demonstrates both clinical competence and effective communication with a young patient. The six minutes must be used in their entirety; the breadth of the differential in this presentation warrants comprehensive questioning across multiple organ systems.

**Opening phase (30–60 seconds):** Confirm patient demographics and haemodynamic stability (heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature). Establish rapport with an open-ended question and allow the patient to provide a narrative statement. Respond with a bridging statement that acknowledges the patient's concern, demonstrates empathy, and transitions into structured history taking.

**Systematic history phase (3–4 minutes):** Apply the SOCRATES framework to characterise the pain, followed by a systematic review of gastrointestinal symptoms. In this station type, the candidate is expected to go beyond the standard acute abdomen framework and expand into targeted questions for IBD, coeliac disease, lactose intolerance, functional disorders, and chronic gastrointestinal infections. Red flag questions must be asked regardless of the patient's age.

**ICE elicitation (30–60 seconds):** Elicit the patient's Ideas (what they think is causing the pain), Concerns (what they are worried about), and Expectations (what they hope will happen). In young patients, concerns may include fear of serious illness, anxiety about school or work, or worry about the impact on daily life.

**Psychosocial and social assessment (30–60 seconds):** Conduct a brief HEADSS-style assessment for adolescent and young adult patients — Home, Education/Employment, Activities/Stress, Depression screening. Chronic pain in a young patient warrants consideration of psychogenic and functional contributions.

**Closing phase:** Summarise findings, explain the working diagnosis and differentials with clinical reasoning, outline the investigation plan, and provide a clear safety net.

## Systematic Approach

### SOCRATES Pain Assessment

Begin with an open-ended invitation: *"Can you tell me in your own words what's been happening with your stomach pain?"* Allow the patient to provide an uninterrupted narrative, then proceed systematically.

**Site:** Ask the patient to point to where the pain is. In this clinical cluster, the pain is frequently described as diffuse or generalised — *"all over my stomach."* Accept this answer and proceed; a non-localising pain does not preclude a systematic approach. However, note that upper abdominal predominance raises peptic ulcer disease, hepatobiliary, and gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD); lower abdominal predominance raises colonic, urinary, and pelvic pathology.

**Onset:** *"When did this pain first start? Did it come on suddenly or gradually?"* Establish clearly whether the onset was acute (hours to days) or insidious (weeks to months). The chronic nature — typically defined as pain persisting beyond four weeks — fundamentally changes the differential diagnosis toward IBD, coeliac disease, functional gastrointestinal disorders, and chronic infection.

**Character:** *"How would you describe the pain — is it sharp, dull, cramping, burning, or something else?"* Cramping or colicky pain is consistent with bowel pathology including IBD and coeliac disease. Burning epigastric pain suggests peptic ulcer disease or GORD.

**Radiation:** *"Does the pain move or spread anywhere — to your back, shoulder, or groin?"* Radiation to the back may suggest pancreatic or retroperitoneal pathology. Radiation to the groin raises renal colic. Diffuse non-radiating pain is consistent with functional disorders and IBD.

**Associated symptoms:** Systematically enquire about nausea, vomiting, bloating, flatulence, diarrhoea, constipation, fever, anorexia, and weight loss. Note the quality of any altered bowel habit — frequency, consistency, presence of blood or mucus. Soft, loose, or bulky stools are relevant even without frank diarrhoea.

**Timing:** *"Is the pain there all the time, or does it come and go? Is it getting worse, better, or staying the same? Are there particular times of day it is worse?"* Postprandial worsening is characteristic of coeliac disease and lactose intolerance. Nocturnal pain that wakes the patient suggests organic rather than functional pathology.

**Exacerbating and relieving factors:** *"Is there anything that makes the pain better or worse — specific foods, body position, bowel movements, stress?"* Pain relief in the foetal position is non-specific but may suggest peritoneal irritation. Worsening with gluten-containing foods (bread, pasta, wheat-based products) is a cardinal feature of coeliac disease. Worsening with dairy products suggests lactose intolerance.

**Severity:** *"On a scale of zero to ten, where zero is no pain and ten is the worst pain you can imagine, how would you rate your pain at its worst and on a typical day?"* Severity guides urgency but is less discriminating diagnostically in chronic presentations.

### Expanded Gastrointestinal Review — Targeted Differential Questioning

In young patients with chronic abdominal pain, the standard gastrointestinal review must be expanded beyond acute abdomen questions. The following subsections reflect the specific diagnostic hypotheses that must be evaluated.

#### Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) — Crohn's Disease and Ulcerative Colitis

IBD has a peak incidence in the second and third decades of life, making it a priority diagnosis in young patients with chronic abdominal pain. Beyond the standard diarrhoea and rectal bleeding questions, the examiner expects targeted enquiry into the extra-intestinal manifestations (EIMs) of IBD:

- **Joints:** *"Have you noticed any joint pain or swelling — particularly in your knees, ankles, or elbows?"* IBD-associated peripheral arthropathy and axial arthritis (ankylosing spondylitis) are well-recognised EIMs.
- **Eyes:** *"Have you had any redness or pain in your eyes, or any blurring of your vision?"* Uveitis and episcleritis occur in IBD and may precede or accompany bowel flares.
- **Skin:** *"Have you noticed any skin rashes, particularly on your legs or around your bottom?"* Erythema nodosum (tender red nodules on the shins) and pyoderma gangrenosum are characteristic cutaneous manifestations. When erythema nodosum is found on physical examination — as in the first case — it becomes the pivotal diagnostic clue that confirms IBD as the primary diagnosis despite a relatively non-specific bowel history.
- **Mouth:** *"Do you get mouth ulcers frequently?"* Aphthous ulceration is a recognised feature of Crohn's disease in particular.
- **Family history:** *"Does anyone in your family have IBD, Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, or any other bowel condition?"* There is a significant genetic predisposition to IBD.

#### Coeliac Disease

Coeliac disease may present at any age, and previously undiagnosed cases presenting in adolescence and young adulthood are well-documented. The classical malabsorption symptoms may be subtle or atypical.

- **Dietary triggers:** Rather than asking *"Are you on a special diet?"* (which may be interpreted as asking about a diagnosed condition), ask specifically: *"Have you noticed whether your symptoms are worse after eating bread, pasta, wheat-based cereals, or any baked goods?"* This directly probes gluten sensitivity without assuming prior knowledge.
- **Stool characteristics:** *"Can you describe your stools? Are they particularly loose, bulky, or smelly? Do they tend to float or stick to the pan and be difficult to flush?"* These layperson-friendly descriptors translate clinically to steatorrhoea — the hallmark of fat malabsorption in coeliac disease. Stools that float, are pale, malodorous, and difficult to flush are highly suggestive.
- **Nutritional deficiencies:** *"Have you been told you are anaemic? Have you noticed increased tiredness, pallor, or easy bruising?"* Iron deficiency anaemia in the absence of overt blood loss in a young patient is a red flag for malabsorptive pathology, particularly coeliac disease. In the second case, the background of iron deficiency anaemia managed with supplementation is a pivotal clue pointing toward coeliac disease.
- **Family history:** *"Does anyone in your family have coeliac disease or a gluten intolerance?"* Coeliac disease has strong HLA-DQ2/DQ8 genetic associations with first-degree relatives at significantly increased risk.

#### Lactose Intolerance

- *"Do you notice that your pain, bloating, or diarrhoea is triggered particularly by milk, cheese, yoghurt, or other dairy products?"* Symptom onset typically occurs 30 minutes to two hours after dairy ingestion. Lactose intolerance may be primary (adult-type hypolactasia) or secondary to intestinal mucosal damage from coeliac disease or gastroenteritis.

#### Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)

IBS is a diagnosis of exclusion but is common in young adults, particularly those with psychosocial stressors. Clinical features include altered bowel habit (diarrhoea-predominant, constipation-predominant, or mixed), bloating, abdominal cramping relieved by defaecation, and absence of alarm symptoms. The Rome IV criteria require symptoms for at least six months with active symptoms in the preceding three months.

#### Chronic Gastrointestinal Infection

- **Travel history:** *"Have you travelled overseas in the past 12 months? Where did you go? Did you have any gastrointestinal illness during or after your trip?"* Giardia lamblia, Cryptosporidium, and other protozoal infections may cause chronic or relapsing diarrhoea and abdominal pain following travel to endemic regions.
- **Occupation and exposures:** *"What do you do for work or study? Have you had any close contact with someone who has had similar symptoms?"*

### Upper Gastrointestinal, Genitourinary, and Other Differential Enquiry

Despite the chronic and diffuse nature of the pain, the systematic history must not neglect the standard upper and lower differential groups.

**Peptic ulcer disease and GORD:**
- *"Do you ever get a burning sensation in your chest or a sour or bitter taste in your mouth?"*
- *"Do you take anti-inflammatory medications such as ibuprofen or aspirin regularly?"* NSAID use is a major risk factor for peptic ulceration.
- *"Do you drink alcohol or coffee? Do you smoke?"* Even in a 16-year-old, alcohol and caffeine consumption must be enquired about tactfully.
- *"Do you eat a lot of spicy or fatty foods?"*

**Hepatobiliary:**
- *"Have you noticed any yellowing of your skin or the whites of your eyes?"*
- *"Have you had any previous episodes of severe pain after large or fatty meals?"* Biliary colic is characterised by episodic right upper quadrant or epigastric pain following fatty meals.

**Genitourinary:**
- *"Do you have any pain or burning when you pass urine? Are you passing urine more frequently than usual? Have you noticed any blood in your urine?"* Urinary tract infection, nephrolithiasis, and pelvic pathology in females must be excluded.
- In female patients: menstrual history is essential — endometriosis, ovarian cysts, and pelvic inflammatory disease are important differentials for chronic lower abdominal pain in young women.

**Metabolic — Diabetic Ketoacidosis:**
- *"Have you been unusually thirsty? Are you passing much more urine than usual? Have you lost weight recently?"* DKA presents acutely rather than chronically, but a first presentation of type 1 diabetes in a young patient with abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting warrants consideration when the history suggests new-onset polyuria and polydipsia. This is more relevant as an acute differential than a chronic one.

### Red Flag Screening

Red flag questions must be asked in every patient regardless of age. While malignancy is statistically uncommon in a 16-year-old, the questions serve the additional purpose of detecting complications of inflammatory conditions and systemic disease.

- *"Have you lost any weight recently, and was this intentional?"*
- *"Has your appetite changed — have you been eating less than usual?"*
- *"Have you noticed any blood in your stools — either red blood or dark, tarry-black stools?"*
- *"Have you felt any lumps or swellings anywhere in your body?"*
- *"Have you been feeling generally unwell, fatigued, or feverish?"*
- *"Have you had any night sweats?"*
- *"Has there been any change in your bowel habits over the past few months?"*
- *"Is there a family history of bowel cancer or any other cancers?"*

---

### Psychosocial Assessment — HEADSS Framework

In adolescent and young adult patients with chronic pain, psychosocial contributors must be assessed. A brief and sensitively conducted HEADSS assessment is appropriate:

- **Home:** *"Can you tell me a bit about your home situation — who do you live with? How are things at home?"*
- **Education/Employment:** *"Are you currently at school or working? How is that going? Are you finding it stressful?"*
- **Activities:** *"What do you do in your spare time? Do you exercise regularly?"*
- **Depression/Affect:** *"How has your mood been? Have you been feeling down, anxious, or stressed?"*
- **Social supports:** *"Do you have good support from friends and family?"*

Psychogenic abdominal pain and IBS are recognised diagnoses in young patients and may coexist with organic pathology. Acknowledging the mind-body connection without dismissing the patient's symptoms is a critical communication skill.

---

### ICE Elicitation

- **Ideas:** *"What do you think might be causing your stomach pain?"* Young patients may have significant anxiety about serious illness, particularly if symptoms have persisted without a clear diagnosis.
- **Concerns:** *"Is there anything particular you are worried this pain might be?"* Concerns about cancer, chronic illness, or impact on school, sport, or social life are common.
- **Expectations:** *"What were you hoping I could help you with today? What would make the biggest difference for you?"*

Addressing ICE directly demonstrates patient-centred care and significantly influences patient satisfaction scores in the AMC examination.

## Red Flags — Must Not Miss

- **Unintentional weight loss** — Raises concern for malignancy, severe malabsorption (coeliac disease, Crohn's disease), or systemic illness. Requires urgent investigation including full blood count, inflammatory markers, and timely specialist referral.

- **Rectal bleeding or melaena** — In any patient, rectal bleeding warrants colonoscopy to exclude IBD flare with mucosal ulceration, colorectal polyp, or, rarely, malignancy. Melaena suggests upper gastrointestinal haemorrhage.

- **Iron deficiency anaemia without clear cause** — In a young patient not menstruating heavily, unexplained iron deficiency anaemia strongly suggests gastrointestinal blood loss or malabsorption. Coeliac serology and upper and lower GI endoscopy are required.

- **Erythema nodosum** — Tender, red, firm nodules on the shins are a significant dermatological finding requiring systematic evaluation. In the context of chronic abdominal pain in a young patient, IBD is the leading diagnosis. Sarcoidosis must also be excluded (chest X-ray for bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy). Never dismiss skin findings as incidental in this clinical context.

- **Night sweats with constitutional symptoms** — Suggests lymphoma, tuberculosis, or severe systemic inflammatory disease. Requires haematological investigation and imaging.

- **Pain that wakes the patient from sleep (nocturnal pain)** — Distinguishes organic from functional pathology. IBS and psychogenic pain do not typically cause nocturnal waking. Nocturnal symptoms warrant investigation for peptic ulcer disease, IBD, or other structural pathology.

- **Family history of early-onset colorectal cancer or polyposis syndromes** — In patients with a first-degree relative diagnosed with colorectal cancer under 50 years of age, or in the context of known hereditary syndromes (Lynch syndrome, familial adenomatous polyposis), colonoscopic surveillance is indicated even in young adults.

- **Fever with abdominal pain** — Suggests infective or severe inflammatory aetiology. In the context of IBD, fever may indicate disease flare, abscess formation, or toxic megacolon.

- **Rapidly progressive or severe pain** — Warrants exclusion of acute surgical pathology (appendicitis, bowel obstruction, perforation) even in patients presenting with a background of chronic pain. A new acute-on-chronic presentation must not be attributed to the chronic condition without appropriate assessment.

## Investigations

**Bedside:**
- Urinalysis (dipstick and microscopy) — exclude urinary tract infection, haematuria, glycosuria
- Blood glucose level — screen for diabetes mellitus (in context of polyuria/polydipsia)
- Urine pregnancy test (females of reproductive age)
- Stool microscopy, culture, and sensitivity — assess for enteric pathogens, ova, and parasites (particularly Giardia) in those with travel history or chronic diarrhoea

**Bloods:**
- Full blood count (FBC) — anaemia (iron deficiency in coeliac disease, normocytic in chronic disease), leucocytosis (infection or IBD flare)
- Iron studies (serum iron, ferritin, TIBC) — iron deficiency anaemia as marker of malabsorption
- Coeliac serology: tissue transglutaminase IgA antibody (tTG-IgA) with total IgA level — gold standard serological screen for coeliac disease; must be performed on a normal gluten-containing diet
- Liver function tests (LFTs) — assess for hepatobiliary involvement; elevated GGT and ALP may suggest biliary or hepatic pathology; primary sclerosing cholangitis is associated with IBD
- Inflammatory markers: C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) — elevated in IBD, infection, and malignancy; normal in IBS and functional disorders
- Urea and electrolytes (U&E) — assess hydration and renal function
- Blood glucose, HbA1c — screen for diabetes mellitus
- Faecal calprotectin — elevated in IBD; useful non-invasive marker to differentiate organic bowel disease from IBS in primary care
- Thyroid function tests (TFTs) — hypothyroidism may present with constipation and fatigue in young patients
- Hepatitis A, B, C serology — if travel history or risk factors present

**Imaging:**
- Abdominal ultrasound — assess liver, gallbladder, biliary tree, kidneys, and ovaries; first-line imaging for right upper quadrant pain and suspected renal pathology
- Chest X-ray — mandatory when erythema nodosum is identified, to exclude bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy of sarcoidosis
- CT abdomen and pelvis — reserved for diagnostic uncertainty, suspected complications (abscess, fistula, obstruction), or when ultrasound is inconclusive; avoid in young patients unless clearly indicated given radiation exposure
- MRI small bowel (MR enterography) — preferred imaging modality for assessing small bowel involvement in Crohn's disease; avoids ionising radiation and is preferred in young patients

**Specialist:**
- Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (gastroscopy) with duodenal biopsies — definitive diagnosis of coeliac disease (Marsh classification on histology); diagnosis of peptic ulcer disease and H. pylori testing
- Colonoscopy with ileoscopy and biopsies — definitive diagnosis of IBD (Crohn's disease vs ulcerative colitis); biopsy of terminal ileum and multiple colonic segments
- Hydrogen breath testing — diagnosis of lactose intolerance and small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO)
- Referral to paediatric gastroenterologist or general gastroenterologist — depending on patient age, for endoscopic evaluation, biopsy, and ongoing management of IBD or coeliac disease
- Dermatology referral — if erythema nodosum or other dermatological manifestations require further assessment
- Ophthalmology referral — if uveitis or episcleritis is suspected

## Management

**Inflammatory Bowel Disease:**
- Refer to gastroenterologist for colonoscopy and biopsy to confirm diagnosis and distinguish Crohn's disease from ulcerative colitis
- Induction of remission: corticosteroids (prednisolone) for moderate-to-severe flares; aminosalicylates (mesalazine) for mild-to-moderate ulcerative colitis
- Maintenance therapy: immunomodulators (azathioprine, 6-mercaptopurine); biological therapies (anti-TNF agents: infliximab, adalimumab) for moderate-to-severe or steroid-dependent disease
- Nutritional assessment and supplementation — iron, folate, vitamin B12, vitamin D as indicated
- Skin involvement (erythema nodosum): typically resolves with treatment of the underlying bowel disease; systemic corticosteroids if severe
- Multidisciplinary care: gastroenterologist, dietitian, colorectal surgeon, dermatologist, ophthalmologist as required
- IBD nursing support and patient education on disease monitoring and medication adherence
- Regular surveillance colonoscopy for colorectal cancer risk after eight years of extensive colitis

**Coeliac Disease:**
- Strict lifelong gluten-free diet (GFD) — elimination of wheat, rye, barley, and oats (unless certified gluten-free oats are tolerated)
- Dietitian referral — essential for education on gluten avoidance, hidden sources of gluten, and nutritional adequacy of the GFD
- Correction of nutritional deficiencies: iron supplementation for iron deficiency anaemia; calcium and vitamin D for bone health; folate and vitamin B12 as indicated
- DEXA bone density scan at diagnosis — malabsorption may lead to osteopenia or osteoporosis even in young patients
- Repeat coeliac serology (tTG-IgA) at 6–12 months to assess dietary compliance and mucosal healing
- Annual monitoring: FBC, iron studies, LFTs, folate, B12, vitamin D, and coeliac serology
- Pneumococcal and influenza vaccination — coeliac disease is associated with functional hyposplenism
- Family screening — first-degree relatives have 10% risk of coeliac disease; recommend screening

**IBS and Functional Gastrointestinal Disorders:**
- Reassurance and explanation of the diagnosis using a biopsychosocial model
- Dietary modification: low-FODMAP diet under dietitian supervision; increased dietary fibre for constipation-predominant IBS; reduced soluble fibre and gas-producing foods for diarrhoea-predominant IBS
- Antispasmodics (hyoscine butylbromide, mebeverine) for cramping
- Psychological support: cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), mindfulness-based therapies, and stress management
- Avoid unnecessary investigations and polypharmacy
- Regular GP review with clear safety netting for alarm symptoms

**Lactose Intolerance:**
- Trial of lactose-free diet or lactase enzyme supplementation
- Ensure adequate calcium and vitamin D intake through alternative sources (fortified plant-based milks, calcium supplementation)

## Communication and Patient Education

**Explaining IBD to the patient:**
Use clear, accessible language appropriate to the patient's age. *"James, based on your history and the examination findings — particularly the rash on your legs — I believe you have a condition called inflammatory bowel disease. This is an autoimmune condition, which means your immune system, rather than protecting you from external threats, is mistakenly attacking the lining of your bowel. This creates inflammation in the bowel, which leads to the pain and changes in your stools that you have been experiencing. The rash on your legs is actually related to the same immune process — it is a well-recognised skin manifestation of this condition called erythema nodosum."*

**Explaining coeliac disease to the patient:**
*"Based on your history — particularly the fact that your pain is worse after eating bread and pasta, and the way you have described your stools — I believe you may have coeliac disease. Gluten is a protein found naturally in wheat, rye, and barley. In coeliac disease, your bowel is sensitive or allergic to gluten, and whenever you eat foods containing gluten, it triggers inflammation in the lining of your bowel. This inflammation prevents your bowel from absorbing nutrients properly, which explains the anaemia we know you have. The good news is that with a strict gluten-free diet, the inflammation settles and your bowel heals."*

**Addressing patient concerns:**
- Acknowledge and validate the patient's distress at having had unexplained symptoms for months
- Provide honest, age-appropriate information about the diagnosis and prognosis
- Emphasise that both IBD and coeliac disease are manageable conditions with effective treatments available
- Discuss the importance of specialist follow-up and regular monitoring
- Address questions about impact on diet, sport, school, and social activities proactively

**Written resources:**
- Crohn's and Colitis Australia (CCA) patient information resources
- Coeliac Australia patient resources and gluten-free diet guides
- GP Management Plan (GPMP) and Team Care Arrangement (TCA) for multidisciplinary management under Medicare

**Follow-up plan:**
- Arrange referral to paediatric or general gastroenterologist within 2–4 weeks
- Review blood test results within one week
- Provide clear written instructions and a point of contact for symptom deterioration

## AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

- **Do not omit red flag questions because the patient is young.** The examiner specifically assesses whether red flags are asked. Blood in the stool, unintentional weight loss, and change in bowel habit are asked not only to exclude malignancy but also to assess severity of IBD and other conditions. Skipping red flags in young patients is a common and penalised mistake.

- **Expand your GI differential in any young patient with chronic abdominal pain.** The examiner expects IBD, coeliac disease, lactose intolerance, IBS, and chronic gastrointestinal infection to appear in your working differential. A differential list limited to the acute abdomen framework (appendicitis, peptic ulcer, UTI) will not score well.

- **Ask about extra-intestinal manifestations of IBD systematically.** Joint pain, eye symptoms (redness, blurred vision), skin rashes, and oral ulcers are high-yield questions that distinguish a comprehensive IBD history from a superficial one. These questions often provide the diagnostic key when bowel symptoms are non-specific.

- **Erythema nodosum on physical examination is a pivotal finding that confirms IBD as the primary diagnosis.** When the examination card describes tender, red, firm nodules on the shins, recognise erythema nodosum immediately. Explain this finding explicitly in your diagnosis communication. Also exclude sarcoidosis by mentioning the need for a chest X-ray.

- **Ask about stool characteristics using lay terminology.** Stools that float, are difficult to flush, malodorous, or bulky are the patient-accessible descriptions of steatorrhoea. These features are diagnostic for coeliac disease and must be elicited with appropriately worded questions rather than clinical jargon.

- **Iron deficiency anaemia in a young patient without an obvious cause is a red flag for malabsorption — prioritise coeliac disease.** In the second case, the background of anaemia with iron supplementation is the pivotal historical clue. Candidates who miss this connection miss the diagnosis.

- **Use the full six minutes.** Unlike acute abdominal pain stations where site and onset often narrow the differential rapidly, chronic diffuse pain in young patients requires broad systematic questioning. Time pressure is not a constraint — the breadth of the history is the expected standard.

- **Conduct a brief HEADSS-style psychosocial screen in all adolescent patients.** Home situation, school or work stressors, mood, and social supports are relevant to both functional gastrointestinal disorders and medication adherence. Omitting psychosocial assessment in a 16-year-old represents an incomplete history.

- **Distinguish acute from chronic presentation explicitly in your diagnosis communication.** State clearly to the patient (and examiner) that the chronic duration of symptoms, the absence of fever and acute-onset features, and the associated findings collectively support a chronic GI diagnosis rather than an acute surgical emergency.

- **Do not suggest appendicitis as a differential in a patient with months of chronic pain.** Appendicitis is an acute condition. Including it as a differential for a months-long history of chronic pain demonstrates a failure to distinguish acute from chronic pathology and will attract examiner scrutiny.

- **When explaining coeliac disease, name specific foods containing gluten** — wheat, rye, barley — rather than simply saying "gluten-containing foods." The examiner assesses the quality of the patient education component; specific and actionable dietary guidance demonstrates clinical competence.

- **After erythema nodosum is identified, always mention sarcoidosis as a differential and state that a chest X-ray is required.** Candidates who correctly identify erythema nodosum but fail to exclude sarcoidosis demonstrate incomplete clinical reasoning. The two diagnoses are the most important causes of erythema nodosum in young adults in the AMC examination context.

## Safety Net

Provide the following safety net instructions explicitly to the patient and document them clearly in the clinical record:

**Return immediately to the emergency department or call an ambulance if:**
- Severe or rapidly worsening abdominal pain
- Signs of peritonism (rigid abdomen, inability to move without pain)
- Profuse or persistent rectal bleeding
- High fever with rigors
- Vomiting that prevents any oral intake
- Confusion or loss of consciousness
- Signs of shock (rapid heart rate, pallor, dizziness on standing)

**Return to see your GP urgently (within 24–48 hours) if:**
- New rectal bleeding or change in stool colour to dark black
- Significant unintentional weight loss noticed
- New onset fever
- Eye redness or pain, or sudden vision changes
- Marked deterioration in the frequency or severity of symptoms

**Routine follow-up:**
- Blood test results to be reviewed within one week of collection
- Specialist gastroenterology appointment within two to four weeks
- Return to GP for review of specialist referral outcome and further symptom monitoring
- If waiting for specialist appointment and symptoms significantly worsen, contact the GP promptly rather than waiting

**Written safety net information:** Provide written instructions summarising the above, along with the GP clinic's after-hours contact number and the address of the nearest emergency department. Ensure the patient and, where appropriate, their parent or guardian understand when and how to seek urgent review.

## AMC Exam Key Points

- Do not omit red flag questions because the patient is young. The examiner specifically assesses whether red flags are asked. Blood in the stool, unintentional weight loss, and change in bowel habit are asked not only to exclude malignancy but also to assess severity of IBD and other conditions. Skipping red flags in young patients is a common and penalised mistake.
- Expand your GI differential in any young patient with chronic abdominal pain. The examiner expects IBD, coeliac disease, lactose intolerance, IBS, and chronic gastrointestinal infection to appear in your working differential. A differential list limited to the acute abdomen framework (appendicitis, peptic ulcer, UTI) will not score well.
- Ask about extra-intestinal manifestations of IBD systematically. Joint pain, eye symptoms (redness, blurred vision), skin rashes, and oral ulcers are high-yield questions that distinguish a comprehensive IBD history from a superficial one. These questions often provide the diagnostic key when bowel symptoms are non-specific.
- Erythema nodosum on physical examination is a pivotal finding that confirms IBD as the primary diagnosis. When the examination card describes tender, red, firm nodules on the shins, recognise erythema nodosum immediately. Explain this finding explicitly in your diagnosis communication. Also exclude sarcoidosis by mentioning the need for a chest X-ray.
- Ask about stool characteristics using lay terminology. Stools that float, are difficult to flush, malodorous, or bulky are the patient-accessible descriptions of steatorrhoea. These features are diagnostic for coeliac disease and must be elicited with appropriately worded questions rather than clinical jargon.
- Iron deficiency anaemia in a young patient without an obvious cause is a red flag for malabsorption
- Use the full six minutes. Unlike acute abdominal pain stations where site and onset often narrow the differential rapidly, chronic diffuse pain in young patients requires broad systematic questioning. Time pressure is not a constraint
- Conduct a brief HEADSS-style psychosocial screen in all adolescent patients. Home situation, school or work stressors, mood, and social supports are relevant to both functional gastrointestinal disorders and medication adherence. Omitting psychosocial assessment in a 16-year-old represents an incomplete history.
- Distinguish acute from chronic presentation explicitly in your diagnosis communication. State clearly to the patient (and examiner) that the chronic duration of symptoms, the absence of fever and acute-onset features, and the associated findings collectively support a chronic GI diagnosis rather than an acute surgical emergency.
- Do not suggest appendicitis as a differential in a patient with months of chronic pain. Appendicitis is an acute condition. Including it as a differential for a months-long history of chronic pain demonstrates a failure to distinguish acute from chronic pathology and will attract examiner scrutiny.

## Management Summary

1. Inflammatory Bowel Disease:**
2. Refer to gastroenterologist for colonoscopy and biopsy to confirm diagnosis and distinguish Crohn's disease from ulcerative colitis
3. Induction of remission: corticosteroids (prednisolone) for moderate-to-severe flares; aminosalicylates (mesalazine) for mild-to-moderate ulcerative colitis
4. Maintenance therapy: immunomodulators (azathioprine, 6-mercaptopurine); biological therapies (anti-TNF agents: infliximab, adalimumab) for moderate-to-severe or steroid-dependent disease
5. Nutritional assessment and supplementation — iron, folate, vitamin B12, vitamin D as indicated
6. Skin involvement (erythema nodosum): typically resolves with treatment of the underlying bowel disease; systemic corticosteroids if severe
7. Multidisciplinary care: gastroenterologist, dietitian, colorectal surgeon, dermatologist, ophthalmologist as required
8. IBD nursing support and patient education on disease monitoring and medication adherence
