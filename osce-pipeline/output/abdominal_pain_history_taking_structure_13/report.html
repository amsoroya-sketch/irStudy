Abdominal Pain — History Taking Station

Clinical Presentation

Abdominal pain represents one of the highest-frequency presentations in the AMC Clinical Examination and demands a structured, systems-based approach that demonstrates clinical reasoning rather than pattern recognition from recalled cases. The canonical AMC station presents a patient — commonly a middle-aged adult — attending a general practice or emergency department with abdominal pain of varying acuity, duration, and localisation. The clinical information provided in the stem is deliberately limited, and the candidate must elicit a comprehensive, organised history to narrow a broad differential and demonstrate sound diagnostic thinking.

This station is clinically important because abdominal pain spans multiple organ systems contained within a single body cavity: the gastrointestinal tract, hepatobiliary system, urinary tract, reproductive organs, vascular structures, and — critically — referred pain from thoracic pathology. A candidate who approaches this presentation with GI-only thinking will systematically fail to identify serious diagnoses including ectopic pregnancy, acute coronary syndrome, mesenteric ischaemia, and lower lobe pneumonia. The AMC examiners explicitly assess breadth of differential generation alongside the quality of clinical reasoning that connects the history to those differentials.

The scoring of this station reflects its complexity: history taking commands the majority of available marks, with communication skills assessed throughout the consultation. A candidate who simply lists recalled diagnoses or asks disorganised questions without demonstrating a logical clinical framework will not achieve a pass score, regardless of whether they correctly identify the final diagnosis. The AMC explicitly warns against premature diagnostic closure — arriving at a diagnosis before sufficient clinical data has been gathered — and against a "scatter-gun" approach: asking a random sequence of loosely associated questions without evident clinical reasoning.

Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

The AMC Clinical Examination history taking station for abdominal pain assesses four integrated competencies. First, the candidate must identify key symptoms — both positive and negative findings — that are relevant to narrowing the differential. The examiner is looking for systematic completeness, not encyclopaedic listing. Second, the candidate must demonstrate a relevant, organised, step-by-step approach that makes their clinical reasoning visible to the examiner throughout the consultation. Third, the candidate must respond appropriately to patient cues, verbal and non-verbal, including expressed concern, emotional distress, and behavioural indicators of severity. Fourth, the candidate must apply context — modifying the standard framework based on age, sex, pain location, and acuity — without abandoning the structure.

The station is structured around four sequential phases. The introductory phase establishes hemodynamic stability, opens the consultation with an open-ended question, and addresses the patient's initial concern with acknowledgement and empathy. The pain characterisation phase applies a systematic mnemonic — SICORA is recommended — to fully analyse the pain before introducing associated symptoms. The differential ruling-out phase is the most critical and mark-intensive component: the candidate methodically works through each differential group, asking targeted questions to include or exclude each diagnosis. The closing phase, when time permits, addresses social, family, and occupational context relevant to the presentation.

A four-minute station requires the candidate to prioritise high-yield questions and accept that the closing phase may be incomplete. A six-minute station allows full coverage including the closing phase. The structure must be designed to function at both time intervals without requiring a separate framework for each.

Socrates Flow

Systematic Approach

Phase 1 — Introductory Phase

Hemodynamic Stability Assessment

Before initiating the consultation, the candidate should confirm the patient's stability. In the face-to-face AMC examination, this is achieved by addressing the examiner directly before turning to the patient: "Before I begin, I want to confirm my patient is hemodynamically stable. Could you please provide the vital signs — blood pressure, pulse rate, respiratory rate, and temperature?"

If the stem already includes vital signs, this step is complete. If not, eliciting all four parameters confirms the patient is stable enough to proceed with history taking rather than immediate resuscitation. Abdominal pain presentations carry a significant burden of life-threatening diagnoses — ruptured ectopic pregnancy, aortic aneurysm rupture, mesenteric ischaemia, bowel perforation — in which hemodynamic compromise would fundamentally alter the initial management approach. Performing this check demonstrates structured clinical prioritisation, not box-ticking.

Open-Ended Opening

Following introduction, the consultation begins with a genuinely open-ended question that invites the patient to tell their story without restriction:

  • "Jill, how can I help you today?"
  • "Can you tell me more about what's been happening with your pain?"
  • "Before I ask you anything specific, could you tell me your story — in your own words?"

The purpose of the open-ended opening is to activate the patient's narrative and capture the opening statement. AMC role-players are provided with a scripted opening statement containing three to four lines of clinical information. This information is only released in response to an open-ended prompt. A candidate who begins with a closed question ("Where is your pain?") will receive monosyllabic responses and fail to extract this early diagnostic information.

Addressing the Patient's Initial Concern

Every patient presenting to a clinical encounter carries a concern. In the AMC examination context, this concern is either explicitly stated by the patient during their opening statement or observable through non-verbal cues — guarding the abdomen, distress, or anxious affect. The AMC examination framework explicitly assesses whether the candidate responds to this concern appropriately.

A complete response to the patient's concern contains three elements:

  1. Acknowledgement: "I understand this has been very worrying for you."
  2. Empathy: "I'm sorry to hear you've been experiencing this — it sounds like it's been quite difficult."
  3. Reassurance and redirection: "I'm here to help you. I need to ask you some questions so I can understand what's going on and make the best plan for you. Is that alright?"

This response should be delivered in under thirty seconds. It is not an extended counselling exchange. The AMC examination marks this competency once — at the moment the patient expresses a concern. Subsequent empathic statements are not marked separately and should not consume consultation time at the expense of clinical data gathering.

Phase 2 — Pain Characterisation (SICORA)

The second phase is dedicated exclusively to characterising the pain. The mnemonic SICORA provides a systematic, sequenced framework for pain assessment that works across all pain presentations in the AMC examination — abdominal pain, chest pain, headache, joint pain — without requiring separate structures for each.

S — Site

"Can you show me exactly where you feel the pain?"

The site of pain provides the first differential-narrowing information. Epigastric pain directs attention toward peptic ulcer disease, pancreatitis, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, and acute coronary syndrome. Right upper quadrant pain suggests hepatobiliary pathology — cholecystitis, cholelithiasis, hepatitis, cholangitis. Right lower quadrant pain raises appendicitis as the priority, with ovarian pathology in females and inguinal hernia in both sexes. Left lower quadrant pain suggests diverticular disease, sigmoid pathology, and ovarian cysts. Diffuse pain is associated with generalised peritonitis, mesenteric ischaemia, irritable bowel syndrome, and large bowel obstruction.

In cases of right lower quadrant pain, one additional question is mandatory:

"Where did the pain start? Was it always in that spot, or did it begin somewhere else and then move?"

Migration of pain from the periumbilical region to the right iliac fossa — McBurney's point — is the classical history of appendicitis and must not be missed. In ectopic pregnancy, the pain typically originates at the site of the fallopian tube and does not migrate. This single question can distinguish two high-stakes diagnoses with divergent management.

I — Intensity

"On a scale of one to ten, where ten is the worst pain you've ever experienced and one is barely noticeable — how would you rate your pain right now?"

A numeric scale converts a subjective experience into a communicable datum. A rating of eight to ten demands urgent consideration of serious pathology. A rating of three or four in the context of chronic symptoms directs thinking toward functional disorders or mild inflammatory conditions. Intensity also guides analgesic prescribing in subsequent management.

C — Character (Quality)

"Can you describe what the pain feels like — is it a sharp, stabbing pain, or more of a dull, heavy ache?"

Character is a key differentiating feature:

  • Sharp, colicky, waxing and waning pain — ureteric colic, biliary colic, bowel obstruction
  • Constant, severe, worsening pain — appendicitis, pancreatitis, peritonitis, ischaemia
  • Dull, cramping pain — irritable bowel syndrome, dysmenorrhoea, early appendicitis
  • Burning, gnawing epigastric pain — peptic ulcer disease, gastritis
  • Tearing, ripping pain (though rare in history taking stations) — aortic dissection

If the patient does not understand the question, prompt with examples: "For instance, some people describe it as sharp — like a knife — while others describe it as a heavy or squeezing sensation. Which is closer to what you feel?"

O — Onset and Time Course

Three distinct questions cover onset comprehensively:

  1. "When did the pain first start? How long have you had it?"
  2. "Has the pain been constant and continuous since it started, or does it come and go?"
  3. "Since it began, has the pain been getting progressively worse?"

The onset pattern is clinically critical. Sudden onset — pain reaching maximum intensity within seconds to minutes — suggests perforation, rupture, or vascular catastrophe. Gradual onset over hours suggests evolving inflammatory processes such as appendicitis or cholecystitis. Intermittent, colicky pain over days suggests biliary or ureteric colic. Chronic, fluctuating pain over weeks to months directs thinking toward irritable bowel syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, or malignancy.

R — Radiation

"Does the pain travel or spread anywhere — to your back, your shoulder, your groin?"

Radiation patterns are clinically diagnostic:

  • Right shoulder tip pain — diaphragmatic irritation from haemoperitoneum or gallbladder inflammation
  • Back radiation — pancreatitis (boring through to the mid-back), aortic aneurysm
  • Groin radiation — ureteric colic (right or left flank to ipsilateral groin)
  • Jaw or left arm radiation — atypical presentation of acute coronary syndrome with epigastric pain
  • Interscapular radiation — aortic dissection

A — Alleviating and Aggravating Factors

Begin with a general open question:

"Is there anything that makes the pain better or worse?"

Then ask specifically about two factors critical to abdominal pain:

  1. Relationship to food and eating: "Does the pain get worse after you eat? Or does eating actually make it better?"
  2. Peptic ulcer disease: epigastric pain relieved by food (duodenal ulcer) or worsened by food (gastric ulcer)
  3. Pancreatitis: worsened after eating, particularly fatty meals
  4. Cholecystitis: triggered by fatty meals

  5. Mesenteric ischaemia (chronic): post-prandial pain — "intestinal angina"

  6. Positional effect: "Is there any position that makes the pain better or worse?"

  7. Pancreatitis: pain relieved by leaning forward
  8. Peritonism: pain worsened by movement and relieved by lying still
  9. Renal colic: patient is restless, unable to find a comfortable position

Phase 3 — Systematic Differential Ruling-Out

This is the most mark-intensive phase of the history taking station. The candidate must work through each differential group in a logical sequence, asking targeted questions to include or exclude diagnoses. A head-to-toe list of associated symptoms without evident clinical reasoning does not demonstrate the organised approach the AMC examination requires.

The recommended sequence groups differentials by organ system and is modified by the site of pain once established.

Group 1 — Gastrointestinal Differentials (All Cases)

General GI screening questions:

These questions address symptoms that are common across multiple GI differentials and should be asked in all abdominal pain cases, regardless of site:

  • "Have you had any nausea or vomiting?" — Present in appendicitis, gastroenteritis, bowel obstruction, pancreatitis; probe for volume, frequency, and content if positive
  • "Have you been passing wind (gas)? Are your bowels moving?" — Obstipation (failure to pass flatus or stool) is a cardinal sign of bowel obstruction and must not be omitted
  • "Have you had any diarrhoea?" — Acute gastroenteritis, Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, infective colitis
  • "Have you had any constipation?" — Large bowel obstruction, diverticular disease, colorectal malignancy
  • "Have you had any fever or chills?" — Infective or inflammatory aetiology: appendicitis, cholangitis, pyelonephritis, pelvic inflammatory disease

Red flag — cancer screening questions (general):

These questions are mandatory in all abdominal pain presentations, regardless of whether the pain appears acute. The clinical purpose extends beyond colorectal cancer — anorexia and weight loss are also features of appendicitis, Crohn's disease, and pancreatitis, and thus carry diagnostic value beyond oncological screening.

  • "Have you noticed any unintentional weight loss? How much over what period of time?" — Probe further if positive
  • "Has your appetite changed? Have you been eating less than usual?" — Anorexia of appendicitis is a classic teaching point
  • "Have you noticed any new lumps or swellings anywhere on your body?" — Lymphadenopathy, abdominal mass
  • "Have you been feeling unusually tired or lightheaded?" — Anaemia from chronic GI blood loss; lightheadedness in the context of acute abdominal pain raises concern for haemoperitoneum or significant haemorrhage

Red flag — cancer screening questions (specific to GI):

These questions are of the highest clinical priority in abdominal pain. Do not omit them:

  • "Have you noticed any blood in your stools? Is it bright red, or mixed through the stool?" — Fresh rectal blood: anorectal pathology, colitis, diverticular disease, mesenteric ischaemia. Blood mixed through stool: colonic malignancy, IBD
  • "Have you noticed your stools are dark, black, or tar-like?" — Melaena indicates upper GI bleeding (proximal to the ligament of Treitz): peptic ulcer disease, gastric malignancy, oesophageal varices
  • "Have you noticed any change in your bowel habits — for instance, going more or less frequently than usual, or a change in the consistency of your stools?" — Altered bowel habit is a cardinal red flag for colorectal malignancy
  • "Is there any family history of bowel cancer, or any other bowel conditions?" — First-degree relative with colorectal cancer significantly elevates risk

GI-specific additional questions:

  • "Have you had any previous abdominal surgeries?" — Post-surgical adhesions are a common cause of small bowel obstruction
  • "Are you taking any regular medications, including anti-inflammatories, aspirin, or steroids?" — NSAIDs are a leading cause of peptic ulcer disease and upper GI bleeding; SGLT2 inhibitors are associated with euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis and pancreatitis; opioids cause constipation and functional obstruction

Group 2 — Renal/Urinary Tract (KUB) Differentials (All Cases)

The kidneys lie in the retroperitoneum above the level of the umbilicus; the ureters traverse the entire length of the abdominal cavity; the bladder occupies the pelvis. KUB pathology can, therefore, produce pain in the upper, lateral, or lower abdomen and must be screened in all cases regardless of pain location.

  • "Do you have any pain or burning sensation when you pass urine?" — Dysuria: urinary tract infection (lower — cystitis; upper — pyelonephritis), ureteric colic
  • "Are you passing urine more frequently than usual?" — Frequency: cystitis, urethritis, bladder irritation from adjacent pelvic pathology
  • "Have you noticed any blood in your urine — either visible or detected on testing?" — Haematuria: ureteric calculus, urinary tract infection, bladder or renal malignancy

Group 3 — Lower Abdominal Pain Specific (Apply When Pain is Left Iliac Fossa, Right Iliac Fossa, or Suprapubic)

Inguinal Hernia:

  • "Have you noticed any swelling or a lump in your groin — either side?" — A positive response requires exploration of reducibility, duration, and whether the swelling is tender. Note: even if lumps and bumps were asked generally earlier in the consultation, a specific question about the groin is required because role-players are instructed to provide this information only when the anatomical site is specified.

Gynaecological and Obstetric Differentials (Female Patients):

Symptoms:

  • "Have you had any vaginal bleeding — outside of your normal period?" — Essential screening for ectopic pregnancy, threatened miscarriage, pelvic inflammatory disease
  • "Have you had any vaginal discharge? If so, can you describe it — colour, amount, odour?" — Purulent discharge: pelvic inflammatory disease

Menstrual history:

  • "When was your last menstrual period? Can you give me a date?" — This question is the single most important question in lower abdominal pain in a female patient of reproductive age. Missing this question in the context of a possible ectopic pregnancy represents a critical clinical failure. Even if the candidate has a male standardised patient, the habit of considering this question should be ingrained.
  • "Are your periods regular? How often do they come?"
  • "Have you had heavier periods or any spotting between periods?"

Sexual and reproductive history (screening only):

  • "Are you sexually active?"
  • "Do you use contraception or practise safe sex?"
  • "Have you ever been diagnosed with a sexually transmitted infection?" — Chlamydial or gonorrhoeal infection is the primary risk factor for pelvic inflammatory disease

This is a screening history, not a full sexual health history. A full sexual history (number of partners, gender of partners, specific sexual practices, swab sites) is reserved for a dedicated sexual health station.

Scrotal and Testicular Pathology (Male Patients):

  • "Do you have any pain or swelling in your scrotum or testicles?" — Testicular torsion and epididymo-orchitis can present with lower abdominal and groin pain as referred pain. Testicular torsion in a young male with sudden onset lower abdominal pain is a surgical emergency.

Group 4 — Upper Abdominal Pain Specific (Apply When Pain is Epigastric, Right Upper Quadrant, or Left Upper Quadrant)

Hepatobiliary Pathology (hepatitis, cholecystitis, cholelithiasis, cholangitis, hepatocellular carcinoma):

  • "Have you noticed any yellowing of your skin or the whites of your eyes?" — Jaundice: biliary obstruction (choledocholithiasis, cholangitis, malignancy), hepatocellular disease
  • "Have you noticed your urine has become very dark, or your stools have become pale or clay-coloured?" — Dark urine and pale stools indicate obstructive jaundice
  • "Have you travelled internationally recently, or had any contact with someone who has been unwell with a liver infection?" — Hepatitis A (travel, contaminated food/water), Hepatitis B and C (blood-borne)

Peptic Ulcer Disease and Gastro-oesophageal Reflux Disease:

  • "Is the pain related to eating? Does it get worse shortly after a meal, or does it come on when your stomach is empty?"
  • "Do you drink coffee or alcohol? Do you eat spicy or fatty foods regularly?" — Risk factors for GORD and peptic ulcer disease; alcohol intake also serves as a risk factor for pancreatitis

Cardiac and Pulmonary Referred Pain:

These diagnoses are screened with two to three targeted questions and should not be omitted in epigastric or upper abdominal pain presentations:

  • "Do you have any chest pain or tightness?" — Acute coronary syndrome with epigastric or chest pain; note that inferior MI characteristically presents with epigastric pain, nausea, and diaphoresis
  • "Are you short of breath?" — Pulmonary embolism, lower lobe pneumonia, pleuritis
  • "Have you had a cough recently, particularly with any fever?" — Lower lobe pneumonia causing diaphragmatic irritation and referred abdominal pain

Group 5 — Other Important Differentials (All Cases, Asked Toward End of Consultation)

These differentials are lower probability but carry significant clinical consequence if missed. They are placed toward the end of the structure to preserve time for higher-yield questions earlier:

  • Mesenteric ischaemia: "Have you noticed any irregular or racing heartbeat — where your heart beats fast or flutters?" — Atrial fibrillation is the most common source of a mesenteric artery embolus in older patients. Mesenteric ischaemia should be considered in any elderly patient with severe abdominal pain seemingly out of proportion to clinical findings.

  • Herpes zoster (shingles): "Have you noticed any rash, blisters, or skin changes on your abdomen or side?" — Prodromal zoster presents with severe dermatomal pain before the vesicular rash appears. The diagnosis may not be clinically apparent on first presentation.

  • Abdominal trauma: "Have you had any injury or trauma to your abdomen recently?" — Splenic and hepatic injury may present with delayed haemoperitoneum. If traumatic aetiology were the primary concern, the stem would typically indicate this.

  • Diabetic ketoacidosis: "Have you noticed increased thirst or passing much more urine than usual?" — Abdominal pain is a recognised feature of DKA and may be the presenting complaint in undiagnosed Type 1 diabetes mellitus, particularly in young patients.

Phase 4 — Closing Phase (Time-Permitting)

The closing phase addresses the broader clinical and social context. In a four-minute station, this phase may need to be abbreviated or omitted entirely without loss of marks, provided the preceding phases were completed competently. In a six-minute station, the closing phase should be attempted.

  • Alcohol: "How much alcohol do you drink on a typical week?" — Risk factor for peptic ulcer disease, pancreatitis, hepatitis, cirrhosis, and oesophageal pathology. If not asked earlier, this is a critical question to include.
  • Medications and allergies: "Are you taking any regular medications, prescribed or over-the-counter? Any allergies to medications?" — If not already asked within the GI section.
  • Past medical history: "Do you have any significant medical conditions you've been diagnosed with?"
  • Family history: "Is there any family history of bowel cancer, stomach cancer, or other bowel conditions?" — If not asked within the GI red flag section.
  • Social and occupational context: "What do you do for work? Does this pain affect your daily activities or your work?"
  • Smoking: "Do you smoke? How much, and for how long?"

Redflags Panel
Clinically Enriched

Red Flags — Must Not Miss

  • Haemodynamic instability (tachycardia, hypotension, pallor, diaphoresis) — Indicates significant haemorrhage or septic shock. Immediate resuscitation and surgical consultation required. Do not proceed with history alone; initiate primary survey. Differentials include ruptured ectopic pregnancy, ruptured aortic aneurysm, and bowel perforation with peritonitis.

  • Missed menstrual period with lower abdominal pain in a female of reproductive age — Ectopic pregnancy until proven otherwise. A serum beta-hCG and pelvic ultrasound are mandatory. Delay in diagnosis is a leading cause of preventable maternal death in Australia.

  • Absolute constipation — failure to pass flatus or stool — Cardinal sign of complete bowel obstruction. Requires urgent imaging (erect chest X-ray and abdominal X-ray) and surgical assessment. Do not confuse with simple constipation where flatus is still passed.

  • Melaena or haematochezia with haemodynamic compromise — Upper or lower GI haemorrhage requiring urgent resuscitation, cessation of anticoagulants, and emergency endoscopic assessment. Notify surgical and gastroenterological teams immediately.

  • Severe epigastric pain radiating to the jaw or left arm, with diaphoresis and nausea — Inferior myocardial infarction presenting as acute abdomen. An ECG is mandatory in all patients with upper abdominal pain and cardiovascular risk factors. Missing this diagnosis carries catastrophic consequences.

  • Severe abdominal pain in an elderly patient with atrial fibrillation, out of proportion to clinical findings — Acute mesenteric ischaemia is a surgical emergency with high mortality. The diagnosis is frequently delayed because early physical examination may appear unremarkable despite severe ischaemia. Urgent CT angiography and vascular surgery consultation are required.

  • Unintentional weight loss with altered bowel habit — Colorectal malignancy must be excluded. Urgent colonoscopy referral is indicated. In patients over 50 years, this combination mandates a two-week-wait pathway equivalent under Australian guidelines.

  • Jaundice with fever and rigors (Charcot's triad) — Acute cholangitis is a life-threatening biliary infection. Charcot's triad (jaundice, fever, right upper quadrant pain) and Reynolds' pentad (adding hypotension and altered consciousness) indicate sepsis of biliary origin requiring urgent IV antibiotics, fluid resuscitation, and emergency biliary decompression (ERCP or percutaneous drainage).

  • Vesicular dermatomal rash on the trunk preceding or accompanying severe unilateral abdominal pain — Herpes zoster. The prodromal pain of zoster may precede the rash by 48–72 hours and is frequently misdiagnosed as acute abdominal pathology. Early antiviral therapy (valaciclovir) within 72 hours of rash onset reduces the risk of post-herpetic neuralgia.

  • Severe abdominal pain in a known or suspected diabetic with vomiting and altered consciousness — Diabetic ketoacidosis. Abdominal pain is a recognised manifestation of DKA and may be the first presentation of Type 1 diabetes mellitus. Immediate blood glucose, blood gas, and serum ketones are mandatory.

Investigations

Bedside:
- Vital signs (blood pressure, pulse rate, respiratory rate, temperature, oxygen saturation)
- Capillary blood glucose (exclude DKA, hypoglycaemia)
- Urine dipstick (haematuria, glycosuria, ketonuria, nitrites, leucocytes)
- Urine beta-hCG (all females of reproductive age with lower abdominal pain — mandatory)
- ECG (all patients with epigastric or upper abdominal pain, cardiac risk factors, or haemodynamic instability)

Bloods:
- Full blood count (anaemia, leucocytosis)
- Electrolytes, urea, creatinine (renal function, electrolyte derangement in vomiting)
- Liver function tests (hepatobiliary pathology)
- Amylase and lipase (pancreatitis — lipase more sensitive and specific)
- C-reactive protein, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (inflammatory markers)
- Blood cultures (if systemic sepsis is suspected)
- Coagulation screen (if hepatic pathology or haemorrhage suspected)
- Serum beta-hCG (quantitative, if urine beta-hCG positive)
- Lactate (if mesenteric ischaemia or septic shock suspected — elevated lactate indicates end-organ hypoperfusion)
- Group and screen / crossmatch (if haemorrhage or emergency surgery anticipated)

Imaging:
- Erect chest X-ray (free gas under diaphragm — bowel perforation; lower lobe consolidation — pneumonia)
- Abdominal X-ray (bowel obstruction — air-fluid levels, dilated loops; calcified renal calculi; faecal loading)
- Ultrasound abdomen and pelvis (first-line for hepatobiliary pathology, appendicitis in select populations, ectopic pregnancy, ovarian pathology, renal calculi, aortic aneurysm)
- CT abdomen and pelvis with contrast (gold standard for acute abdomen; required for bowel obstruction, perforation, mesenteric ischaemia, aortic pathology, and equivocal cases)
- CT pulmonary angiography (if pulmonary embolism is suspected)
- CT angiography (mesenteric ischaemia, aortic dissection or aneurysm)

Specialist:
- Surgical consultation (appendicitis, bowel obstruction, perforation, ectopic pregnancy, aortic emergency)
- Gastroenterology referral (IBD, GI haemorrhage, deferred colonoscopy for malignancy workup)
- Gynaecology consultation (ectopic pregnancy, PID, ovarian torsion)
- Cardiology consultation (acute coronary syndrome)
- Vascular surgery (mesenteric ischaemia, aortic aneurysm)

Management

Immediate stabilisation (if patient is unwell):
- Secure IV access (two large-bore cannulae in haemodynamically compromised patient)
- IV fluid resuscitation (Hartmann's solution or normal saline)
- Supplemental oxygen if oxygen saturation is below 94%
- Analgesia — IV opioid analgesia (morphine or fentanyl) titrated to pain; the provision of adequate analgesia does not mask peritonism and should not be withheld pending diagnosis
- Nil by mouth if surgical pathology is suspected
- Urinary catheter and fluid balance monitoring in the severely unwell patient
- Surgical registrar or specialist notification if life-threatening diagnosis is confirmed

Diagnosis-specific management:

  • Appendicitis: Appendicectomy (laparoscopic approach preferred); perioperative IV antibiotics (cephalosporin and metronidazole)
  • Ectopic pregnancy: Emergency salpingostomy or salpingectomy (haemodynamically unstable); medical management with methotrexate (haemodynamically stable, unruptured, beta-hCG below 5000 IU/L, no embryonic cardiac activity)
  • Acute cholecystitis: IV antibiotics (cephalosporin); elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy; early surgery within 72 hours of symptom onset is preferred per Australian surgical guidelines
  • Acute pancreatitis: IV fluids (aggressive early resuscitation); analgesia; nil by mouth initially; nutritional support; investigation and management of underlying cause (gallstones, alcohol cessation)
  • Peptic ulcer disease: Proton pump inhibitor (high-dose IV or oral); Helicobacter pylori eradication therapy if positive (clarithromycin-based triple therapy or quadruple therapy per Australian guidelines); cessation of NSAIDs
  • Acute coronary syndrome: Aspirin, anticoagulation, nitrates, oxygen, cardiology referral; primary percutaneous coronary intervention if ST-elevation MI
  • Bowel obstruction: IV fluids, nasogastric decompression, surgical consultation; operative intervention for complete obstruction or strangulation
  • Urinary tract infection / pyelonephritis: Oral nitrofurantoin or trimethoprim for uncomplicated lower UTI; IV cephalosporin for pyelonephritis or systemically unwell patient; analgesia; adequate hydration; urine culture to guide antibiotic rationalisation

Communication and Patient Education

Explaining the diagnosis:
The explanation must be delivered in clear, non-technical language. Confirm the patient's current understanding before providing new information. Use the teach-back method: "I want to make sure I've explained this clearly — can you tell me in your own words what we're going to do next?"

Addressing patient concerns:
Patients with acute abdominal pain are frequently frightened that they have cancer, appendicitis, or a serious surgical condition. Acknowledge the concern directly and explain the diagnostic process: "I understand you're worried about what might be causing this. The tests I've arranged will help us find out what's going on, and we'll work through this together."

Key questions to anticipate from patients:
- "Do I need surgery?" — Explain that this depends on the investigation results and will be determined with the surgical team.
- "How long will I need to stay in hospital?" — Provide an honest, realistic timeframe based on the likely diagnosis.
- "Is this serious?" — Do not minimise or falsely reassure. Acknowledge the uncertainty: "We need to get the results of your tests before I can give you a clear answer. What I can tell you is that we're taking this seriously and working to find out what's happening."
- "Could this be cancer?" — If red flags are present, acknowledge the possibility without creating disproportionate alarm. Explain the investigation pathway and timeline.

AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

  • Never arrive at a diagnosis before gathering sufficient data. Premature diagnostic closure is explicitly cited in AMC examiner guidelines as a reason for failing a candidate. Even if the clinical picture strongly suggests appendicitis from the stem, complete the full structured history before forming a conclusion — the examiner is watching for the process, not the answer.

  • Ask about passage of flatus in every abdominal pain case. Obstipation — the inability to pass flatus or stool — is the hallmark of complete bowel obstruction and must not be missed. This single question can identify a surgical emergency.

  • Never omit the last menstrual period in a female of reproductive age with lower abdominal pain. Ectopic pregnancy is a must-not-miss diagnosis. This question should be so habitual that it is asked regardless of whether the patient has mentioned gynaecological symptoms. The role-player will not volunteer this information unless specifically asked.

  • Ask about migration of pain to the right iliac fossa in all right lower quadrant cases. The classical history of appendicitis — central, periumbilical pain migrating to the right iliac fossa over 12–24 hours — is a high-yield teaching point. This question distinguishes appendicitis from ectopic pregnancy, right-sided ureteric colic, and ovarian pathology.

  • Request an ECG in all patients with epigastric or upper abdominal pain. Inferior myocardial infarction classically presents with epigastric pain, nausea, and diaphoresis. Failure to consider and exclude acute coronary syndrome in a patient with upper abdominal pain and cardiovascular risk factors represents a critical diagnostic error.

  • Probe all positive findings immediately. When the patient reports weight loss, do not move on — determine how much weight over what period of time. When the patient reports vaginal bleeding, characterise it fully. Brushing past positive findings without probing is a hallmark of the candidate who has memorised a case rather than learned clinical reasoning.

  • Do not use double-barrelled questions. Asking "Do you have any nausea, vomiting, or constipation?" prevents the patient from accurately responding to individual symptoms and risks gathering inaccurate clinical data. Ask one question at a time. One or two minor double-barrelled questions (such as nausea and vomiting together, which are closely related) are acceptable, but habitual doubling or tripling of questions signals poor consultation technique.

  • Use lay language throughout. Patients do not understand "obstipation", "haematochezia", "melaena", or "dysuria". Ask: "Are you passing any wind?", "Have you seen any blood in your stools?", "Is there pain or burning when you pass urine?" The role-player may not respond appropriately to medical terminology.

  • Screen for mesenteric ischaemia in every elderly patient with severe abdominal pain. Ask specifically about palpitations and irregular heartbeat. A diagnosis of atrial fibrillation significantly elevates the risk of a mesenteric arterial embolus. The pain of mesenteric ischaemia is often described as out of proportion to the physical findings, which is the key clinical clue.

  • Structure your differential groups aloud when presenting to the examiner. When asked for your differentials, present them by system: "In terms of gastrointestinal causes, I'm considering... In terms of urological causes... In terms of gynaecological causes..." This demonstrates organised thinking and avoids the common error of listing only GI diagnoses.

  • Manage time by prioritising — not by skipping. In a four-minute station, the introductory phase, pain characterisation, GI red flags, and KUB screening must be completed. The closing phase (social, family, and occupational history) is the lowest priority and can be abbreviated without significant mark loss.

  • Complete your opening address to the patient's concern before asking clinical questions. Moving directly from the open-ended opening to "Where is the pain?" without acknowledging the patient's expressed distress will cost marks in the communication domain and misses a clear instruction from AMC examiner guidelines.

Safety Net

Immediate return to the emergency department is required if any of the following develop:

  • Sudden, severe worsening of abdominal pain — particularly if it becomes constant, generalised, or associated with a rigid, board-like abdomen
  • Collapse, fainting, or feeling too unwell to stand — indicates possible haemodynamic compromise
  • Vomiting blood or passing black, tarry stools (melaena) — upper gastrointestinal haemorrhage
  • Bright red rectal bleeding in large volume — lower GI haemorrhage
  • Fever above 38.5°C with worsening abdominal pain — evolving sepsis or peritonitis
  • Complete inability to pass wind or open bowels — new bowel obstruction
  • Vaginal bleeding in pregnancy, or suspicion of pregnancy — ectopic pregnancy until proven otherwise
  • Chest pain, shortness of breath, or palpitations accompanying the abdominal pain — possible cardiac or pulmonary emergency

Planned follow-up:
- Patients discharged with a working diagnosis of mild gastroenteritis or irritable bowel syndrome: review in 48–72 hours if symptoms do not improve, or earlier if any of the above features develop
- Patients with potential sinister pathology (red flags present, investigations pending): urgent follow-up within 24–48 hours or direct admission pending results
- All patients should be provided with a clear contact pathway — the after-hours clinic telephone number and, where appropriate, the direct contact for the treating specialist team

Key Facts & Clinical Priorities

Red Flags — Must Not Miss

  • Haemodynamic instability (tachycardia, hypotension, pallor, diaphoresis)
  • Missed menstrual period with lower abdominal pain in a female of reproductive age
  • Absolute constipation
  • Melaena or haematochezia with haemodynamic compromise
  • Severe epigastric pain radiating to the jaw or left arm, with diaphoresis and nausea
  • Severe abdominal pain in an elderly patient with atrial fibrillation, out of proportion to clinical findings
  • Unintentional weight loss with altered bowel habit
  • Jaundice with fever and rigors (Charcot's triad)

Differential Diagnoses

  • Acute gastroenteritis
  • Peptic ulcer disease
  • Renal or ureteric colic
  • Appendicitis
  • Biliary colic / Cholecystitis
  • Diverticular disease
  • Inflammatory bowel disease
  • Pancreatitis
