# Acute Appendicitis — History Taking Station

## Clinical Presentation

A 27-year-old woman presents to the emergency department with abdominal pain. The stem provides minimal information — no specific site, no duration, no associated symptoms — which is entirely deliberate. The AMC Clinical Examination frequently presents candidates with a lean stem to test whether they possess an organised, systematic framework rather than a template-driven response. The clinical scenario unfolds through skilled, sequential questioning, and the diagnosis of acute appendicitis emerges only as the candidate methodically explores the character of the pain, associated symptoms, and relevant system-specific differentials.

Acute appendicitis is the most common surgical emergency in Australia, with a lifetime risk of approximately 8% in males and 6.7% in females. The classic presentation — periumbilical pain migrating to the right iliac fossa (RIF) over 12–24 hours, accompanied by anorexia, nausea, and localised peritoneal signs — is well-recognised. However, in women of reproductive age, the differential diagnosis is considerably broader, encompassing gynaecological, urological, and gastrointestinal pathology. This complexity makes the 27-year-old female with right lower quadrant (RLQ) pain one of the highest-yield history-taking scenarios in the AMC Clinical Examination.

The station demands more than factual knowledge. It requires the candidate to demonstrate structured clinical reasoning: eliciting the pathognomonic shift of pain, identifying anorexia as a cardinal symptom, systematically excluding gynaecological and urological differentials, and communicating findings with diagnostic confidence while maintaining empathic rapport.

---

## Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

This is an eight-minute history-taking station with a structured prompt at four minutes. The prompt bell signals the candidate to move from history-taking into requesting physical examination findings from the examiner, followed by explanation of diagnosis and differentials to the patient. Candidates who have not yet covered essential history elements by the prompt must do so efficiently within the remaining time.

The examiner assesses four domains sequentially:

**1. Opening and Initial Assessment**
Candidates are expected to confirm patient identity, sanitise hands, introduce themselves, and immediately request vital signs to establish haemodynamic stability before proceeding. A single open-ended question then invites the patient's opening statement.

**2. Exploration of the Presenting Complaint**
Using SOCRATES methodology, the candidate systematically characterises the pain. The opening statement from the patient will contain key clinical information — site, onset, and expressed concern — all of which require acknowledgement before closed questioning begins.

**3. Systematic Differential Screening**
Following pain characterisation, the candidate screens three primary system groups in order: gastrointestinal (GI), kidney-ureter-bladder (KUB), and pelvic/gynaecological. Red flag questioning is integrated within the GI screen. Each group must be addressed within the time constraint.

**4. Closure — ICE, Safety Net, and Communication**
The candidate summarises findings, acknowledges the patient's concerns, provides a clear explanation of the most likely diagnosis with clinical reasoning, presents a structured differential, and arranges appropriate investigations and management.

## Systematic Approach

### Opening, Vital Signs, and Responding to the Patient's Opening Statement

**Initial stabilisation check:**
Before any detailed history is taken, the candidate must confirm haemodynamic stability by requesting observations. In a 27-year-old with acute abdominal pain, the immediate concern is physiological decompensation — septic shock from a perforated viscus, or haemodynamic instability from ruptured ectopic pregnancy. Normal observations (BP 110/70 mmHg, PR 88, RR 12, Temp 36.5°C) are reassuring but do not eliminate serious pathology.

**The open-ended question:**
Begin with: *"How can I help you today?"* or *"Can you tell me what's been troubling you?"*

The patient's opening statement delivers layered clinical information simultaneously — site, onset, trajectory, and emotional state. For this patient: *"I've been having this pain in the right lower side of my stomach. It started yesterday, it's getting worse, and I'm getting worried."*

**Responding to the opening statement — the three-step response:**
Do not immediately proceed to closed questioning. The examiner observes whether the candidate demonstrates clinical empathy before systematic inquiry:

1. **Empathy and acknowledgement:** *"I'm sorry to hear you're in pain, Jenna. What you're describing is concerning to me as well."*
2. **Reassurance and process explanation:** *"I need to ask you a few questions to better understand your pain and take good care of you. Is that all right?"*
3. **Pain assessment offer:** *"Are you in significant pain right now? Would you like me to arrange something for the pain?"* If the patient accepts, respond: *"I'll arrange that for you — let's continue with our questions while we get that organised."* Do not invest further time in allergy history or analgesic selection at this stage; these are appropriately deferred until after history, examination, and diagnosis.

### SOCRATES Assessment of Abdominal Pain

SOCRATES is the structural backbone of pain assessment in this station. Each domain must be explicitly addressed; omission of any element, particularly the migration of pain, constitutes a significant clinical omission.

**Site:**
Even when the patient has volunteered the location, confirm it directly: *"You mentioned pain in the lower right side — can you point to exactly where you feel it most?"* Reconfirmation catches imprecise patient reporting and demonstrates methodical precision to the examiner.

**Onset:**
*"When did this pain start?"* — Pain commenced yesterday evening.
*"Was the onset sudden or did it come on gradually?"* — Gradual onset.

**The critical question — migration of pain:**
*"You've told me the pain is now in your lower right side. Can you tell me where it started? Was it always in that position, or did it begin somewhere else?"*

The patient discloses: *"It actually started around my belly button yesterday, and today it's moved to the lower right side."*

This single piece of information — periumbilical pain migrating to the right iliac fossa — is the pathognomonic feature of acute appendicitis. It reflects visceral pain from appendiceal distension (transmitted via T10 visceral afferents to the periumbilical region) transitioning to somatic pain as parietal peritoneal inflammation localises to the RIF. Failure to elicit this migration is a critical omission.

**Character:**
*"How would you describe the pain — is it sharp, stabbing, cramping, or more of a dull ache?"*
Response: Dull, vague ache. Note that early appendicitis typically presents as visceral pain (colicky, poorly localised) transitioning to somatic pain (sharp, well-localised). The current description of a dull ache localised to the RIF is consistent with developing peritoneal irritation.

**Radiation:**
*"Does the pain travel anywhere — to your back, your shoulder tip, or down into your groin?"*
Response: No radiation. Absence of flank radiation (against renal colic), absence of shoulder tip pain (against diaphragmatic irritation from haemoperitoneum or perforated peptic ulcer), and absence of groin radiation (against ureteric colic) all refine the differential.

**Associated symptoms:**
Covered in depth under system-based screening below.

**Timing:**
*"Is the pain there all the time, or does it come and go?"*
Response: Constant and unremitting. Constant somatic pain is characteristic of peritoneal inflammation; colicky pain would suggest hollow viscus obstruction (bowel or ureter).

*"Is it getting better, worse, or staying the same?"*
Response: Progressively worsening — consistent with evolving peritonitis.

**Exacerbating and relieving factors:**
*"Have you noticed anything that makes the pain better or worse — movement, eating, certain positions, passing wind?"*
Response: No positional relief, no relation to food, no relief with passing flatus. Note: pain worsened by movement (particularly the journey to hospital, walking, coughing) suggests peritoneal irritation, which should be explored if the patient volunteers it.

**Severity:**
*"On a scale of zero to ten, where zero is no pain and ten is the worst pain you can imagine, how would you rate your pain right now?"*
Response: 6/10. A moderate severity score in a young woman is consistent with acute appendicitis and distinguishes this from the 9–10/10 of renal colic or ruptured ectopic pregnancy.

### System-Based Differential Screening

Following pain characterisation, the candidate transitions to structured screening of three differential groups. By the end of SOCRATES, the differential has already substantially narrowed: RLQ pain with periumbilical migration, constant, worsening, moderate severity, no radiation — appendicitis is the leading diagnosis. Nonetheless, each system group must be screened thoroughly.

**Group 1 — Gastrointestinal**

*General GI symptoms:*
- Nausea and vomiting: *"Have you felt nauseated? Have you vomited?"* — Nausea present; vomited once. Nausea and vomiting occur in approximately 75% of appendicitis cases, typically following the onset of pain (pain before vomiting supports appendicitis over gastroenteritis).
- Bowel habit: *"Have you had any diarrhoea or constipation?"* — None. Diarrhoea would raise gastroenteritis or IBD; obstipation would suggest bowel obstruction.
- Bloating and flatus: *"Any bloating? Are you able to pass wind?"* — No bloating; flatus present. Inability to pass flatus would raise concern for obstruction.
- Fever: *"Have you had any fevers or chills?"* — No fever reported, though the temperature of 36.5°C from vitals is noted. Low-grade pyrexia (37.5–38.5°C) is common in appendicitis; high fever suggests perforation or alternative diagnosis.

*Red flag screen — systemic and malignancy:*
Although malignancy is rare in a 27-year-old, the red flag screen is non-negotiable in any abdominal presentation:
- Unintentional weight loss: *"Have you lost any weight recently without trying?"* — No.
- Anorexia: *"How has your appetite been?"* — **Reduced since yesterday.** This is a cardinal symptom. Anorexia occurs in approximately 68% of confirmed appendicitis cases and features in the Alvarado Score. Its presence, elicited actively, significantly supports the diagnosis.
- Change in bowel habit (over weeks to months): *"Have you noticed any change in your usual bowel pattern over recent weeks?"* — No.
- Rectal bleeding or melaena: *"Have you seen any blood in your stools, or noticed your stools becoming dark or tarry?"* — No.
- Family history of bowel cancer: *"Is there any family history of bowel cancer or polyps?"* — No.
- Lumps or lymphadenopathy: *"Have you noticed any lumps anywhere in your body?"* — No.
- Lethargy or pallor: *"Have you felt unusually tired or light-headed?"* — No.

**Group 2 — Kidney, Ureter, Bladder (KUB)**

- Dysuria: *"Is there any pain or burning when you pass urine?"* — No.
- Frequency: *"Are you passing urine more frequently than usual?"* — No.
- Haematuria: *"Have you noticed any blood in your urine, or has your urine appeared dark or discoloured?"* — No.
- Loin to groin pain: Already excluded under radiation.

The absence of urinary symptoms substantially reduces the probability of urinary tract infection (UTI) or ureteric colic, though these remain in the differential given anatomical proximity.

**Group 3 — Pelvic and Gynaecological**

This group is essential in any woman of reproductive age presenting with lower abdominal or RLQ pain. Screen it methodically regardless of the apparent diagnosis:

- Vaginal bleeding: *"Have you noticed any vaginal bleeding outside of your normal periods?"* — No.
- Vaginal discharge: *"Any unusual vaginal discharge — change in colour, amount, or odour?"* — No. Absence of discharge reduces the probability of pelvic inflammatory disease (PID).
- Last menstrual period (LMP): *"When was your last period?"* — Three weeks ago. Regular cycle. A period three weeks prior makes pregnancy less likely but does not exclude it, as implantation bleeding can mimic menstruation.
- Menstrual regularity: *"Are your periods usually regular?"* — Yes.
- Sexual history: *"Are you sexually active?"* — Yes. *"Do you use contraception, such as condoms?"* — Yes. Safe sexual practices reduce but do not eliminate STI risk.
- Groin swelling or mass: *"Have you noticed any swelling or lump in the groin area?"* — No.

*Additional gynaecological symptoms if time permits:*
- Intermenstrual bleeding, post-coital bleeding, dyspareunia, and STI history can be explored if the clinical picture warrants it. In time-constrained examinations, the three-week LMP, regular cycles, vaginal symptom screen, and basic sexual history constitute an adequate gynaecological review.

### ICE — Ideas, Concerns, and Expectations

The patient has already expressed concern in her opening statement (*"I'm starting to get worried"*). The ICE framework must nonetheless be explicitly addressed, either integrated within the history or consolidated during closure.

**Ideas:** *"What do you think might be causing this pain?"* — The patient may have no specific concern or may fear something serious. Elicit and acknowledge.

**Concerns:** *"What worries you most about this pain?"* — The patient may express fear of cancer, something being "seriously wrong," or concern about time away from work or family responsibilities. Acknowledge and validate: *"That's a very understandable concern, and I want to make sure we find out exactly what's causing this."*

**Expectations:** *"What were you hoping we might be able to do for you today?"* — She may want a diagnosis, pain relief, or reassurance. Expectations frame the management plan and shared decision-making conversation.

## Differential Diagnosis

In a 27-year-old woman with RLQ pain and periumbilical migration, the differential encompasses three primary anatomical groups. The following table presents a structured differential with key discriminating features:

| Category | Diagnosis | Key Distinguishing Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Most Likely** | Acute appendicitis | Periumbilical → RIF migration; constant pain; anorexia; nausea; rebound tenderness; positive Rovsing's and psoas signs |
| **Common** | Urinary tract infection / cystitis | Dysuria, frequency, suprapubic pain; urinalysis positive for nitrites/leucocytes; no migration pattern |
| **Common** | Gastroenteritis | Diarrhoea, vomiting often preceding pain; diffuse crampy pain; possible sick contacts; no peritoneal signs |
| **Important** | Ectopic pregnancy | Missed period or irregular bleeding; peritoneal signs; haemodynamic instability; positive βhCG |
| **Important** | Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) | Bilateral pelvic tenderness; cervical motion tenderness; vaginal discharge; fever; history of STI |
| **Important** | Ovarian torsion | Sudden severe unilateral pain; nausea/vomiting; absent venous flow on Doppler ultrasound |
| **Important** | Ovarian cyst (rupture or haemorrhage) | Mid-cycle timing; acute onset; may resolve spontaneously; ultrasound diagnostic |
| **Important** | Renal/ureteric colic | Loin-to-groin radiation; colicky (not constant) pain; haematuria; no peritoneal signs |
| **Must Not Miss** | Ruptured ectopic pregnancy | Haemodynamic instability; peritoneal signs; positive βhCG; requires emergency surgery |
| **Must Not Miss** | Perforated appendix | Sudden improvement followed by generalised peritonitis; rigid abdomen; sepsis; surgical emergency |
| **Must Not Miss** | Mesenteric ischaemia | Pain out of proportion to signs; atrial fibrillation; vascular risk factors; requires urgent CT angiography |
| **Consider** | Meckel's diverticulitis | Clinically indistinguishable from appendicitis; diagnosed intraoperatively |
| **Consider** | Crohn's disease / IBD | Chronic diarrhoea; weight loss; perianal disease; young patient; elevated CRP/ESR; colonoscopy diagnostic |
| **Consider** | Inguinal hernia | Groin swelling; reducible or incarcerated; worse with Valsalva |

## Red Flags — Must Not Miss

The following features, if present, require immediate escalation and must be actively screened in every candidate presenting with abdominal pain:

- **Haemodynamic instability (hypotension, tachycardia)** — Suggests ruptured ectopic pregnancy, perforated viscus, or septic shock. Requires immediate resuscitation and senior review before proceeding with history.
- **Positive pregnancy test (βhCG) in the context of abdominal pain and amenorrhoea** — Ectopic pregnancy must be excluded urgently. Do not await ultrasound confirmation before involving the surgical or gynaecological team.
- **Shoulder tip pain** — Diaphragmatic irritation from haemoperitoneum (ruptured ectopic or perforated viscus); requires urgent imaging.
- **Generalised rigidity or board-like abdomen** — Generalised peritonitis indicating perforation; requires immediate surgical referral.
- **High fever (≥38.5°C) with rigidity** — Perforation with peritonitis or alternative infective aetiology; escalate immediately.
- **Sudden improvement in pain after severe pain** — Classic indication of appendiceal perforation with temporary decompression; paradoxically warrants urgent escalation, not reassurance.
- **Anorexia with weight loss over weeks** — Raises concern for underlying malignancy or chronic inflammatory bowel disease; must be pursued with further investigation.
- **Melaena or frank rectal bleeding** — Lower GI bleed requiring urgent colonoscopy and haematological assessment.
- **Pain out of proportion to clinical signs** — Classic presentation of mesenteric ischaemia, particularly in older or vascularly compromised patients; requires urgent CT angiography.
- **Inability to pass flatus with distension and vomiting** — Bowel obstruction; plain abdominal X-ray and surgical review required urgently.

## Investigations

**Bedside:**
- Urine dipstick and microscopy — screen for UTI, haematuria (renal colic), proteinuria
- Urine βhCG — mandatory in all women of reproductive age with lower abdominal pain, regardless of reported contraceptive use or menstrual history
- Blood glucose level — exclude diabetic ketoacidosis as a cause of abdominal pain
- Observations with postural blood pressure — confirm haemodynamic stability; detect occult hypotension

**Bloods:**
- Full blood count (FBC) — leucocytosis with neutrophilia (shift to left) is the most characteristic haematological finding in acute appendicitis; white cell count typically 10–18 × 10⁹/L
- C-reactive protein (CRP) — elevated; rises with severity of inflammation; useful for trend monitoring
- Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) — non-specific but elevated in inflammatory states
- Electrolytes, urea, creatinine (EUC) — baseline renal function; assess for dehydration
- Liver function tests (LFTs) — if upper abdominal pathology considered
- Lipase — if pancreatitis is in the differential
- Blood cultures — if fever present or sepsis suspected

**Imaging:**
- Ultrasound (USS) abdomen and pelvis — first-line imaging; avoids ionising radiation in a young woman; can visualise a non-compressible, aperistaltic appendix (>6mm in diameter) and free fluid; also assesses for ovarian pathology. Sensitivity for appendicitis approximately 75–90%; a negative USS does not exclude appendicitis
- CT abdomen and pelvis (with IV contrast) — gold standard for appendicitis when clinical uncertainty persists or USS is non-diagnostic; sensitivity >95%, specificity >95%; reserved for cases where surgical decision remains unclear to minimise radiation exposure in young women
- Transvaginal ultrasound — if gynaecological pathology (ovarian torsion, PID, ectopic) requires further characterisation

**Specialist:**
- Surgical review — all confirmed or probable appendicitis requires early surgical referral; do not delay for imaging if clinical picture is compelling
- Gynaecology review — if ectopic pregnancy or ovarian torsion suspected
- Alvarado Score — a validated clinical scoring tool incorporating migration, anorexia, nausea/vomiting, RIF tenderness, rebound tenderness, elevated temperature, leucocytosis, and shift to left; score ≥7 is associated with high probability of appendicitis and supports operative intervention

## Management

**Acute management (emergency department):**

1. **Nil by mouth** — patient must be fasted in anticipation of general anaesthesia for appendicectomy
2. **Intravenous access and fluid resuscitation** — insert two large-bore cannulae; commence IV crystalloid (0.9% sodium chloride or Hartmann's solution) to correct dehydration from reduced oral intake, nausea, and vomiting
3. **Analgesia** — opioid analgesia (IV morphine or fentanyl) is appropriate and does not mask clinical signs to a degree that compromises surgical assessment; withholding analgesia in acute abdominal pain is not evidence-based practice and is ethically unjustifiable
4. **Antiemetics** — IV ondansetron or metoclopramide for nausea and vomiting
5. **IV antibiotics** — commence prior to surgery; typically a combination providing Gram-negative and anaerobic cover; common regimens include IV cefazolin + metronidazole or piperacillin-tazobactam per local institutional protocol and microbiology guidelines
6. **Urinary catheter** — insert prior to surgery for fluid balance monitoring
7. **Surgical referral** — urgent review by general surgical registrar or consultant; operative decision based on clinical findings, Alvarado Score, and imaging results

**Definitive management:**
- **Appendicectomy** — urgent surgical removal of the appendix; laparoscopic approach is standard of care in Australia for uncomplicated appendicitis, with conversion to open surgery if perforation, abscess, or anatomical complexity demands it
- **Open appendicectomy** — indicated in cases of generalised peritonitis, perforation with widespread contamination, or when laparoscopic approach is technically not feasible

**Perforated appendicitis:**
- Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics (extended duration, typically 3–5 days post-operatively)
- Consideration of CT-guided drainage for periappendiceal abscess before interval appendicectomy
- ICU involvement if septic shock

**Post-operative care:**
- Resume oral intake as tolerated post-operatively
- Wound care and monitoring for surgical site infection
- Discharge typically 24–48 hours post-laparoscopic appendicectomy; longer for open or complicated cases
- Outpatient follow-up at 4–6 weeks

## Communication and Patient Education

**Explaining the diagnosis — acute appendicitis:**

Deliver the diagnosis using clear, jargon-free language. The technical term must be stated — both for the examiner and for the patient's medical understanding — followed immediately by a simple lay explanation:

*"Jenna, based on everything you've told me and what I found on examination, I believe you have a condition called acute appendicitis. Let me explain what that means. The appendix is a small, finger-shaped pouch attached to your large bowel, here on the lower right side of your abdomen. It's become inflamed — that is, infected and swollen — and that is what's causing your pain."*

**Clinical reasoning — always verbalise the evidence:**

*"I've reached this diagnosis because of several things: you told me your pain started around your belly button and then moved to the lower right side — that's a classic pattern we see with appendicitis. Your appetite has been poor since yesterday, and you've felt nauseated. When I examined your abdomen, you had tenderness and what we call rebound tenderness on the right side, and the specific tests we do for appendicitis were positive."*

This structured reasoning demonstrates to the examiner that the diagnosis is evidence-based, not intuitive.

**Explaining differentials to the patient:**

Introduce differentials in plain language with brief reasoning for their exclusion:

- *"I also considered whether this could be a pregnancy outside the womb — what we call an ectopic pregnancy — but your pregnancy test was negative and your last period was recent, so that's unlikely."*
- *"I thought about an infection in the pelvis — called pelvic inflammatory disease — but you haven't had any vaginal discharge and you don't have a fever, so that's less likely."*
- *"I also considered a hernia in the groin, but you have no swelling there, and the examination was normal in that area."*
- *"A bladder infection was considered, but you have no burning or increased frequency when passing urine."*

**Addressing the patient's concern:**
Return explicitly to the concern expressed at the outset: *"I remember you said you were worried about this. I want you to know that you've done the right thing by coming in today. This is a condition we treat very successfully, and you're in the right place."*

**What to tell the patient about next steps:**
- *"We'll need to take some blood tests and perform an ultrasound scan of your abdomen."*
- *"You'll need to avoid eating and drinking from now in case you need surgery."*
- *"The surgical team will come to review you shortly, and they'll talk to you in detail about the operation."*
- *"Surgery to remove the appendix — called an appendicectomy — is a common, well-established procedure. Most patients recover well and go home within one to two days."*

## AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

- **Always request vital signs before beginning history.** In any patient presenting to the emergency department, haemodynamic stability must be confirmed. A 27-year-old with abdominal pain could have a ruptured ectopic pregnancy; unstable vitals change everything about the clinical approach and must be identified immediately.

- **The migration of pain is the single most discriminating feature of appendicitis — ask it explicitly.** Do not assume the patient will volunteer it. Ask directly: *"Where did the pain start before it moved to where it is now?"* This is a deliberate key point of the station; its omission cannot be compensated for by any other positive finding.

- **Anorexia must be actively elicited, not assumed.** Ask: *"How has your appetite been since the pain started?"* Anorexia is a component of the Alvarado Score and a cardinal symptom of appendicitis. Many candidates omit this question and lose a critical differentiating mark.

- **Nausea and vomiting in appendicitis follow the pain — not precede it.** If vomiting precedes the pain, consider gastroenteritis or bowel obstruction. The temporal relationship of symptoms is diagnostically important.

- **Complete the pelvic screen even when you are confident of appendicitis.** A complete, systematic approach to a woman of reproductive age requires exclusion of gynaecological pathology. Ovarian torsion, ectopic pregnancy, and PID can all present identically to appendicitis on history. The examiner expects clinical thoroughness, not premature diagnostic closure.

- **Urine βhCG is mandatory in every woman of reproductive age with lower abdominal pain.** This is non-negotiable in clinical practice and in the examination context. A missed ectopic pregnancy is a medico-legal catastrophe.

- **Structure your differential presentation into two tiers: good differentials with reasoning, followed by a list.** For time-pressured stations, name your top four differentials with a brief explanation and exclusion rationale first, then list remaining differentials rapidly. Do not begin with a long undifferentiated list — it signals absence of clinical hierarchy.

- **Offer analgesia — do not over-invest in it.** In 2025 AMC Clinical Examination settings, some role players actively express pain. Ask two questions: *"Are you in pain now?"* and *"Would you like me to arrange something for the pain?"*. If accepted: *"I'll arrange that — let's continue while we get that sorted."* Do not take an allergy history, discuss opioid choices, or calculate dosing at this stage. Return to history immediately.

- **When verbalising your diagnosis to the examiner and patient, always include your clinical reasoning.** Stating the diagnosis alone is insufficient. Name the diagnosis, explain it simply, then articulate the specific findings — migration of pain, anorexia, rebound tenderness, positive Rovsing's sign — that support it. This demonstrates diagnostic formulation, which is the cognitive skill the examiner is marking.

- **The Alvarado Score does not need to be recited or scored formally in the examination.** However, knowing its components — migration, anorexia, nausea/vomiting, RIF tenderness, rebound tenderness, elevated temperature, leucocytosis, left shift — ensures you ask all the clinically relevant questions without omission.

- **Physical examination findings in this station are obtained by requesting them from the examiner.** Ask specifically: *"On palpation, is there tenderness in the right iliac fossa?"* and *"Is there rebound tenderness?"* Rebound tenderness is the single most important positive finding to request; its presence confirms peritoneal irritation and clinches the appendicitis diagnosis in context.

- **Do not attempt to perform special tests on the role player.** Rovsing's sign, the psoas sign, and the obturator sign are requested from the examiner by name, who will report the result. Ask for no more than two; excessive special tests consume time without adding diagnostic value once two are positive.

## Safety Net

**Before discharge or at any point of clinical uncertainty, provide the following specific safety-netting information:**

*Immediate return to the emergency department if any of the following occur:*

- **Sudden severe worsening of pain** — may indicate perforation of the appendix
- **Sudden unexpected improvement in pain followed by generalised abdominal pain** — the paradoxical improvement of perforation, followed by generalised peritonitis, is a surgical emergency
- **Fever or rigors (temperature >38.5°C)** — suggests evolving sepsis or perforation
- **Vomiting that prevents fluid intake** — risk of dehydration and deterioration
- **Light-headedness, fainting, or racing heart rate** — possible haemodynamic compromise; ruptured ectopic must be excluded immediately if pregnant
- **Abdominal distension or inability to pass flatus** — concern for bowel obstruction
- **Signs of wound infection post-operatively** — increasing redness, swelling, discharge, or fever at the incision site

**Recommended follow-up:**
- Surgical outpatient clinic at 4–6 weeks post-operatively for wound review and histopathological confirmation of appendiceal pathology
- GP review within one week of hospital discharge to confirm recovery trajectory and address any post-operative concerns
- Written discharge instructions must include the above red flag symptoms in plain English and provide after-hours contact details for the treating surgical unit

**Written information:**
Provide the patient with institution-specific written discharge information about appendicectomy, including wound care, activity restrictions, dietary guidance, and red flag symptom recognition. In Australia, MyHealth Record may be used to document the discharge summary and operative details for the benefit of future treating clinicians.
