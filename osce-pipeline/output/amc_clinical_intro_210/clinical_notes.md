# The AMC Clinical Examination: Examination Design, Assessment Framework, and Candidate Preparation

## Clinical Presentation

The Australian Medical Council (AMC) Clinical Examination is an Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) designed to assess the clinical competence of internationally trained medical graduates seeking registration to practise medicine in Australia. Unlike written knowledge assessments, this examination evaluates candidates in simulated clinical encounters using trained role-players in structured stations, each with defined tasks and a marking rubric completed by an examiner in real time.

The examination is clinically significant for candidates because it operates on a fundamentally different paradigm from knowledge-based assessments. A candidate may possess extensive theoretical knowledge yet fail the examination if they cannot translate that knowledge into a coherent, patient-centred clinical encounter within the defined time constraints. The historical pass rate of approximately 25% reflects this disconnect: the majority of candidates who fail are clinically knowledgeable practitioners who have not adequately prepared for the performance and delivery dimensions of an OSCE format. Understanding this distinction before beginning clinical preparation is essential to structuring an effective study plan.

The examination is grounded in Miller's Pyramid of Clinical Competence — a foundational framework in medical education. The base levels of the pyramid ("Knows" and "Knows How") are assessed by the AMC Part 1 Multiple Choice Question (MCQ) examination, which candidates will have already passed at this stage. The AMC Clinical Examination assesses the upper levels — "Shows How" and "Does" — requiring candidates to demonstrate clinical competence in a simulated environment. This distinction defines the entire preparation strategy: the question is no longer *what do you know*, but *how do you show what you know*.

---

## Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

Each AMC Clinical Examination station is eight minutes in duration and comprises one or more defined tasks. Common task structures include:

- **History only (6 minutes):** Take a focused history, state your working diagnosis and differential diagnoses.
- **History plus physical examination (4 minutes each):** Take a focused history, perform a directed physical examination, state findings and diagnosis.
- **Communication and counselling stations:** Counsel the patient, address concerns, provide information, formulate a shared management plan.
- **Management stations:** Interpret investigations, formulate a management plan, communicate findings to the patient or a colleague.

The examiner scores the candidate against a pre-defined marking checklist. Each item on the checklist is either completed or not. There is no partial credit for incomplete tasks. Failure to complete all defined tasks within the allotted time results in automatic failure of the station, regardless of the quality of work completed up to that point.

The examiner does not have the discretion to pass a candidate based on their perceived knowledge, demeanour, or credentials. The scoring is strictly observational: the examiner records only what the candidate *demonstrates*, not what the candidate *knows*. This principle has profound implications for preparation — every piece of relevant knowledge must be actively verbalised and performed during the consultation.

## Systematic Approach

### The Two-Component Model of AMC Clinical Examination Performance

Candidate performance in the AMC Clinical Examination can be understood as the product of two distinct and equally necessary components: the **Knowledge Component** and the **Delivery Component**. Deficiency in either component, regardless of strength in the other, will result in failure. These components must be prepared for separately and concurrently throughout the study period.

**Knowledge Component**

The knowledge required for the AMC Clinical Examination is a carefully defined *slice* of clinical knowledge — not the encyclopaedic depth required for subspecialty practice, and not the breadth of a comprehensive textbook. For any given clinical presentation, the candidate must possess:

- A structured understanding of the **differential diagnosis**, ranked by probability and clinical urgency
- Knowledge of the **targeted history** relevant to that presentation, including cardinal features, associated symptoms, red flags, and relevant risk factors
- Familiarity with the **directed physical examination** appropriate to the presentation
- Understanding of the **key investigations** and their clinical rationale
- A coherent **management framework** applicable to the Australian primary care and hospital context

The critical distinction is that this knowledge must be structured around *clinical approach* rather than isolated facts. For example, for a presentation of headache, the candidate does not need to recall the molecular mechanism of trigeminovascular activation. The candidate must be able to demonstrate: what the differential diagnoses of headache are, which features in the history distinguish them, which red flags mandate urgent investigation, which examination findings support each diagnosis, and what the management pathway is for each. This is applied, structured, contextualised clinical knowledge.

Candidates preparing from recall-based notes — compiled from past candidate feedback, peer-generated summaries, or recall repositories — face a significant risk. Such notes capture *what was asked* in a prior station without capturing *how the consultation was conducted*, *why those questions were clinically relevant*, or *how the examiner scored the performance*. A list of questions to ask in a headache station is not the same as a conceptual framework for approaching a headache presentation. The former produces mechanical, disjointed questioning; the latter produces a logical, patient-centred clinical encounter that earns marks.

**Delivery Component**

The Delivery Component encompasses all the factors that determine whether a candidate's knowledge is successfully communicated to the examiner within the constraints of the examination. It is multifactorial and requires deliberate preparation through structured practice.

The principal elements of the Delivery Component are:

1. **Stress Management** — The AMC Clinical Examination is a high-stakes, face-to-face examination conducted in an unfamiliar environment with strangers. Examination stress is universal and predictable. Its effect on performance is well-documented: under acute stress, candidates default to habitual behaviours, lose access to structured frameworks, make decisions impulsively, and underperform relative to their rehearsed ability. Stress management is not eliminated by knowing more; it is ameliorated by repeated, deliberate practice under conditions that simulate the examination environment.

2. **Time Management** — Each station has defined time boundaries enforced by external signals. Candidates do not have access to timing devices during the station. Failure to complete all tasks results in automatic station failure. Time management is a skill acquired through practice, not through knowledge acquisition. Candidates must develop an internal sense of time through repeated timed role-plays so that they can self-regulate the pace of their consultation without external cues.

3. **Professional Interviewing Skills** — This is the most clinically significant and frequently misunderstood element of the Delivery Component. Professional interviewing skills are not equivalent to empathy, nor to fluency in English. They represent the technical craft of conducting a clinical consultation: active listening, appropriate follow-up of patient responses, integration of volunteered information into the clinical narrative, and acknowledgement of patient concerns before proceeding with systematic questioning.

4. **Communication Skills** — In the context of the AMC Clinical Examination, communication skills are assessed across all station types, not only in designated communication stations. From 2025, the AMC has increased the weighting of communication performance across the examination. Communication skills in this context include: the ability to address the patient's presenting concern at the outset of the consultation; the ability to elicit the patient's ideas, concerns, and expectations (ICE); the ability to convey clinical information in accessible language; and the ability to formulate a shared management plan that reflects patient preferences and clinical evidence.

### Professional Interviewing Skills — The Distinguishing Factor Between Pass and Fail

In stations where two candidates ask identical questions and arrive at identical differentials, the candidate who passes is distinguished by *how* they conduct the interview, not *what* they ask. This principle is consistently observed by AMC examiners and is a reliable predictor of station outcome.

Professional interviewing in the AMC Clinical context requires:

**Active Listening**
- The candidate listens to the patient's full response before formulating the next question
- Positive findings are acknowledged, explored, and followed up — not recorded and discarded
- When a patient volunteers a symptom, that symptom is incorporated into the clinical reasoning being demonstrated to the examiner

**Example:** A patient presenting with chest pain volunteers that the pain is worse when they lie flat. The non-proficient candidate acknowledges this and proceeds to the next item on their mental list. The proficient candidate says: *"You mentioned the pain is worse when you lie down — can you tell me more about that? Does it ease when you sit forward?"* This demonstrates clinical reasoning (pleuritic or pericardial aetiology), active listening, and systematic follow-up — all of which are scored behaviours.

**Addressing Presenting Concerns at the Outset**
The patient (role-player) will often open the consultation with an expressed concern — a fear, a worry, or a specific expectation. In a real clinical encounter, an experienced clinician acknowledges this concern before proceeding with systematic history taking. In the AMC Clinical Examination, failing to acknowledge the patient's concern at the outset is a scored deficiency.

**Appropriate opening statement:**
> *"I can see this has been worrying you. Before I ask you some questions to help me understand what's going on, I want you to know that I'm here to work through this with you. Can you start by telling me what's been concerning you most?"*

This opening accomplishes four scored behaviours: acknowledgement of distress, establishment of a therapeutic alliance, invitation for the patient to lead with their concern, and a structured transition into the history.

**The Distinction Between Empathy and Communication Skill**
Empathy — the expression of warmth, care, and emotional attunement — is a necessary but insufficient component of the communication domain. Candidates who are counselled to "just be empathic" in the AMC Clinical Examination routinely fail, because empathic expression without clinical structure does not satisfy the marking rubric. The examiner is scoring specific, observable communication behaviours: Was the patient's concern elicited? Was the diagnosis explained in lay terms? Was the patient's understanding checked? Were follow-up instructions provided? These are technical communication skills, not emotional responses.

Empathy has an appropriate place within the consultation — it should be expressed genuinely and fluidly, woven into the clinical encounter rather than performed as a discrete act. However, it does not substitute for clinical structure, systematic history taking, accurate diagnosis, or appropriate management.

### The Role of Practice in Developing Delivery Skills

Role-play practice is the primary vehicle for developing Delivery Component skills. However, role-play is only effective when conducted with clearly defined learning objectives for each session.

**Structured practice session framework:**

- Define 2–3 specific skills to develop in the session (e.g., time management, ICE elicitation, closing a consultation)
- Select cases appropriate to the skills being practised, not simply the cases that feel most comfortable
- Use a timer strictly to replicate examination conditions
- Review the session against specific criteria: Was the task completed within time? Was the patient's concern addressed? Were red flags screened? Was a differential stated?
- Practise without a partner by narrating the consultation aloud to a simulated patient — this develops fluency of expression and structural memory in the absence of a practice partner

The goal of role-play practice is not to memorise a script for each case. It is to develop an automatic, flexible clinical consultation structure that can be applied to any presentation, including unfamiliar presentations, within the examination time frame.

## Differential Diagnosis

For the AMC Clinical Examination as an assessment context, the following framework captures the differential scenarios candidates face across station types:

| Category | Station Scenario | Key Distinguishing Features |
|----------|-----------------|----------------------------|
| Common | History-taking station — acute presentation | Focused differential, red flag screen, 6-minute limit |
| Common | Communication station — information giving | ICE elicitation, lay language explanation, shared decision making |
| Common | Physical examination station | Directed examination relevant to history, clinical findings verbally communicated |
| Important | Combined history and examination station | Time-critical task completion; both domains must be completed |
| Important | Communication station — breaking bad news | Structured approach (e.g., SPIKES framework adapted to Australian context), exploring patient's prior understanding |
| Must Not Miss | Station involving a medical emergency presentation | Recognition of urgency, appropriate immediate management communicated, safety netting |
| Must Not Miss | Station with a distressed or emotionally complex patient | Failure to address affect before proceeding with clinical tasks is a scored deficiency |

## Red Flags — Must Not Miss

The following represent critical performance failures in the AMC Clinical Examination that result in automatic or near-certain station failure:

- **Failure to complete all defined tasks** — Each task in the station is a discrete scored domain. An incomplete task cannot be compensated for by excellence in a completed task. Candidates must practise rigorous time management to ensure all tasks are reached.

- **Failure to address the patient's presenting concern** — The AMC marking framework includes specific items for patient-centredness. Proceeding directly to systematic questioning without acknowledging the patient's expressed worry or distress is a consistently scored deficit.

- **Mechanical, list-driven questioning without clinical integration** — Asking questions from a memorised list without integrating patient responses into a clinical narrative is recognisable to trained examiners and is negatively scored. The consultation must demonstrate active clinical reasoning, not recall recitation.

- **Absence of a differential diagnosis** — In history and examination stations, the examiner expects the candidate to explicitly state a working diagnosis and a structured differential. Failure to do so leaves the clinical reasoning domain unscored.

- **Red flag omission** — If a presentation contains a potential red flag symptom (e.g., haemoptysis in a cough presentation, weight loss in a gastrointestinal presentation, sudden-onset severe headache) and the candidate fails to elicit or acknowledge it, this is a patient safety failure and a critical mark loss.

- **Over-reliance on empathic language at the expense of clinical content** — Delivering a consultation that is emotionally warm but clinically incomplete does not satisfy the marking rubric. Both dimensions must be present.

- **Neglect of the English language as a barrier** — While the AMC Clinical Examination does not score English fluency or accent, candidates who have studied medicine in a non-English language and have not practised clinical communication in English should invest time in developing their lay medical vocabulary. The ability to explain clinical concepts in accessible English is a scored communication skill.

## Investigations

In the context of the AMC Clinical Examination as a preparation framework, investigations are station-specific. The general principle for investigation ordering in any station is:

**Bedside:** Vital signs, oxygen saturation, ECG, urinalysis, blood glucose — order what is immediately available and clinically indicated for the presenting complaint

**Bloods:** Full blood count, electrolytes, renal and liver function, inflammatory markers, coagulation — ordered logically based on the differential, not reflexively

**Imaging:** Selected based on clinical indication — chest X-ray, CT, ultrasound — with clinical rationale stated to the examiner

**Specialist:** Referral or specialist investigation ordered when the differential includes a condition requiring subspecialty assessment

In the examination, the candidate must demonstrate *why* each investigation is ordered, not simply list investigations. The examiner is scoring clinical reasoning, not a list.

## Management

Management in the AMC Clinical Examination context is assessed through the candidate's ability to:

1. **Formulate a coherent management plan** appropriate to the diagnosis and clinical context
2. **Communicate that plan to the patient** in accessible language with appropriate explanation of rationale
3. **Incorporate patient preferences** through a shared decision-making approach
4. **Provide clear safety netting** — what the patient should watch for, when to return, and under what circumstances to seek emergency care
5. **Arrange appropriate follow-up** — general practitioner review, specialist referral, or outpatient investigation as clinically indicated

The management plan should be structured as:
- **Immediate:** Any acute interventions required
- **Short-term:** Investigations, pharmacological or non-pharmacological treatment initiation, referrals
- **Ongoing:** Monitoring, chronic disease management, preventive health measures
- **Safety net:** Patient education about warning signs and follow-up pathway

## Communication & Patient Education

The communication domain of the AMC Clinical Examination warrants specific preparation independent of clinical knowledge. The following principles govern effective communication performance across all station types:

**Opening the Consultation**
Every consultation should begin with a warm, professional greeting, introduction of the candidate's role, and an open invitation for the patient to describe their concern. The patient's presenting concern must be acknowledged before systematic questioning begins. This is not merely good practice — it is a scored item.

**Eliciting ICE (Ideas, Concerns, and Expectations)**
The ICE framework is central to patient-centred communication in Australian clinical practice and is assessed across multiple station types:

- **Ideas:** *"What do you think might be causing your symptoms?"* / *"Has anything occurred to you about what this might be?"*
- **Concerns:** *"What worries you most about this?"* / *"Is there something specific you were concerned it might be?"*
- **Expectations:** *"What were you hoping we might be able to do today?"* / *"What would be most helpful for you from this consultation?"*

ICE is not a rigid script — it is a clinical orientation. In a well-conducted AMC consultation, ICE is elicited fluidly within the history, not bolted on as a checklist exercise.

**Information Giving — The Explain-Check-Explore Cycle**
When providing information to a patient (diagnosis, management plan, investigation results), the candidate should:

1. *Explain* — Provide information in clear, simple language, free of unexplained medical jargon
2. *Check* — Ask the patient to reflect back their understanding: *"Can I ask you to tell me in your own words what you've understood about what I've just explained?"*
3. *Explore* — Invite questions and address residual concerns: *"Is there anything that is still worrying you, or anything that hasn't been clear?"*

**Shared Decision Making**
Management decisions in the AMC Clinical Examination should be presented as collaborative, not directive. The candidate presents options with their respective benefits and risks, checks the patient's values and preferences, and arrives at a plan that the patient has actively agreed to. This is particularly important in communication stations involving treatment choices, lifestyle modifications, or screening decisions.

**Closing the Consultation**
Every consultation should close with:
- A summary of the agreed plan
- Clear safety net instructions
- An expression of availability: *"Please don't hesitate to come back if things change or you have any more questions."*
- A professional farewell

## AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

- **Understand the design before beginning clinical preparation.** The AMC Clinical Examination assesses *performance*, not *knowledge*. Every study session should be oriented toward building a performable consultation structure, not accumulating facts.

- **Recall-based notes are a preparation aid, not a preparation strategy.** Notes derived from past candidate feedback capture what was asked in a prior consultation; they do not capture how the consultation was scored. Use them to supplement a conceptual understanding of each case, not to replace it.

- **Never complete a task and leave clinical reasoning implicit.** In every station, verbalise your clinical reasoning explicitly to the examiner. State your differential. Explain why you are ordering an investigation. Connect examination findings to your diagnosis. The examiner scores what they observe, not what they infer.

- **Address the patient's concern before beginning systematic questioning.** This is a reliably scored item. Even a brief acknowledgement — *"I can see this has been worrying you — let's work through this together"* — satisfies this criterion and establishes a patient-centred consultation tone.

- **Time management must be practised, not planned.** An intellectual understanding of time limits does not translate into timed performance. Use a stopwatch in every practice session. Develop an internal sense of how long each component of a consultation takes. Never sacrifice a task for depth in a prior task.

- **ICE is not optional.** In history and communication stations, the candidate who fails to elicit the patient's ideas, concerns, and expectations misses scored items regardless of the quality of their systematic history. ICE elicitation should be practised until it is automatic.

- **Empathy is necessary but not sufficient.** A warm, emotionally attuned consultation that lacks clinical structure, a differential diagnosis, or appropriate red flag screening will fail. Both the clinical and communication domains must be present and complete.

- **Interviewing skill is the differentiator between candidates with equal knowledge.** Follow up positive responses. Acknowledge what the patient tells you. Integrate volunteered information into your clinical reasoning out loud. This is the observable behaviour that separates a passing from a failing performance.

- **English fluency is not the target; clinical communication is.** The examination does not score accent or grammatical precision. It scores the candidate's ability to conduct a coherent, patient-centred clinical consultation. Candidates with limited English confidence should focus on practising lay explanations of common diagnoses, not on perfecting pronunciation.

- **Prepare specifically for the slice of knowledge required.** For any presentation, the candidate needs: a structured differential, a targeted history framework including red flags, a directed examination, an investigation plan, and a management approach. This is the necessary and sufficient scope of clinical knowledge for the AMC Clinical Examination.

- **Practice sessions must have defined objectives.** Role-play without a specific learning target does not build the skills required for the examination. Each session should focus on one or two specific delivery skills: time completion, ICE elicitation, differential delivery, or closing a consultation.

- **Failure to complete all station tasks is automatic station failure.** This is non-negotiable. If the candidate is running low on time, it is always preferable to state a differential rapidly and incompletely than to miss the task entirely. Partial completion of a final task scores more marks than no attempt.

## Safety Net

In the context of the AMC Clinical Examination preparation, the safety net for candidates is structured as follows:

**Recognise early preparation failure patterns** — If practice sessions consistently result in incomplete tasks, over-running time, or inability to state a differential, these are examination-failure patterns that must be addressed through targeted practice before the examination date.

**Do not sit the examination without delivery preparation** — Knowledge alone is insufficient. Candidates who sit the examination relying on clinical knowledge without having developed timed, structured consultation skills are at high risk of failure regardless of their clinical experience.

**Seek structured feedback from qualified sources** — Feedback from peers, while valuable, may not replicate examiner scoring criteria. Where possible, candidates should seek feedback from those familiar with AMC Clinical Examination marking frameworks to ensure their practice is calibrated to actual examination standards.

**Follow-up pathway if unsuccessful** — Candidates who are unsuccessful in the AMC Clinical Examination should specifically review which station tasks were incomplete, whether the communication domain was adequately addressed, and whether timed practice was sufficient in their preparation. Targeted preparation addressing identified deficits is more effective than repeating the same preparation strategy.

**Return for further preparation if** — the candidate cannot consistently complete all tasks within the allotted time in practice conditions; if the candidate cannot formulate a coherent differential for common presentations; or if the patient's concerns and ICE have not been routinely integrated into practice consultations.
