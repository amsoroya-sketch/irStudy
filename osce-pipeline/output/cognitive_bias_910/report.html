Cognitive Biases in Clinical Reasoning — AMC Clinical Examination

Clinical Presentation

The AMC Clinical Examination assesses not merely whether a candidate arrives at a correct diagnosis, but whether the candidate demonstrates a sound, structured, and transparent clinical reasoning process. One of the most consequential — yet least discussed — domains of examination performance is the presence of systematic cognitive errors that distort clinical thinking. These cognitive biases are well recognised in the international medical education literature and are explicitly incorporated into AMC examiner training. A candidate may reach the correct diagnosis and still fail a station if the examiner observes that the reasoning process was driven by shortcutting, assumption, or anchoring rather than by genuine clinical enquiry.

Cognitive biases in the OSCE context are not random errors or momentary lapses in concentration. They are systematic, reproducible patterns of flawed reasoning — predictable distortions in the way the brain processes information when under time pressure, stress, or when prior knowledge (such as familiarity with common station recalls) creates a premature cognitive framework. The AMC Clinical Examination is designed, in part, to detect and penalise these patterns. Examiners are trained to recognise them. Candidates who are unaware of their own biases are consequently at significant disadvantage.

This topic is clinically important beyond the examination context. Cognitive bias in clinical practice is a well-documented contributor to diagnostic error, patient harm, and medicolegal risk. The AMC Clinical Examination, in testing for the absence of cognitive bias, is therefore assessing a core competency of safe medical practice in Australia. Understanding, recognising, and actively counteracting cognitive bias is essential preparation for both examination success and for safe, independent clinical practice as a registered medical practitioner in Australia.

Counselling Pathway

Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

In any AMC Clinical Examination station — whether history taking, physical examination, or communication — the examiner is assessing the process of clinical reasoning as much as the outcome. The marking rubric rewards candidates who demonstrate a structured, systematic approach applied consistently regardless of how early or confidently a diagnosis seems apparent.

The examiner expects the candidate to:

  • Demonstrate an open, hypothesis-generating approach from the outset, using broad open-ended questions before narrowing to specific diagnostic criteria
  • Maintain a working differential diagnosis throughout the encounter, actively seeking information that supports and refutes each differential
  • Explicitly verify the leading diagnosis by eliciting all key diagnostic features — not merely those that confirm the presumptive diagnosis
  • Avoid telegraphing a premature diagnosis through the sequence or specificity of questions asked
  • Respond to new information dynamically, adjusting the diagnostic framework when findings are inconsistent with the initial hypothesis
  • Demonstrate appropriate uncertainty calibrated to the information available at each stage of the encounter

The examiner is not looking for a candidate who knows the answer. The examiner is looking for a candidate who knows how to find the answer safely, reliably, and reproducibly — even in the face of unfamiliar presentations or atypical findings.

Systematic Approach

Understanding Cognitive Bias in Clinical Reasoning

A cognitive bias is a systematic error in thinking that arises when the brain attempts to simplify information processing by applying mental shortcuts — heuristics — in place of rigorous analytical reasoning. In everyday clinical practice, heuristics are often adaptive: an experienced clinician rapidly pattern-matches to common presentations, saving time and cognitive effort. However, in complex, ambiguous, or atypical presentations, these same shortcuts generate predictable and consequential errors.

In the AMC Clinical Examination, cognitive bias is particularly common because:

  • Candidates possess prior knowledge of frequently tested cases (so-called "recalls") and have often revised clinical scenarios in advance
  • The examination environment creates significant time pressure and emotional stress, both of which favour rapid, heuristic-driven reasoning over slower, analytical reasoning
  • Candidates are motivated to appear competent, which can produce overconfidence and resistance to revising initial impressions

The four cognitive biases most relevant to the AMC Clinical Examination are: premature closure, confirmation bias, diagnostic momentum, and overconfidence bias. Each has a distinct mechanism, a recognisable behavioural signature, and a specific counteracting strategy.

Premature Closure

Definition: Premature closure is the tendency to cease diagnostic enquiry once an apparently satisfactory diagnosis has been reached, before that diagnosis has been fully verified against all necessary diagnostic criteria.

Mechanism: The candidate identifies one or two features consistent with a known diagnosis and, motivated by the familiarity of that pattern, accepts the diagnosis as confirmed. Subsequent history taking is either truncated or redirected away from the diagnostic verification process.

Clinical example — Headache presentation:

Consider a 35-year-old woman presenting with a six-month history of unilateral headache that improves with sleep in a dark, quiet room. A candidate familiar with migraine correctly recognises that photophobia, phonophobia, and improvement with rest are characteristic features. However, relief with sleep in a quiet room is not pathognomonic of migraine — tension-type headache, cluster headache, and other primary headache disorders may also partially improve with rest. Premature closure occurs when the candidate accepts this single feature as diagnostic of migraine without proceeding to verify:

  • Aura — visual, sensory, or motor symptoms preceding the headache
  • Nausea and vomiting — cardinal associated features of migraine
  • Photophobia and phonophobia — formally required for migraine diagnosis under ICHD-3 criteria
  • Triggers — dietary, hormonal, sleep disruption, stress
  • Frequency and duration — to establish the episodic versus chronic pattern
  • Exclusion of secondary causes — raised intracranial pressure, temporal arteritis, intracranial haemorrhage

Behavioural signature observed by the examiner: The candidate's questioning sequence narrows abruptly after one or two confirmatory responses. The candidate fails to ask the remaining key diagnostic questions. The physical examination may be abbreviated or omitted. The diagnosis is announced with inappropriate confidence given the available information.

Why the examiner will not pass this candidate: The examiner observes that the candidate has not demonstrated knowledge of the full diagnostic criteria for migraine, and has not excluded clinically important differentials. Arriving at the correct diagnosis by shortcut does not demonstrate clinical competence — it demonstrates pattern-matching from recall, which is an unreliable clinical skill.

Counteracting strategy: Regardless of how confident the candidate feels about a diagnosis after the first few responses, the candidate must continue through the full, structured history. No diagnosis should be verbally committed to until all key diagnostic criteria have been explored and the principal differentials have been addressed. The internal rule is: "I do not confirm a diagnosis until I have actively sought reasons to exclude it."

Confirmation Bias

Definition: Confirmation bias is the tendency to selectively seek and prioritise information that supports a pre-existing diagnostic hypothesis, while failing to seek information that might refute it or support an alternative diagnosis.

Mechanism: The candidate enters the station with a strong prior belief about the likely diagnosis — typically derived from familiarity with common examination cases. The history-taking sequence is then structured to elicit positive confirmatory responses, and the candidate interprets the accumulation of confirmatory data as evidence of thoroughness. Critically, the candidate does not pursue lines of enquiry that might produce negative or contradictory findings, and does not systematically rule out differential diagnoses.

Clinical example — The returning traveller with constitutional symptoms:

Consider a 25-year-old man who presents feeling unwell after returning from Indonesia, with a history of intravenous drug use. A candidate who has previously encountered this presentation as a hepatitis B or C case will commonly ask, in rapid succession:

  • "Have you had any abdominal pain?" — Yes
  • "Have you had any nausea?" — Yes
  • "Have you noticed any yellowing of your skin or eyes?" — Yes
  • "Have you had dark urine or pale stools?" — Yes

The candidate accumulates confirmatory responses and interprets this as successful history taking. When the confirmatory responses are exhausted, the history-taking process often falters — the candidate has no structured plan for what comes next, because the plan was never based on a differential diagnosis framework; it was based on a single diagnostic hypothesis.

What confirmation bias causes the candidate to miss:
In this scenario, the candidate fails to actively pursue the differential diagnoses that are equally — or more — clinically plausible in a returning traveller with intravenous drug use and constitutional symptoms:

  • HIV primary infection (acute seroconversion illness): fever, pharyngitis, morbilliform rash, cervical lymphadenopathy, myalgia — presentation within weeks of exposure
  • Infectious mononucleosis (Epstein-Barr virus): pharyngitis, lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, atypical lymphocytosis
  • Malaria: cyclic fevers, rigors, headache, splenomegaly — critical in a traveller returning from an endemic region
  • Dengue fever: high fever, retro-orbital pain, arthralgia, thrombocytopaenia
  • Leptospirosis: fever, myalgia, conjunctival suffusion, potential jaundice and renal impairment
  • Secondary syphilis: constitutional symptoms, rash, lymphadenopathy in a patient with high-risk sexual behaviour

Behavioural signature observed by the examiner: The candidate's questions follow a narrow, disease-specific sequence with no evidence of systematic differential generation. After collecting confirmatory data, the candidate appears uncertain how to proceed. There is no explicit attempt to "rule out" competing diagnoses.

Counteracting strategy: Begin every history with an open-ended question and spend the first portion of the encounter exploring the presenting complaint without a fixed diagnostic destination. Generate the differential diagnosis from the patient's words rather than from prior knowledge. For every diagnosis considered, explicitly ask at least one question that could exclude that diagnosis, not merely one that confirms it. Ask: "What would I need to find to rule out HIV in this presentation? What about malaria? What would make me less concerned about meningitis?"

Diagnostic Momentum

Definition: Diagnostic momentum is the tendency to adhere to a previously established diagnostic label even when new clinical information is inconsistent with — or directly contradicts — that diagnosis.

Mechanism: The candidate has an established cognitive framework for a given clinical presentation, often reinforced by prior exposure to the same or similar cases. When new information fails to conform to the expected pattern, the candidate experiences cognitive dissonance. Rather than revising the diagnostic hypothesis, the candidate unconsciously discounts, ignores, or reinterprets the contradictory information to preserve the original diagnosis.

Clinical example — Same stem, different diagnosis:

The same 25-year-old man returning from Indonesia with intravenous drug use is presented again. However, in this version, the history-taking yields:

  • Abdominal pain — absent
  • Nausea — absent
  • Jaundice — absent
  • Dark urine / pale stools — absent

And the physical examination reveals:

  • Morbilliform (measles-like) rash across the trunk
  • Bilateral cervical lymphadenopathy — two enlarged, non-tender nodes
  • Pharyngitis — erythematous, inflamed tonsils
  • Urine dipstick: protein positive, bilirubin negative
  • Abdominal examination: entirely normal — no hepatomegaly, no tenderness

A candidate operating under diagnostic momentum reads this constellation of findings and still documents hepatitis as the working diagnosis, despite the complete absence of hepatitic features and the presence of a rash, lymphadenopathy, and pharyngitis — findings that are cardinal features of acute HIV seroconversion illness or infectious mononucleosis.

Radiological corollary: A candidate familiar with scaphoid fracture as a commonly examined X-ray finding is shown a radiograph demonstrating a distal radial fracture (Colles' fracture). The candidate, primed by prior recall, identifies a "scaphoid fracture," despite the fracture being clearly located at the distal radius. The examiner observes that the candidate's visual processing was directed not by the radiograph but by the anticipated diagnosis.

Why diagnostic momentum is particularly dangerous: Unlike premature closure, which occurs at the beginning of the clinical encounter, diagnostic momentum is most likely to manifest at the physical examination stage — when new information arrives that does not fit the expected pattern. A candidate vulnerable to diagnostic momentum has no mechanism to change direction when the evidence contradicts the original hypothesis.

Counteracting strategy: Treat the physical examination findings as a new, independent data set — not as a confirmation exercise. After completing history taking, momentarily set aside the working diagnosis and ask: "If I had no prior history, what diagnoses would these examination findings suggest?" Then reconcile the history and examination findings. If they do not cohere around a single diagnosis, the differential must be broadened rather than the contradictory findings discarded.

Overconfidence Bias

Definition: Overconfidence bias is the systematic tendency to overestimate one's own clinical knowledge, diagnostic accuracy, and examination performance relative to actual competence.

Mechanism: In the AMC Clinical Examination context, overconfidence is most commonly generated by familiarity with examination recalls. A candidate who has reviewed case summaries from previous examination sittings may feel a sense of preparedness that exceeds actual clinical competence. This false confidence manifests as abbreviated clinical encounters, failure to pursue uncertainty, reluctance to revise diagnostic impressions, and post-failure attribution of failure to external factors rather than clinical shortcomings.

Clinical manifestations of overconfidence in the examination:

  • Truncating history taking once a presumptive diagnosis is reached
  • Presenting a single diagnosis without acknowledgement of clinical uncertainty or differential diagnoses
  • Failing to perform a systematic physical examination because findings are "expected"
  • Dismissing atypical features rather than incorporating them into an expanded differential
  • Attributing examination failure to examiner subjectivity, case difficulty, or systemic unfairness rather than recognising specific clinical deficits

The relationship between overconfidence and recalls: Familiarity with recalls creates a false equivalence between recognising a case pattern and demonstrating clinical competence. Recognising that a case is "the hepatitis scenario" does not demonstrate the ability to generate an appropriate differential, apply diagnostic criteria, exclude competing diagnoses, or manage clinical uncertainty. The examination specifically assesses these higher-order competencies, which recall-based preparation does not develop.

Counteracting strategy: Actively cultivate a posture of calibrated uncertainty. Before entering each station, remind yourself: "My task is not to demonstrate that I know the answer. My task is to demonstrate how I would safely approach this problem." After each practice case, self-critique with specific, structured questions: "Did I rule out each differential? Did I ask all the key diagnostic questions? Would an examiner observing my reasoning process be satisfied that I applied rigorous clinical thinking?"

AMC Examination Design — Systematic Counteraction of Cognitive Bias

The AMC has explicitly acknowledged, in its annual examination reports, that recall-based preparation represents an examination malpractice problem, and that examination content is periodically modified to neutralise and penalise recall-dependent reasoning. Specific design changes include:

Rapid case turnover: Rather than cycling through a limited pool of scenarios over multiple examination sittings, the AMC has progressively increased the rate at which new cases and variations are introduced. Candidates relying on the last six to twelve months of recalls as their primary preparation strategy are therefore at significant risk of encountering materially different presentations.

Modified findings within familiar stems: The same clinical stem (e.g., 25-year-old man returning from Indonesia with intravenous drug use) may be presented with findings that point to a different diagnosis than previously encountered versions of the same case. Candidates who have memorised the expected findings for the "Indonesia recall" will either prematurely close on the wrong diagnosis or demonstrate diagnostic momentum when the findings do not conform to expectations.

Multiple concurrent pathologies: Increasingly, examination cases include findings consistent with more than one diagnosis, or include findings that complicate the primary diagnostic question. This directly penalises candidates whose approach is oriented toward identifying a single diagnosis and ticking confirmatory boxes, rather than synthesising a complex clinical picture.

The implication for examination preparation is clear: structured clinical reasoning — the ability to generate, pursue, and systematically evaluate a differential diagnosis — is not a supplementary skill. It is the primary competency the examination assesses, and it cannot be simulated by recall-based shortcuts.

Red Flags — Must Not Miss

  • Non-blanching petechial or purpuric rash — Raises concern for meningococcaemia or disseminated intravascular coagulation; requires immediate emergency assessment and empirical antibiotics; never attribute to drug reaction without excluding sepsis

  • Altered conscious state or confusion — May indicate cerebral malaria, viral encephalitis, bacterial meningitis, or hepatic encephalopathy in the context of acute liver failure; warrants urgent CT brain and lumbar puncture if safe

  • Neck stiffness and photophobia — Must prompt immediate consideration of bacterial meningitis; do not defer lumbar puncture for imaging unless focal neurology or papilloedema is present; empirical ceftriaxone must not be delayed

  • Haemodynamic instability — Tachycardia, hypotension, or signs of shock in a febrile returning traveller require immediate resuscitation and consideration of falciparum malaria, bacterial sepsis, or dengue haemorrhagic fever

  • Jaundice with coagulopathy — In the context of IVDU and Southeast Asian travel, may indicate acute liver failure from hepatitis B, drug hepatotoxicity, or leptospirosis with Weil's disease; INR, bilirubin, and hepatic function must be urgently assessed

  • New cardiac murmur in IVDU — Raises significant concern for right-sided infective endocarditis (tricuspid valve most commonly affected with intravenous drug use); requires blood cultures and urgent echocardiography

  • Rapidly progressive respiratory compromise — In the febrile IVDU patient, consider pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP) indicating advanced HIV immunosuppression, pulmonary septic emboli from endocarditis, or pneumonia

  • Signs of raised intracranial pressure — Papilloedema, Cushing's triad, or progressive neurological deficits suggest intracranial pathology (cryptococcal meningitis in HIV, cerebral abscess, or herniation); imaging before lumbar puncture is mandatory

Investigations

Bedside:
- Observations: temperature, heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, respiratory rate
- Urine dipstick: bilirubin, urobilinogen, protein, blood, leucocytes
- Blood glucose (point-of-care)
- ECG if haemodynamically compromised

Bloods:
- Full blood examination and film (atypical lymphocytes for EBV/HIV; thrombocytopaenia for dengue/malaria; anaemia for haemolysis)
- Thick and thin blood films for malaria parasites (repeat every 12–24 hours if initial negative and suspicion high)
- Malaria rapid diagnostic antigen test
- Liver function tests (bilirubin, ALT, AST, ALP, GGT, albumin)
- Coagulation studies (INR, APTT — if liver failure or DIC suspected)
- Urea, creatinine, electrolytes (renal involvement in leptospirosis, dengue)
- C-reactive protein, erythrocyte sedimentation rate
- Hepatitis B surface antigen, hepatitis B core antibody (IgM), hepatitis C antibody
- HIV fourth-generation antigen/antibody combination test (Ag/Ab combo); HIV RNA PCR if seroconversion window period suspected
- EBV serology (heterophile antibodies, EBV-specific antibodies: VCA IgM/IgG, EBNA)
- Leptospira serology (MAT or ELISA), leptospira PCR if acute phase
- Dengue NS1 antigen (days 1–5), dengue IgM/IgG serology
- Blood cultures ×3 (before antibiotics if endocarditis or bacteraemia suspected)
- RPR/VDRL for syphilis with confirmatory TPPA/FTA-ABS
- Renal function, urinalysis for microscopy, culture and sensitivity

Imaging:
- Chest X-ray: assess for pneumonia, pulmonary emboli (septic emboli from endocarditis), cardiomegaly
- Abdominal ultrasound: hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, biliary dilatation, lymphadenopathy
- Echocardiography (transthoracic initially, transoesophageal if high suspicion): if endocarditis suspected

Specialist:
- Infectious diseases consultation for complex returning traveller with IVDU history
- Haematology if significant haematological abnormalities
- Gastroenterology if evidence of acute liver failure
- Cardiology if endocarditis confirmed or probable

Clinically Enriched

Management

Acute stabilisation:
Ensure haemodynamic stability. If the patient is febrile, tachycardic, or hypotensive, initiate resuscitation according to sepsis principles (intravenous access, fluid resuscitation, blood cultures before antibiotics, empirical antibiotic therapy if bacterial sepsis cannot be excluded). Do not delay treatment for diagnostic certainty in an unstable patient.

Malaria (Must Not Miss — highest priority in febrile returning traveller):
If malaria is confirmed or strongly suspected, initiate definitive antimalarial treatment without delay. Falciparum malaria can progress to cerebral malaria, multi-organ failure, and death within hours. Consult Therapeutic Guidelines: Antibiotic (eTG) and infectious diseases for current regimen (artemisinin-based combination therapy for uncomplicated falciparum malaria; intravenous artesunate for severe falciparum malaria).

Acute viral hepatitis:
Supportive management in most cases. Advise abstinence from alcohol and hepatotoxic medications. Monitor for acute liver failure with serial liver function tests and coagulation studies. Refer to gastroenterology or hepatology. Notify Public Health Unit as required under NSW Health mandatory notification requirements for hepatitis B and C.

Acute HIV seroconversion:
Refer urgently to HIV/sexual health medicine or infectious diseases. Early antiretroviral therapy (ART) initiation during acute seroconversion is now recommended — reduces viral reservoir, preserves immune function, and may reduce onward transmission risk. Ensure all sexual contacts and needle-sharing contacts are notified and offered testing and post-exposure prophylaxis where applicable.

Infectious endocarditis in IVDU:
Empirical antibiotics targeting Staphylococcus aureus (including MRSA) as the predominant organism in IVDU-associated endocarditis. Prolonged intravenous antibiotics guided by blood culture sensitivities and in consultation with infectious diseases and cardiology. Cardiac surgery consultation if complicated by valve destruction, heart failure, or persistent bacteraemia.

Harm reduction and public health:
All patients with IVDU should be offered opioid substitution therapy referral, needle and syringe programme access, and hepatitis B vaccination if not immune. Provide information on safe injecting practices and wound care. Arrange HIV and hepatitis B and C testing and counselling. Notify relevant public health authorities for notifiable infectious diseases as required.

Communication & Patient Education

Explaining the diagnostic process:
When discussing findings with a patient who has presented with constitutional symptoms following travel and intravenous drug use, transparency about the diagnostic process is essential. Explain that several different infections can produce similar symptoms, and that a structured series of tests is required before a definitive diagnosis can be made. Avoid premature diagnostic labelling in communication with the patient, as this can cause unnecessary anxiety and may be incorrect.

"James, you've told me about feeling unwell since returning from Indonesia, and I want to make sure we look at all the possible causes carefully. There are a number of infections that can cause these symptoms, particularly given your travels and some of the activities you mentioned. I'd like to run some blood tests and other investigations to find out exactly what's causing this, so we can give you the right treatment."

Addressing blood-borne virus risk sensitively:
Discussing intravenous drug use and blood-borne virus risk requires a non-judgmental, patient-centred approach. Use language that is neutral and collaborative. The patient may have concerns about confidentiality, stigma, or the implications of a positive diagnosis.

"I want to ask you about some of the things you mentioned — using heroin and sharing needles. I'm not here to judge you at all. I'm asking because these things can affect which infections we need to check for, and I want to make sure we don't miss anything important for your health."

Explaining mandatory notification:
If a notifiable disease (hepatitis B, hepatitis C, HIV) is confirmed, explain the mandatory notification process clearly and compassionately. Clarify that the patient's GP and specialist will be informed, and that certain public health authorities require notification — not for punitive purposes, but for contact tracing and community health protection.

Contact tracing and partner notification:
Sensitively explore recent sexual contacts and needle-sharing partners. Explain the importance of informing contacts who may have been exposed so that they can be tested and treated early. Offer referral to the sexual health service or public health unit for partner notification support if the patient is uncomfortable doing so independently.

Harm reduction counselling:
Provide clear, practical information about harm reduction strategies: needle and syringe programmes, opioid substitution therapy, safe injecting practices, and the availability of naloxone. Frame this as health promotion rather than moral instruction.

Written resources:
Provide patient information sheets on hepatitis B and C, HIV, and malaria as appropriate, sourced from the NSW Health or Australian Government Department of Health websites. Provide contact details for community support services (NUAA for people who use drugs in NSW, ACON for HIV-related support).

AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

  • Never commit to a diagnosis until you have actively verified it against all key diagnostic criteria. In a headache case, do not accept "dark quiet room relief" as sufficient for migraine — systematically elicit aura, nausea, photophobia, phonophobia, triggers, and duration before reaching a diagnostic conclusion.

  • Structure your history around a differential diagnosis list, not around a single suspected diagnosis. Before entering the station, generate at least three to five plausible differentials for the presenting complaint, and ensure your questioning sequence addresses each one.

  • Treat physical examination findings as an independent data set. If the examination findings are inconsistent with your working diagnosis from the history, revise the differential — do not ignore or minimise contradictory findings. The examiner is specifically watching for this.

  • Ask at least one question that could exclude each differential. Confirmation bias is most easily detected when the candidate asks only questions that generate positive confirmatory responses. Include questions with expected negative answers to demonstrate genuine differential reasoning: "Have you noticed any rash?" / "Has anyone close to you been unwell with similar symptoms?"

  • In returning travellers, malaria must be considered and explicitly excluded. Falciparum malaria is the single most dangerous and time-critical diagnosis in a febrile returning traveller. Failure to mention malaria in the differential is a significant clinical error in the AMC context.

  • In IVDU patients, always expand the differential beyond hepatitis. HIV seroconversion, infective endocarditis, skin and soft tissue infections, and wound botulism are all clinically important considerations that must be actively pursued rather than assumed absent.

  • Do not allow your questioning sequence to telegraph your working diagnosis to the examiner. A candidate who asks five consecutive hepatitis-specific questions before completing a standard presenting complaint exploration demonstrates confirmation bias regardless of whether the diagnosis is ultimately correct.

  • Demonstrate calibrated clinical uncertainty. When presenting a diagnosis to the examiner, explicitly acknowledge your differential and state what additional investigations or information would increase or decrease your confidence. Saying "My leading diagnosis is acute hepatitis B, however I would also like to exclude HIV seroconversion, malaria, and EBV infection with the following investigations..." demonstrates superior clinical reasoning compared to an unqualified diagnostic statement.

  • Recognise that the AMC deliberately modifies case findings within familiar stems. If you encounter a familiar clinical scenario and the findings do not conform to what you expected, this is not an error in the station — it is an intentional design feature intended to test your capacity to reason from evidence rather than from memory.

  • Overconfidence is demonstrably harmful to examination performance. The candidate who approaches a station certain they know the answer is measurably more likely to skip key steps, miss atypical features, and fail to demonstrate the systematic reasoning the examiner is marking. Approach each station as a clinical problem to be solved, not as a pattern to be recognised.

  • The quality of the reasoning process is marked, not merely the correctness of the conclusion. Two candidates who both arrive at a diagnosis of hepatitis B will receive different marks if one demonstrates systematic differential generation and explicit diagnostic verification, and the other demonstrates confirmation bias and premature closure.

  • Self-calibrate after every practice station. After each role-play or practice case, ask yourself: "Did I have a structured differential from the beginning? Did I ask questions that could exclude diagnoses, not only confirm them? Did I revise my working diagnosis when new information contradicted it? Did I demonstrate uncertainty proportionate to the information available?" These four questions directly map to the four cognitive biases described above.

Safety Net

For the patient (clinical safety netting — acute febrile illness in returning traveller):

Instruct the patient to return immediately to the emergency department or call 000 if any of the following develop before definitive results are available:

  • Sudden worsening of fever, particularly if accompanied by rigors, confusion, or extreme fatigue
  • Development of a non-blanching rash (rash that does not fade with pressure applied via a glass tumbler)
  • Neck stiffness, severe headache, or sensitivity to light
  • Shortness of breath or chest pain
  • Vomiting blood, black stools, or significant rectal bleeding
  • Deterioration in conscious state, confusion, or difficulty being roused
  • Inability to tolerate oral fluids for more than 12–24 hours

Follow-up timeline:
- Blood results: review within 24–48 hours or sooner if deterioration occurs — arrange for results to be reviewed by a clinician with the patient contacted directly
- If malaria films are negative but clinical suspicion remains high: repeat thick and thin films at 12 and 24 hours
- HIV result: if fourth-generation Ag/Ab test is negative but exposure is within the last 72 hours, offer HIV post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) and arrange repeat testing at 45 days post-exposure
- Hepatitis serology: follow-up with GP or infectious diseases specialist within one week if acute hepatitis confirmed

For the candidate (examination safety netting — cognitive bias prevention):

Before each AMC Clinical Examination station, commit to the following protocol:

  1. Generate a minimum of three differentials from the presenting complaint alone, before entering the room
  2. For each differential, identify one question that would exclude it
  3. After the physical examination, ask: "Do these findings fit my working diagnosis? If not, what new differential do they suggest?"
  4. Never announce a diagnosis to the examiner without explicitly acknowledging the differential and the next investigative steps
  5. If a station feels "too familiar," exercise heightened vigilance — the AMC may have modified the findings specifically to penalise candidates who rely on recall recognition rather than clinical reasoning
