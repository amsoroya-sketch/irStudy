# Why Candidates Fail the AMC Clinical Examination: Understanding Performance Deficits and Achieving Competence

## Clinical Presentation

The AMC Clinical Examination is a structured objective assessment of clinical competence designed to determine whether internationally trained medical graduates (IMGs) are ready to practise safely and independently in Australia. Unlike written examinations that test recall of factual knowledge, the clinical examination specifically assesses whether a candidate can *demonstrate* clinical reasoning, communication skill, and procedural competence in a simulated patient encounter. This distinction — between knowing and doing — is fundamental to understanding why many otherwise knowledgeable clinicians do not achieve a pass outcome.

The overall pass rate for the AMC Clinical Examination sits at approximately 25%, meaning that on any given sitting, three in four candidates do not meet the standard required for registration. This is not an incidental statistic; it reflects the deliberate design of an examination that requires synthesis, application, and authentic clinical engagement rather than information recitation. Candidates who approach the examination as an extension of their Part 1 preparation — studying lists, memorising scenarios, and rehearsing scripted responses — are systematically disadvantaged because the assessment framework specifically penalises these behaviours.

Understanding *why* candidates fail is not a secondary concern for exam preparation; it is central to it. The AMC has identified consistent, repeatable patterns of underperformance across its candidate cohort, and these patterns cluster around two distinct domains: knowledge application and clinical delivery. Addressing both domains with genuine structural preparation — rather than shortcut strategies — is the only pathway to reliable, sustainable success in this examination.

## Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

The AMC Clinical Examination uses structured mark sheets with predefined expectations for each station. Examiners are trained assessors — consultants, registrars, and general practitioners across Australia — who score candidates against specific key steps. The examination is deliberately designed to reduce subjectivity by providing examiners with defined, observable criteria. A candidate is awarded marks for demonstrating particular behaviours, not for arriving at a correct diagnosis alone.

Each station has a **predominant assessment area** — history taking, physical examination, or communication — and the mark allocation reflects this weighting. Critically, the predominant assessment area determines the core skill being evaluated, and candidates who reorient the station toward a different focus (for example, delivering a diagnosis monologue during a history-taking station) fail to demonstrate what is actually being assessed.

Examiners are specifically trained to identify and resist cognitive biases in their scoring; they are also trained to recognise when candidates are exhibiting biased clinical reasoning themselves. A candidate who prematurely closes a clinical encounter, who seeks only confirmatory information, or who performs a scripted response regardless of what the patient presents will not score at the level required for a pass, regardless of whether their final diagnosis is correct.

The framework the examiner applies can be summarised as follows:

- **Authentic engagement**: Is the candidate genuinely interacting with the patient, or performing a rehearsed routine?
- **Active listening**: Does the candidate respond to what the patient actually says, or proceed regardless?
- **Clinical reasoning**: Is the candidate synthesising information in real time, or recalling a predetermined answer?
- **Structured delivery**: Does the candidate demonstrate systematic, organised thinking within the time constraints?
- **Appropriate communication**: Does the candidate explain, negotiate, and share decisions clearly and in a patient-centred manner?

## Systematic Approach

### Domain 1 — The Knowledge-Application Gap

Clinical knowledge for the AMC examination is not simply a matter of factual volume. Many candidates possess extensive medical knowledge accumulated through years of practice and study. The specific requirement of the AMC examination is that this knowledge be *contextualised to Australian clinical practice* and *applied in real time to a patient presentation*, rather than recited in the abstract.

**What this means practically:**

- When a candidate enters a chest pain station, the examiner is not assessing whether the candidate knows that myocardial infarction is a cause of chest pain. The examiner is assessing whether the candidate can conduct a structured, patient-centred clinical interview that would allow a competent Australian clinician to appropriately evaluate chest pain in that patient.
- When a candidate enters a skin examination station, the examiner is assessing whether the candidate knows the relevant dermatological conditions in the Australian context — including conditions such as solar keratosis, melanoma, and squamous cell carcinoma — and can examine and describe findings accurately.

**The recall trap:**

The most common knowledge-domain failure is the substitution of case recall for clinical reasoning. Recall-based preparation involves studying previous or predicted examination cases, identifying the most likely diagnosis for each, and constructing a performance around confirming that diagnosis. This approach fails consistently because:

1. The candidate enters the station with a predetermined conclusion and applies a cognitive filter that prevents genuine information processing.
2. The candidate asks questions in a manner that telegraphs the expected answer, allowing the simulated patient to recognise that the candidate is not engaging authentically.
3. If the case contains atypical features — which AMC stations frequently do — the candidate fails to elicit or integrate them.
4. The candidate often reaches the "correct" diagnosis without having asked the key clinical questions needed to justify that diagnosis. The examiner marks against key steps, not against the diagnosis alone.

**Cognitive biases in clinical reasoning:**

Three specific cognitive errors are commonly observed in failing candidates:

- **Confirmation bias**: The candidate selects and weighs information that supports the suspected diagnosis while ignoring or discounting contradictory findings. Clinical questions are framed to confirm rather than to test. Rule-out differentials are not explored because the candidate is confident in their leading hypothesis.

- **Premature closure**: The clinical reasoning process is terminated before sufficient information has been gathered. The candidate concludes that the diagnosis is established and moves toward management before adequately characterising the presentation. This is particularly common when the diagnosis matches a recalled scenario.

- **Overconfidence bias**: The candidate's self-assessment of performance is substantially higher than the examiner's assessment. This manifests as surprise at a fail outcome, attribution of failure to external factors, and reluctance to identify personal performance deficits. Overconfidence bias significantly delays the candidate's ability to make effective changes to their preparation.

### Domain 2 — Clinical Delivery

Clinical delivery refers to the candidate's ability to perform the clinical encounter competently *on the day*, under examination conditions. A candidate may possess excellent clinical knowledge and still fail if that knowledge cannot be expressed through a structured, timed, patient-centred performance.

**Key delivery components assessed:**

**History-taking stations:**

- **Opening**: Introduce yourself, confirm patient identity, state your purpose. Failure to open correctly creates a poor first impression and disrupts the flow of the encounter.
- **Open-ended questioning**: Begin with a broad, open invitation — "Can you tell me what has been bringing you here today?" — and then *actively listen* to the response. The second open-ended question — "Is there anything else you wanted to tell me about that?" or "Can you tell me more about that?" — is a critical but frequently omitted step. The candidate's willingness to explore the patient's narrative before moving to closed questioning is directly observable and assessed.
- **Active listening**: The candidate must visibly respond to the patient's statements. This means reflecting content, acknowledging emotion, and adjusting the direction of questioning based on what has been heard. Interrupting the patient mid-response to proceed to the next question on a mental list is a clear fail behaviour.
- **Systematic progression**: The history should progress logically from the presenting complaint through its characterisation (onset, duration, severity, associated features, modifying factors, context), relevant past history, medications, allergies, family history, social history, and functional impact.
- **Running commentary in physical examination**: When performing physical examination, the candidate is expected to verbalise their actions and findings continuously. Silence during examination is penalised. Inaccurate technique is penalised. A formula recitation of findings without genuine clinical observation is penalised.

**Communication stations:**

- **ICE elicitation**: The candidate must actively explore the patient's Ideas (what they think is happening), Concerns (what worries them most), and Expectations (what they hope to achieve from this encounter). This is not a checklist item to be rushed; it forms the foundation of a patient-centred response.
- **Clarity in information giving**: If a diagnosis is being communicated, it must be stated clearly and in plain language. Candidates who hedge excessively, who bury the diagnosis in a list of differentials, or who are vague in an attempt to appear diplomatic will not pass communication stations. Patients require clarity, and the examiner is specifically assessing whether clarity has been achieved.
- **Shared decision making**: When management options are being discussed, the candidate must present options, explain risks and benefits in accessible language, check patient understanding, and incorporate patient preference into the plan. Unilateral decision-making without patient input fails this criterion.

**Time management:**

AMC clinical stations are strictly timed. The candidate must achieve all required key steps within the allocated time. Poor time management — spending excessive time on one component and failing to reach others — is a structural failure of delivery, not a knowledge failure. This requires deliberate practice with timed scenarios, not just theoretical knowledge of what should be covered.

### Domain 3 — Examination Performance in High-Difficulty Scenario Types

Certain station types consistently produce higher rates of candidate underperformance. These require specific preparation focus:

**Mental health stations:**

Mental health stations are frequently failed because candidates apply a biomedical model to a presentation that requires a biopsychosocial and communication-centred approach. Common errors include:

- Beginning the mental health history with a closed symptom checklist rather than open exploration of the patient's experience
- Performing a mental state examination as a memorised list of observations rather than as genuine clinical observation of the patient in front of them
- Failing to explore suicidality with appropriate directness and sensitivity
- Missing the emotional content of the presentation and proceeding directly to diagnosis and management

**Women's health stations:**

These stations require familiarity with Australian guidelines for antenatal care, cervical screening, contraception, and menstrual disorders. Candidates who have practised in health systems with different screening protocols or different contraceptive guidelines must specifically update their knowledge to the current Australian framework.

**Prescribing stations:**

These require knowledge of Australian Therapeutic Guidelines, PBS-listed medications, and safe prescribing in the Australian context. Candidates must know dose ranges, contraindications, and monitoring requirements for commonly prescribed medications.

**Interprofessional communication stations:**

Stations involving communication with colleagues, handover scenarios, or referral situations require candidates to demonstrate professional communication frameworks (such as ISBAR — Identification, Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) and to navigate professional roles appropriately.

## Differential Diagnosis

For AMC Clinical Examination preparation, the relevant "differential" is the differential of *performance failure modes* — the categories of deficit that prevent a candidate from passing:

| Category | Failure Mode | Key Distinguishing Features |
|----------|-------------|----------------------------|
| Common | Recall-based preparation | Tunnel vision toward predetermined diagnosis; failure to explore atypical features; premature closure |
| Common | Robotic performance | Scripted question sequences without patient responsiveness; no active listening; fixed interview regardless of patient responses |
| Common | Insufficient Australian contextualisation | Knowledge base reflects different health system; unfamiliar with Australian guidelines, screening programs, or PBS medications |
| Common | Time management failure | Key steps not reached within allocated time; over-investment in history at expense of management or communication |
| Important | Formula responses in mental state examination | Memorised MSE findings applied regardless of observed patient; no genuine clinical observation |
| Important | Lack of running commentary in physical examination | Silent examination technique; findings not verbalised; technique inaccurate or incomplete |
| Important | ICE not elicited in communication stations | Patient's perspective not explored; management plan not negotiated; patient-centred criteria not met |
| Must Not Miss | Overconfidence bias without self-reflection | Candidate unable to identify performance deficits; external attribution of failure; no adjustment to preparation strategy |
| Must Not Miss | Premature closure of clinical reasoning | Diagnosis accepted before key questions asked; mark scheme criteria for history not met even when diagnosis is correct |

## Red Flags — Must Not Miss

The following performance patterns are reliably associated with examination failure and must be identified and corrected during preparation:

- **Interrupting the patient during their response** — This is directly observable as a failure of active listening. The examiner will note that the candidate did not allow the patient to complete their narrative. Address by practising extended silence and follow-up exploration before moving to structured questions.

- **Asking leading questions that reveal the diagnosis** — For example, asking "Do you have pain that gets worse when you lean forward?" before eliciting any chest pain history signals that the candidate already knows the diagnosis. The examiner recognises this as recall-based performance and does not award marks for clinical reasoning.

- **Failing to respond to emotional cues** — When the simulated patient expresses distress, fear, or anger, the candidate is expected to acknowledge and address this before continuing with clinical information gathering. Candidates who proceed with history questions after an emotional disclosure without acknowledging it will fail communication criteria.

- **Memorised MSE without genuine observation** — Reciting a standard list of mental state findings without describing what is actually observable in the patient in front of you is identifiable to trained examiners and is not awarded marks.

- **Vague or unclear diagnosis disclosure** — Using euphemisms, overly technical language, or excessive qualification when communicating a diagnosis to a patient fails the clarity criterion of communication stations.

- **Management without patient involvement** — Providing a management plan without asking the patient's views, checking their understanding, or addressing their concerns fails the shared decision-making criterion.

- **No safety netting** — Concluding a station without addressing what the patient should do if symptoms worsen, when to seek urgent care, and what the follow-up plan is represents an incomplete clinical encounter and will not achieve full marks on relevant stations.

## Investigations

Investigation planning for AMC examination preparation should be oriented around the following framework:

**Bedside:** Appropriate vital signs, ECG, urinalysis, blood glucose, peak flow measurement, pulse oximetry — candidates must know which bedside investigations are relevant to each presenting complaint and must be able to justify their selection.

**Bloods:** Full blood count, electrolytes, renal function, liver function, thyroid function, inflammatory markers, coagulation studies, pregnancy test — must be requested in a clinically justified manner, not as a blanket panel.

**Imaging:** Plain radiography, ultrasound, CT — candidates must know the appropriate first-line imaging for common presentations and must be able to explain the clinical rationale.

**Specialist referral:** Knowledge of when to refer, to which specialty, and with what degree of urgency is assessed in both history and communication stations. Candidates must be familiar with the Australian referral pathway framework and the role of general practice as the primary coordinator of care.

## Management

Effective management responses in the AMC Clinical Examination require:

**Immediate management:**
- Address acute safety concerns first (airway, breathing, circulation in emergency presentations; immediate psychiatric risk in mental health presentations)
- Establish rapport and therapeutic alliance before proceeding to clinical tasks in all station types

**Specific management:**
- Provide management that is specific to the individual patient's presentation, not generic. "Manage the symptoms" is not an acceptable management statement. "Commence a trial of low-dose aspirin 100mg daily, review in six weeks, and arrange a cardiology referral if symptoms persist" is specific.
- Reference current Australian guidelines (RACGP, NHFA, RACGP Red Book, Therapeutic Guidelines) where relevant
- Incorporate non-pharmacological management alongside pharmacological

**Ongoing management:**
- Address lifestyle modification, allied health involvement, and patient education
- Establish a clear follow-up plan with specific timeframes
- Identify the responsible clinician for ongoing care coordination

## Communication and Patient Education

Effective patient communication in the AMC Clinical Examination context requires mastery of the following:

**Explaining the diagnosis:**
State the diagnosis clearly, in plain language, at an appropriate pace. Pause after delivering the diagnosis to allow the patient to process the information. Do not proceed immediately to management. Check whether the patient has understood — "Is that making sense so far?" — before continuing.

**Addressing patient concerns:**
Having elicited the patient's concerns through ICE, the candidate must *return to them* when structuring the response. If the patient expressed fear about a particular outcome, that fear must be directly acknowledged and addressed. Providing generic information without addressing the specific concern expressed is insufficient.

**Health literacy:**
Assume an average Australian health literacy level unless there are cues to adjust upward or downward. Avoid medical jargon. If technical terms are unavoidable, explain them in plain language. Use the teach-back method — "I want to make sure I've explained that clearly — could you tell me in your own words what we've discussed?" — to confirm understanding.

**Cultural sensitivity:**
In encounters with patients from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds, be alert to language barriers (offer interpreter services), different health beliefs, family-centred decision-making structures, and varying levels of familiarity with the Australian health system. Never assume; explore.

**Written resources:**
Offer relevant written information (fact sheets, referral letters, medication information) where clinically appropriate. This demonstrates comprehensive care planning.

## AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

- **Treat every simulated patient as a real patient.** The examination is specifically designed to assess authentic clinical engagement. Examiners are trained to distinguish genuine interaction from scripted performance. The single most reliable predictor of a pass outcome is whether the candidate is truly responding to the person in front of them.

- **Never enter a station with a predetermined diagnosis.** Begin every station by listening. The opening statement from the patient contains critical information. Candidates who have already decided the diagnosis before hearing the patient are consistently failed for premature closure and confirmation bias.

- **Ask a second open-ended question before proceeding to closed questions.** "Can you tell me more about that?" or "What happened next?" after the initial opening response dramatically improves the quality of information elicited and demonstrates active listening to the examiner.

- **Acknowledge emotion before continuing with clinical tasks.** Any expression of distress, worry, or anger from the patient is an invitation to demonstrate empathic communication. Stop, acknowledge, validate, then proceed. Candidates who skip this step in the pursuit of completing their question list are penalised on communication criteria.

- **Know the Australian clinical context, not just the medicine.** Preparation must include current RACGP guidelines, PBS medication listings, Medicare item numbers for relevant investigations, the Australian immunisation schedule, cervical screening protocols, and bowel cancer screening program. This contextualisation is non-negotiable.

- **Practice physical examination with running commentary aloud, every time.** Silent examination is failed examination. The running commentary must be fluent, accurate, and demonstrate that the candidate is observing and interpreting findings in real time, not reciting a memorised sequence.

- **Know your mark scheme structure.** Each AMC station has key steps. The candidate should understand that marks are awarded for *demonstrated behaviours*, not for the correctness of the diagnosis alone. A candidate who reaches the correct diagnosis without demonstrating the steps that justify it will not receive full marks.

- **Complete the station, including safety netting.** Many candidates spend too long on history or examination and do not reach the management and safety-netting components. These components carry marks. Practice timed delivery to ensure the full station can be completed within the allocated time.

- **Do not argue with the examiner or simulated patient.** If the simulated patient presents unexpected findings or resists the expected clinical progression, adapt. The ability to manage an unexpected clinical presentation is itself an assessed competency.

- **Structures, not recalls.** A systematic clinical structure — applied consistently to *any* presentation — will outperform a collection of recalled scenarios in every situation. Recalls are finite; clinical structures are generalisable. The examination is specifically designed to reward structural thinking and penalise recall-based performance.

- **Self-assess honestly.** Overconfidence bias is a genuine barrier to improvement. After each practice encounter, solicit specific, critical feedback. Identify the exact steps that were missed, not just the overall impression. The ability to recognise personal performance deficits is the first requirement for correcting them.

- **Mental health and women's health stations require specific preparation.** These are the most commonly failed station categories. Approach mental health stations with a biopsychosocial model and genuine observational skills. Approach women's health stations with current Australian screening and management guidelines.

## Safety Net

The equivalent of "safety netting" in the context of AMC examination preparation relates to the preparation plan itself:

**When to seek structured preparation:**
- Any candidate who has attempted the AMC Clinical Examination without a structured preparation program and did not pass should seek a formal, structured preparation program before their next attempt.
- Any candidate who has not practised with a trained assessor providing specific, criterion-referenced feedback should arrange this before sitting.

**Indicators that preparation approach requires adjustment:**
- Relying primarily on notes, recalls, or past case collections as the main preparation strategy
- Being unable to complete a practice station within the allocated time
- Receiving feedback that performance feels "robotic" or "scripted"
- Being unable to identify personal performance deficits when asked

**Follow-up plan:**
- Practice with deliberate focus on identified weaknesses, not indiscriminate repetition of all scenarios
- Seek examiner-level feedback — from someone trained to assess against AMC criteria — not general peer feedback
- Ensure preparation includes all station types, including high-difficulty categories (mental health, women's health, prescribing, interprofessional communication)
- Allow adequate preparation time. The examination requires *competence* in clinical performance; competence is not achievable through information acquisition alone. Skill development requires practice with feedback, which requires time.
