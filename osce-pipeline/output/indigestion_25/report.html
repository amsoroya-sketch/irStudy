Dyspepsia and Upper Gastrointestinal Malignancy — AMC Clinical Examination Station

Clinical Presentation

A 50-year-old patient presents with a six-month history of indigestion, a deceptively vague complaint that demands systematic clinical unpacking. The term "indigestion" is patient-generated and non-specific — it may represent gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD), peptic ulcer disease, gastric malignancy, chronic pancreatitis, biliary colic, or even ischaemic heart disease. The clinician's first obligation is to characterise precisely what the patient means by this term before any diagnostic framework is applied.

This case is archetypal for the AMC Clinical Examination in its design: a chronic, vague upper gastrointestinal complaint in a middle-aged patient that conceals significant red flag pathology. The history reveals progressive burning epigastric discomfort worse after eating, initial response to antacids that has subsequently waned, unintentional weight loss, and — critically — melaena (dark stools). This constellation of findings demands that upper gastrointestinal malignancy, specifically gastric carcinoma, be held at the top of the differential until excluded by endoscopy and biopsy.

Clinically, this station tests the candidate's ability to manage a four-task case under time pressure: history taking, physical examination, diagnosis and differentials, and investigations. Time discipline is as important as clinical knowledge. A candidate who delivers an exemplary history but fails to reach the investigation task cannot pass. The station rewards candidates who think in diagnostic hierarchies, screen for red flags efficiently, and communicate a probable cancer diagnosis with both clarity and compassion.

Exam Sequence

Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

This is a four-task case requiring structured time allocation across:

  1. History taking (approximately four minutes for the opening prompt, then time-managed across remaining tasks)
  2. Directed physical examination — requested from the examiner and interpreted in context
  3. Diagnostic formulation — provisional diagnosis with reasoned differentials
  4. Investigations — prioritised and explained to the patient

The examiner expects:

  • An empathic opening that acknowledges the chronic nature of the complaint and reassures the patient
  • Precise clarification of the vague term "indigestion" before applying any diagnostic template
  • Systematic red flag screening integrated naturally into the consultation — not tacked on at the end
  • A physical examination approach that is complete for the core system (abdomen) and appropriately curtailed for supporting systems (cardiovascular, lymphatic)
  • A diagnostic discussion that names gastric malignancy explicitly but sensitively, without unwarranted alarm
  • Endoscopy with biopsy as the unambiguous first investigation

The examiner will use a structured mark sheet. Marks are allocated to the sensitive discussion of cancer, explicit red flag identification, appropriate investigation selection, and complete abdominal examination. Omitting endoscopy as the first investigation is a critical failure point.

Systematic Approach

Step 1 — Clarify the Presenting Complaint

The single most important first move in this consultation is to ask the patient to describe what they mean by indigestion in their own words. This is not a courtesy — it is a clinical necessity that determines the entire diagnostic pathway.

Opening clarification question:
"Before we go further, could you describe in your own words what you mean by indigestion? For example, is it more of a bloating or fullness, a discomfort or pain, or more of a burning or heartburn sensation?"

The patient in this case describes burning epigastric discomfort. This immediately transforms the complaint into a characterisable pain — and the standard pain exploration framework (equivalent to SOCRATES) becomes applicable.

Clarified complaint characteristics:
- Site: Upper abdomen (epigastric)
- Onset: Six months, gradual onset, first episode
- Character: Burning discomfort (not sharp, not colicky)
- Radiation: None (absence of radiation to back or shoulder is noted)
- Associated features: Dark stools, weight loss, reduced appetite
- Timing: Continuous with postprandial exacerbation; worse over the six-month period
- Exacerbating factors: Food (immediate onset after eating), coffee, spicy food, alcohol
- Relieving factors: Antacids (Mylanta) — initially effective, now no longer providing relief
- Severity: 3–4 out of 10

The loss of antacid efficacy over time is a clinically important observation. Simple GORD and uncomplicated peptic ulcer disease typically continue to respond to antacids and acid suppression. Progressive loss of relief, combined with worsening symptoms, raises the clinical index of suspicion for underlying structural pathology, including malignancy.

Step 2 — Systematic History Framework for Dyspepsia

Rather than applying the SOCRATES template rigidly to a non-pain complaint, the clinician should think in clinical information domains:

Domain 1 — Character and Description
Establish whether the complaint is pain, discomfort, bloating, regurgitation, heartburn, or nausea. Each descriptor points toward a different diagnostic cluster. A burning epigastric discomfort post-prandially is the language of peptic ulcer disease and GORD; a dull constant ache radiating to the back suggests pancreatic pathology; colicky right upper quadrant pain after fatty meals suggests biliary disease.

Domain 2 — Temporal Profile
Six months duration establishes chronicity. Determine whether onset was acute or gradual. Sudden onset of dyspepsia in a person over 40 years of age, with no prior history, is a red flag for malignancy until proven otherwise. Establish whether the complaint is continuous or episodic, and the trajectory (improving, stable, worsening).

Domain 3 — Triggers and Modifying Factors
- Relation to food: onset, specific foods (fatty, spicy, dairy)
- Postural factors (lying flat — GORD)
- Medications (NSAIDs, aspirin, corticosteroids, bisphosphonates, iron)
- Alcohol and smoking
- Caffeine and carbonated drinks

Domain 4 — General Gastrointestinal Screen
Nausea, vomiting, haematemesis, early satiety, bloating, change in bowel habit, rectal bleeding, melaena, mucus in stool, tenesmus.

Domain 5 — Red Flag Screen (Prioritised)
In a 50-year-old with chronic progressive dyspepsia, this is non-negotiable:
- Unintentional weight loss (how much? over what period? confirmed non-intentional?)
- Change in appetite (anorexia, early satiety)
- Dysphagia (oesophageal pathology; progressive dysphagia — first to solids, then to liquids)
- Haematemesis or melaena
- Nocturnal symptoms (waking from sleep)
- Fatigue and pallor (occult bleeding)

Domain 6 — Supporting System Screen
Briefly screen for ischaemic heart disease: exertional chest pain, shortness of breath, diaphoresis, radiation of discomfort to jaw or left arm. In a 50-year-old with a vague upper abdominal complaint and cardiovascular risk factors, an atypical anginal equivalent must not be overlooked.

Domain 7 — Risk Factor Profile
Smoking status, alcohol quantity and duration, NSAID or aspirin use, Helicobacter pylori history or prior treatment, family history of upper gastrointestinal malignancy, previous peptic ulcer disease, prior surgery.

Domain 8 — Social Context and Impact
Duration of work disability, dietary habits (high takeaway food consumption, irregular meals), stress (a recognised contributor to peptic ulcer relapse), and how the problem is affecting daily functioning and quality of life.

Step 3 — Physical Examination Framework

When asked to direct physical examination from the examiner, the candidate must clearly articulate:
1. The core examination (abdominal)
2. Relevant supplementary examinations (cardiovascular, lymph node)
3. The specific peripheral signs most relevant to the clinical context

General Appearance and Vital Signs

Begin by observing the patient's general state. In the context of suspected malignancy with occult bleeding, the following must be specifically noted:

  • Pallor — conjunctival and palmar pallor (iron deficiency anaemia from chronic gastrointestinal bleeding)
  • Cachexia and muscle wasting — weight loss, temporal wasting, loss of thenar eminence bulk
  • Jaundice — scleral icterus (hepatic or pancreatic involvement, biliary obstruction)
  • Lymphadenopathy — particularly the left supraclavicular fossa (see below)

Vital signs: blood pressure, pulse rate, respiratory rate, temperature. In the context of occult bleeding, tachycardia and hypotension may be present. Pyrexia is unusual in upper GI malignancy but may occur with superimposed infection or abscess formation.

Lymph Node Examination

Virchow's node — a palpable left supraclavicular lymph node (also called Troisier's sign) — represents metastatic spread of an intra-abdominal malignancy via the thoracic duct to the left supraclavicular fossa. It is a well-established clinical sign of advanced upper gastrointestinal or intra-abdominal malignancy, particularly gastric carcinoma. Examination of this node should be explicitly requested in any patient with suspected upper gastrointestinal malignancy. A positive finding substantially elevates the index of suspicion for gastric cancer with nodal metastasis.

Examine all nodal stations: cervical, supraclavicular, axillary, and inguinal — but left supraclavicular is the highest yield in this context.

Core Examination: Abdominal Examination

Perform a complete and systematic abdominal examination without abbreviation:

Inspection:
- Abdominal contour: flat, scaphoid, distended
- Symmetrical movement with respiration
- Visible peristalsis (suggests gastric outlet obstruction)
- Skin: jaundice, spider naevi, caput medusae, striae, surgical scars
- Hernial orifices at rest
- Visible masses

Palpation (superficial then deep):
- Superficial palpation across all nine regions — assess for tenderness, guarding, masses
- Deep palpation — specifically for epigastric tenderness and masses
- Hepatomegaly: liver enlarged or nodular (hepatic metastases)
- Splenomegaly
- Palpable epigastric mass (advanced gastric tumour)
- Palpable umbilical nodule — Sister Mary Joseph's nodule (peritoneal metastasis via umbilical vein)

Percussion:
- Liver span
- Shifting dullness and fluid thrill (ascites — malignant peritoneal disease)
- Tympany over gastric bubble

Auscultation:
- Bowel sounds: normal, high-pitched (obstruction), absent (ileus)
- Succussion splash in suspected gastric outlet obstruction

Inguinal examination and groins:
- Lymph node assessment
- Hernial orifices

Digital Rectal Examination:
Melaena (dark, tarry stool) is a stated finding in this case. Digital rectal examination confirms the presence of dark stool on the examining glove and may reveal a rectal mass. In this clinical context, melaena is confirmatory of upper gastrointestinal bleeding (proximal to the ligament of Treitz) and must be sought. Blumer's shelf — a palpable mass in the rectovesical or rectouterine pouch from peritoneal metastasis — may be identified on rectal examination in advanced disease.

Cardiovascular Examination

A brief but structured cardiovascular screen is appropriate:
- Heart rate and rhythm
- Auscultation for murmurs (particularly aortic stenosis — abdominal angina can mimic GI pain)
- Peripheral pulses
- Signs of cardiac failure

This is appropriately abbreviated given the clinical priority is upper gastrointestinal.

Step 4 — Diagnostic Formulation

The candidate must construct a diagnostic formulation that integrates history, examination findings, and risk stratification.

Positive findings that drive the formulation:
1. Fifty years of age — above the threshold at which new-onset dyspepsia mandates investigation
2. Six months of progressive burning epigastric discomfort
3. Initial antacid response with subsequent loss of efficacy
4. Unintentional weight loss
5. Anorexia
6. Melaena (dark stools) — upper gastrointestinal bleeding
7. Pallor on examination — anaemia from occult bleeding
8. Positive left supraclavicular lymph node on examination

Diagnostic formulation statement (for communication to examiner and patient):

"This 50-year-old patient presents with a six-month history of progressive epigastric burning discomfort, failure to respond to antacid therapy, unintentional weight loss, and melaena. Physical examination reveals pallor and a left supraclavicular lymph node. The provisional diagnosis is gastric carcinoma, with peptic ulcer disease as a concurrent or contributory pathology. This requires urgent upper gastrointestinal endoscopy with biopsy."

Red Flags — Must Not Miss

  • Unintentional weight loss — loss of more than 3–5 kg without dietary change or deliberate effort is a red flag for malignancy in any patient. Quantify the loss (kilograms over what period) and confirm it is unintentional. In this case, confirmed weight loss in the context of progressive dyspepsia mandates urgent investigation.

  • Melaena (dark, tarry stools) — indicates upper gastrointestinal bleeding proximal to the ligament of Treitz. The black colour results from haemoglobin digestion by gastric acid and intestinal bacteria. Melaena in a patient with progressive dyspepsia suggests bleeding from a peptic ulcer or ulcerating tumour. It demands urgent endoscopy.

  • Anorexia — loss of appetite in the context of progressive dyspepsia and weight loss completes the "triple loss" (weight, appetite, colour) that is strongly associated with upper gastrointestinal malignancy.

  • Pallor — suggests iron deficiency anaemia from chronic occult gastrointestinal blood loss. May indicate significant ongoing bleeding or longstanding nutritional compromise from malignancy.

  • Virchow's node (left supraclavicular lymphadenopathy) — a palpable, firm, non-tender left supraclavicular lymph node represents nodal metastasis from an intra-abdominal malignancy. Its presence in this clinical context strongly supports advanced gastric carcinoma and requires urgent oncological workup.

  • Progressive dysphagia — difficulty swallowing that worsens over weeks to months, beginning with solids then progressing to liquids, is a red flag for oesophageal or gastro-oesophageal junction carcinoma. Ask explicitly about swallowing difficulties in all patients with upper GI complaints.

  • Failure of antacid or PPI therapy — dyspepsia that initially responds to antacids but subsequently fails to respond to adequate proton pump inhibitor therapy is clinically concerning. Structural pathology — including malignancy — does not respond to acid suppression.

  • Age over 45 years with new-onset dyspepsia — Australian and international guidelines (aligned with evidence-based practice) advocate for endoscopic investigation of new-onset dyspepsia in patients over 45 years in the absence of a clear benign aetiology, and urgently so when red flags are present.

  • Nocturnal symptoms — dyspepsia that wakes the patient from sleep suggests an organic rather than functional aetiology and should prompt investigation.

  • Family history of upper gastrointestinal malignancy — a first-degree relative with gastric cancer significantly elevates personal risk, particularly in the context of H. pylori infection or dietary risk factors.

Investigations Grid
Clinically Enriched

Investigations

Bedside:
- Digital rectal examination — confirm presence and character of melaena
- Faecal occult blood testing (FOBT) is NOT indicated here; melaena is clinically apparent and FOBT is a screening tool for asymptomatic populations
- Urine dipstick (incidental finding screen)
- ECG — if cardiovascular differential remains active (rule out atypical angina)

Bloods:
- Full blood count (FBC) — iron deficiency anaemia pattern (low haemoglobin, low MCV, low ferritin); thrombocytosis may be seen in malignancy
- Iron studies — serum iron, ferritin, transferrin saturation (confirm iron deficiency from chronic blood loss)
- Liver function tests — alkaline phosphatase, GGT, bilirubin (hepatic or biliary involvement; hepatic metastases)
- Urea and electrolytes — blood urea elevated disproportionately to creatinine in upper GI bleeding (urea generated by bacterial digestion of haemoglobin in the gut)
- Coagulation profile (INR, aPTT) — prior to endoscopy, particularly if therapeutic intervention anticipated
- CA 19-9, CEA — tumour markers; limited sensitivity and specificity but may contribute to staging workup
- Lipase / amylase — if pancreatic pathology remains a differential
- Blood group and screen — if significant haemorrhage is suspected

Imaging:
- CT abdomen and pelvis with intravenous contrast — staging in confirmed or strongly suspected malignancy (assess tumour extent, nodal involvement, peritoneal disease, hepatic metastases, vascular involvement)
- CT chest — pulmonary metastasis screen in confirmed malignancy
- Abdominal ultrasound — if biliary pathology remains a differential; limited utility for gastric lesions but may identify hepatic metastases or ascites

Specialist — First and Critical Investigation:

Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (OGD — oesophago-gastro-duodenoscopy) with biopsy — this is the single most important investigation and must be named first. It allows direct visualisation of the oesophageal, gastric, and duodenal mucosa; identification and characterisation of ulcers, masses, and mucosal abnormalities; biopsy of any suspicious lesion (minimum six biopsies from the margins and base of any gastric ulcer to maximise sensitivity for malignancy); and H. pylori testing via rapid urease test (CLO test) or histology on biopsy samples. In Australia, this investigation would be arranged urgently (within two weeks) in any patient with red flag symptoms.

Additional specialist investigations:
- Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) — if OGD confirms a lesion, EUS provides superior local T and N staging compared to CT, particularly for depth of invasion through the gastric wall
- PET scan — for staging in confirmed gastric malignancy, particularly prior to surgical planning
- Colonoscopy — not the first priority in this case, but indicated if coexisting lower GI pathology is suspected or if a synchronous colorectal malignancy needs exclusion in the context of a new gastrointestinal cancer diagnosis
- H. pylori serology or urea breath test — if endoscopy is not immediately available; however, endoscopy with CLO test is preferred as it provides both diagnostic and H. pylori testing simultaneously

Management

Immediate priorities:

  • Assess haemodynamic stability. If melaena is associated with haemodynamic compromise (tachycardia, hypotension, pallor, postural drop), this becomes an acute upper gastrointestinal haemorrhage requiring urgent hospital admission, resuscitation with intravenous access and fluid, blood transfusion if haemoglobin is critically low, and emergent endoscopy.
  • Cease all NSAIDs, aspirin (unless cardioprotective dose is mandatory and risk-benefit justifies continuation), and alcohol.
  • If haemodynamically stable: arrange urgent outpatient upper GI endoscopy; refer to gastroenterology.

Medical management:

  • Proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy (e.g., pantoprazole 40 mg daily or esomeprazole 40 mg daily) commenced to reduce acid-related injury to the gastric mucosa; does not treat underlying malignancy but reduces mucosal damage and supports ulcer healing
  • H. pylori eradication therapy if testing confirms infection: standard first-line regimen in Australia is triple therapy — PPI twice daily + clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily + amoxicillin 1 g twice daily for 14 days (or metronidazole 400 mg twice daily in penicillin allergy)
  • Nutritional support and dietary modification (small, frequent meals; avoidance of spicy food, alcohol, and caffeine)
  • Antiemetics as required for symptom control

Oncological management (if gastric malignancy confirmed):

  • Multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting: gastroenterologist, upper GI surgeon, medical oncologist, radiation oncologist, radiologist, pathologist, dietitian, palliative care
  • Staging workup to guide treatment intent (curative vs. palliative)
  • Early-stage gastric cancer: surgical resection (subtotal or total gastrectomy with D2 lymph node dissection) with perioperative chemotherapy (FLOT regimen — fluorouracil, leucovorin, oxaliplatin, docetaxel — as per Australian oncology guidelines)
  • Advanced or metastatic disease: systemic chemotherapy; trastuzumab in HER2-positive tumours; palliative intent in metastatic disease with attention to symptom control, nutritional status, and quality of life

Communication and Patient Education

Breaking the news of a probable cancer diagnosis:

This is a sensitive consultation requiring a structured approach. The examiner marks candidates on their ability to communicate a probable malignancy clearly, compassionately, and without causing unnecessary alarm, while being honest and transparent.

Key communication principles:

  1. Acknowledge and summarise what has been discussed: "Based on our conversation today, I want to share my thoughts with you about what might be causing these symptoms."

  2. Signal before delivering concerning information (warning shot): "I want to be honest with you, and some of what I'm going to say may be a little concerning. Is that okay?"

  3. Name the concern explicitly but with appropriate qualification: "I am concerned about the possibility of a cancer in the stomach. I want to be clear — I cannot confirm that is what this is, and that is exactly why we need to do further tests. But I would not be doing my job if I didn't tell you this is on my list of concerns."

  4. Provide the clinical reasoning: "The reason I am thinking about this is that you have had indigestion for six months that is getting progressively worse despite antacid treatment. You have lost weight without trying to, your appetite has changed, and you have had dark stools, which tells me there may be some bleeding happening in your stomach. These things together are what concern me."

  5. Reassure about next steps: "We are not going to leave things here. The most important step right now is to arrange an endoscopy — a test where we pass a thin flexible tube with a camera down into your stomach — so we can see exactly what is happening and take samples if needed. That will give us a clear answer."

  6. Invite questions and check understanding: "I know this is a lot to take in. What questions do you have for me? Is there anything you would like me to go over again?"

  7. Acknowledge emotional response: "It is completely understandable to feel worried about this. I want you to know that you have done the right thing by coming in, and we are going to work through this together."

What patients commonly ask:
- "Does this mean I have cancer?" — Explain that you have concerns but cannot confirm without the endoscopy results; the test will provide clarity
- "How serious is this?" — Acknowledge the seriousness of the concern while explaining that the stage of any tumour determines treatment options, and that is what the investigations will determine
- "What happens if it is cancer?" — Briefly outline that treatment depends on what is found and will involve a team of specialists

AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

  • Clarify vague complaints before applying any diagnostic template. In the AMC Clinical Examination, a patient who presents with "indigestion," "tiredness," "dizziness," or any non-specific symptom must be asked to describe precisely what they mean before proceeding. Failure to do this represents a fundamental history-taking error.

  • Endoscopy must be your first investigation — always. In any case of upper gastrointestinal pathology with red flags, the examiner expects to hear "upper gastrointestinal endoscopy with biopsy" as the first and non-negotiable investigation. Do not open with blood tests, imaging, or breath tests. Failure to prioritise endoscopy is a critical mark loss.

  • Name gastric malignancy explicitly in the communication task. The examiner marks for a sensitive but direct discussion of malignancy as the leading concern. Euphemisms ("something unusual," "an abnormality") without naming cancer are insufficient. Use the word "cancer" with appropriate qualification and compassion.

  • Virchow's node is the high-yield physical sign in this case. Always request examination of the left supraclavicular fossa in any patient with suspected upper gastrointestinal malignancy. A positive finding in the exam station strongly supports the provisional diagnosis of gastric carcinoma and demonstrates senior clinical thinking.

  • Melaena demands upper GI bleeding as your explanation, not lower. Dark, tarry, malodorous stool (melaena) indicates digested blood from a source proximal to the ligament of Treitz. It is clinically distinct from bright rectal bleeding (haematochezia). This distinction is essential to state confidently.

  • Manage your time ruthlessly in multi-task stations. If a station has four tasks (history, examination, diagnosis, investigations), allocate your time accordingly. An incomplete consultation that never reaches the investigation task cannot pass, regardless of the quality of the history. Prioritise completing all tasks over perfecting any single task.

  • The "triple loss" is a clinical alarm signal. Weight loss + loss of appetite + pallor (loss of colour) in a patient over 40 with new or progressive dyspepsia represents gastric malignancy until proven otherwise. Recognise and name this pattern explicitly.

  • Do not omit the cardiovascular differential in upper abdominal pain. Ischaemic heart disease can present with vague epigastric discomfort, particularly in diabetic patients with autonomic neuropathy or in women with atypical presentations. A brief screen for cardiac symptoms (exertional chest pain, dyspnoea) is mandatory in any patient over 45 with upper abdominal complaints.

  • Document melaena and request digital rectal examination. In a patient who reports dark stools, always request rectal examination to confirm melaena and to complete the abdominal examination properly. Omitting this in the context of gastrointestinal bleeding is an examination error.

  • Peptic ulcer disease and gastric cancer are not mutually exclusive. In this case, the history of initial antacid response suggests an underlying peptic ulcer that has subsequently undergone malignant transformation or been complicated by a superimposed cancer. Both diagnoses can and do coexist. The candidate who recognises this demonstrates sophisticated clinical reasoning.

  • Never perform FOBT in a patient with clinically evident melaena. Faecal occult blood testing is a population-level screening tool for asymptomatic individuals. Ordering it in a patient with overt gastrointestinal bleeding demonstrates a misunderstanding of investigation purpose and will not impress the examiner.

  • Red flag dyspepsia in a patient over 45 requires direct-access endoscopy — do not trial empirical PPI therapy first. While empirical PPI therapy is appropriate for functional dyspepsia or uncomplicated GORD in younger patients without red flags, it must not delay endoscopy in any patient with alarm features. Commencing PPI without arranging endoscopy in this patient would represent a management error.

Safety Net

Provide the patient with specific, actionable guidance about symptoms that require immediate or urgent medical attention before the scheduled endoscopy. These instructions should be given verbally and reinforced in writing.

Return immediately to the emergency department if any of the following occur:

  • Vomiting blood (haematemesis) — fresh red blood or coffee-ground vomitus
  • A significant increase in the volume or frequency of dark, tarry stools
  • Feeling faint, dizzy, or lightheaded (particularly on standing) — suggests acute haemorrhage with haemodynamic compromise
  • Severe or sudden worsening of abdominal pain
  • Fever with abdominal pain
  • Loss of consciousness

Return to your GP urgently (within 24–48 hours) if:

  • Symptoms significantly worsen before the endoscopy appointment
  • New symptoms develop, including dysphagia or increasing vomiting
  • You experience rapid or unexplained further weight loss

Scheduled follow-up:

  • Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy arranged urgently (within two weeks for red flag presentations; sooner if haemodynamically unstable)
  • Return appointment with GP or gastroenterologist to discuss endoscopy results
  • Blood test results (FBC, iron studies, LFTs) to be reviewed at the follow-up appointment

Patient information:
- Advise to avoid NSAIDs, aspirin, alcohol, and smoking until the diagnosis is established
- Provide written information about upper gastrointestinal endoscopy procedure (patient information leaflets available from the Gastroenterological Society of Australia — GESA)
- Advise the patient to bring a support person to the endoscopy appointment if possible
- Ensure the patient has a clear point of contact (GP practice phone number) and knows not to wait if symptoms worsen acutely
