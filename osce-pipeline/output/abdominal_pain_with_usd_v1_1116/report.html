Abdominal Pain with Gallstones on Ultrasound — Avoiding the Diagnostic Trap

Clinical Presentation

A 40-year-old male presents to a general practice clinic with ongoing epigastric abdominal pain. An ultrasound of the abdomen has been performed prior to the consultation and demonstrates gallstones within the gallbladder. Critically, the ultrasound report contains no features of acute cholecystitis — there is no gallbladder wall thickening, no pericholecystic fluid, no sonographic Murphy's sign, and no distension of the gallbladder. This is not an incidental finding to be dismissed; rather, it is a deliberate piece of clinical information provided to test the candidate's capacity for systematic reasoning under diagnostic pressure.

This station is an archetypal AMC Clinical Examination history-taking scenario because it presents a diagnostic decoy — the gallstones are visible on imaging and the pain is in the upper abdomen, creating a powerful cognitive pull towards a diagnosis of biliary colic or cholecystitis. The well-prepared candidate, however, recognises that gallstones are prevalent in the general Australian population (affecting approximately 10–15% of adults) and are frequently asymptomatic or incidental. The presence of gallstones on ultrasound does not, in itself, confirm a biliary cause for the patient's symptoms. The candidate who anchors to the imaging without completing a thorough, systematic history will reach an incorrect diagnosis and fail the station.

The correct clinical approach demands that the candidate treat the ultrasound result as one data point among many and proceed with a full, structured history encompassing all plausible causes of epigastric pain worsened by eating. In this scenario, the clinical history — particularly the epigastric location, relationship to food (including spicy food and coffee), and response to antacids — points strongly toward a peptic acid disorder, most likely peptic ulcer disease or gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD), rather than a biliary pathology. The gallstones are an incidental finding.

Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

This is a history-taking station in the AMC Clinical Examination format, with a weighted scoring distribution that places the majority of marks on the history itself (approximately 10 marks), with additional marks for communication skills (approximately 4 marks) and a minor component for physical examination awareness (approximately 1 mark). The examiner is assessing whether the candidate can:

  1. Establish rapport and an open-ended beginning — allow the patient to volunteer their presenting concern without premature closure
  2. Conduct a systematic, structured pain history using the SOCRATES framework, generating a broad differential rather than anchoring prematurely
  3. Elicit a targeted systems review based on the differential groups identified through pain characterisation
  4. Screen comprehensively for red flags — this is non-negotiable and a common reason for failure in abdominal pain stations
  5. Explore ICE — the patient's Ideas, Concerns, and Expectations — particularly given that the patient has already received imaging and may have formed beliefs about their diagnosis
  6. Integrate the ultrasound result appropriately — acknowledge its findings without allowing it to dominate the diagnostic conclusion
  7. Formulate and communicate a working diagnosis — specifically, to identify peptic ulcer disease or GORD as the most likely diagnosis and explain why the gallstones are likely incidental
  8. Provide safety netting — advising when to seek urgent review

The station should be approached in a logical sequence: vital sign awareness and patient stability → open-ended history opening → SOCRATES pain assessment → targeted systems review organised by differential group → risk factor screening → red flag screen → ICE exploration → brief examination offer → working diagnosis and explanation → safety netting.

Socrates Flow

Systematic Approach

Establishing Stability and Opening the Consultation

Before proceeding to detailed history taking, the candidate must briefly confirm that the patient is haemodynamically stable and not in acute distress. In a general practice setting, this is done through rapid visual assessment and asking about vital signs if available. A patient in extremis (severe pain, haemodynamic compromise, signs of peritonism) warrants emergency assessment rather than a standard GP consultation.

Begin with a warm, open-ended opening statement that invites the patient to tell their story without direction:

"Thank you for coming in today. I understand you've been having some abdominal pain and you've had a recent scan. Tell me, in your own words, what's been going on for you."

This approach:
- Demonstrates patient-centred communication
- Allows the patient to volunteer the most important information first
- Avoids premature anchoring to the ultrasound result
- Begins to establish the ICE landscape

Allow the patient to speak for at least 30–60 seconds before beginning to focus the history. Acknowledge their experience before transitioning to structured questioning.

SOCRATES Pain Assessment

The SOCRATES framework is the foundational structure for any abdominal pain history in the AMC Clinical Examination. Each domain must be actively explored, not passively accepted. The candidate should ask follow-up questions to deepen the clinical picture.

S — Site
- "Can you point to exactly where the pain is?"
- In this case: mid-epigastric, upper central abdomen
- Note: Right upper quadrant pain radiating to the right shoulder tip or back is more suggestive of biliary pathology; epigastric or retrosternal location favours an acid-related disorder

O — Onset
- "When did the pain first begin? Did it come on suddenly or gradually?"
- Gradual, insidious onset over weeks to months is consistent with peptic ulcer disease or GORD
- Sudden onset (especially nocturnal) may suggest perforation or rupture
- Episodic severe attacks relieved completely between episodes suggest biliary colic

C — Character
- "How would you describe the pain — is it sharp, dull, burning, cramping, or something else?"
- A burning or gnawing epigastric pain is characteristic of peptic ulcer disease
- Biliary colic is classically a severe, crescendo-decrescendo colicky pain
- Heartburn (retrosternal burning) suggests GORD

R — Radiation
- "Does the pain travel or spread anywhere — to your back, your chest, your shoulder?"
- Radiation to the back: consider pancreatitis (horizontal band across epigastrium to back) or posterior duodenal ulcer
- Radiation to right shoulder tip: diaphragmatic irritation, consider biliary or hepatic cause
- No radiation in this case

A — Associated Symptoms
- "Are you experiencing any nausea, vomiting, bloating, or indigestion?"
- "Any changes in your bowel habits — diarrhoea or constipation?"
- "Any blood in your stools — black or tarry stools, or red blood?" ← RED FLAG
- "Any vomiting of blood?" ← RED FLAG
- "Any fever or chills?" — fever suggests infectious or inflammatory cause (cholecystitis, cholangitis)
- "Any jaundice — yellowing of your skin or eyes?" ← RED FLAG if present, suggests biliary obstruction or liver disease
- "Any weight loss recently?" ← RED FLAG for malignancy
- "Any difficulty swallowing?" ← RED FLAG for oesophageal pathology

T — Timing
- "How long have you had this pain altogether?"
- "Is it there all the time, or does it come and go?"
- "How long does each episode last?"
- Chronic, persistent epigastric pain (weeks to months) without complete resolution between episodes is atypical for biliary colic (which is episodic and resolves fully)
- In this case: ongoing, not resolving — consistent with peptic ulcer disease or GORD

E — Exacerbating and Relieving Factors
This is the pivotal SOCRATES domain in this case:
- "Have you noticed anything that makes the pain better or worse?"
- "Does eating make it better or worse?"
- "Are there specific foods that trigger the pain?"
- "How soon after eating does the pain start?"
- "Does anything help — antacids, lying down, sitting up?"

In this scenario, the critical findings are:
- Pain worsened by eating — consistent with peptic ulcer disease (particularly gastric ulcer, which classically worsens with food, unlike duodenal ulcer which typically improves with food)
- Worsened by spicy food — consistent with peptic ulcer disease and GORD
- Worsened by coffee — both caffeine and coffee acidity are established triggers for GORD and peptic ulceration
- Relieved by antacids — strongly supportive of an acid-related disorder; this is not a feature of biliary colic or cholecystitis
- "How soon after eating does the pain begin?" — postprandial pain within 30–60 minutes of eating suggests gastric ulcer; pain 2–3 hours after eating or nocturnal pain suggests duodenal ulcer

E — Severity
- "On a scale of zero to ten, where zero is no pain and ten is the worst imaginable, how would you rate this pain at its worst? And right now?"
- Intensity guides urgency but does not determine diagnosis
- Severe pain (8–10/10) with systemic features warrants urgent investigation

Differential Diagnosis Framework — Systematic Group-by-Group Approach

The structured approach to epigastric pain worsened by eating requires the candidate to work through differential groups sequentially, rather than leaping to the most visible diagnosis (gallstones). This disciplined structure is what separates candidates who pass from those who fail.

Group 1 — Peptic Acid Disorders (most common and most consistent with this history)
- Peptic ulcer disease (gastric and duodenal ulcer)
- Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD)
- Gastritis (including Helicobacter pylori-associated gastritis)
- Non-ulcer dyspepsia (functional dyspepsia)

Key targeted questions:
- "Have you ever been diagnosed with a stomach ulcer, or had a breath test or camera test of your stomach?" (prior H. pylori or endoscopy)
- "Do you take anti-inflammatory medications such as ibuprofen, naproxen, or aspirin regularly?" — NSAIDs are a major risk factor for peptic ulceration
- "Do you smoke?" — tobacco is an independent risk factor for peptic ulcer disease
- "How much alcohol do you drink?" — alcohol irritates the gastric mucosa
- "Do you take antacids or acid-reducing tablets? Do they help?" — improvement with antacids strongly supports an acid-related diagnosis
- "Do you experience heartburn — a burning feeling rising from your stomach into your chest?" — confirms GORD component
- "Do you get a sour or bitter taste in your mouth, particularly when lying down or after eating?" — acid regurgitation, characteristic of GORD

Group 2 — Pancreatic Pathology
- Acute pancreatitis
- Chronic pancreatitis
- Pancreatic malignancy (must not miss in relevant age groups)

Key targeted questions:
- "Is the pain a band-like sensation across your upper abdomen going through to your back?" — characteristic of pancreatitis
- "Does the pain improve when you lean forward?" — pancreatic pain is often relieved in the knee-chest or forward-leaning position
- "How much alcohol do you drink?" — gallstones and alcohol are the two most common causes of pancreatitis in Australia
- "Have you ever had episodes of severe upper abdominal pain requiring hospitalisation?" — prior pancreatitis

Group 3 — Hepatobiliary Pathology
- Biliary colic (episodic, severe, fully resolving)
- Acute cholecystitis (constant pain, fever, positive Murphy's sign)
- Choledocholithiasis (biliary obstruction — jaundice, pale stools, dark urine)
- Ascending cholangitis (Charcot's triad: fever, jaundice, RUQ pain — medical emergency)
- Hepatitis

Key targeted questions:
- "Has the pain ever been severe and cramping, coming in waves and then completely resolving?" — biliary colic character
- "Have you ever noticed yellowing of your skin or eyes?" — jaundice suggests obstruction or hepatitis
- "Have you had dark urine or pale, chalky stools?" — biliary obstruction
- "Have you had any fever with these episodes?" — fever is absent in uncomplicated biliary colic, present in cholecystitis and cholangitis
- "Have you had any alcohol or recent viral illness?" — hepatitis risk

Important clinical reasoning point: The ultrasound confirms gallstones are present, but the absence of cholecystitis features (wall thickening, pericholecystic fluid) and the clinical history (chronic, ongoing pain; food-related; antacid-responsive; no fever; no jaundice) are inconsistent with cholecystitis or biliary colic as the primary diagnosis. The gallstones are most likely incidental.

Group 4 — Cardiac and Vascular (Must Not Miss)
- Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) — inferior myocardial infarction can present as epigastric pain
- Mesenteric ischaemia — severe post-prandial pain with weight loss in vascular risk groups

Key targeted questions:
- "Do you have any associated chest tightness, shortness of breath, or sweating?" — atypical ACS presentation
- "Do you have diabetes, high blood pressure, or high cholesterol?" — cardiovascular risk factors
- "Is there any pain after eating that makes you afraid to eat?" — mesenteric ischaemia, "intestinal angina"
- "Any significant unintentional weight loss?" — mesenteric ischaemia or malignancy

Risk Factor Screening

After completing the SOCRATES assessment and differential group questioning, systematically screen for risk factors relevant to the most likely diagnoses:

For peptic ulcer disease and GORD:
- NSAID use (including over-the-counter ibuprofen, aspirin, diclofenac) — the single most important pharmacological risk factor for peptic ulceration
- Corticosteroid use (especially combined with NSAIDs)
- Anticoagulant use (warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants — relevant if bleeding develops)
- Prior H. pylori infection or diagnosis
- Smoking history
- Alcohol intake
- Coffee and caffeine consumption
- Dietary triggers: spicy food, acidic foods, fatty meals
- Stress history (occupational, psychological)
- Family history of peptic ulcer disease or gastric cancer

For cardiac (given age and gender):
- Hypertension
- Dyslipidaemia
- Diabetes mellitus
- Smoking
- Family history of ischaemic heart disease

Medications (complete review):
- All current prescription and over-the-counter medications
- Antacids or proton pump inhibitors already in use
- Herbal or complementary medicines

ICE — Ideas, Concerns, and Expectations

Given that this patient has already received ultrasound results showing gallstones, the ICE component of this consultation is particularly important and likely to be highly scored.

Ideas:
- "What do you think might be causing your pain? Have you had any thoughts about what this could be?"
- Many patients will have attributed their pain to the gallstones, given the temporal association with receiving the ultrasound result
- "When the doctor told you about the gallstones, what did you think that meant for your pain?"

Concerns:
- "Is there anything about this pain, or what the scan showed, that is particularly worrying you?"
- "Some people worry that gallstones might need an operation — is that something that's crossed your mind?"
- Address specifically: is the patient concerned about cancer, the need for surgery, or a serious diagnosis?

Expectations:
- "What were you hoping we might be able to do for you today?"
- "Were you expecting to need further tests or a referral?"
- "Is there anything specific you were hoping to get out of this appointment?"

Exploring ICE here is clinically critical because the patient may arrive with a fixed belief that the gallstones are causing the pain and may resist a re-framing of the diagnosis. The candidate must be prepared to address this sensitively and clearly, explaining the significance (or lack thereof) of the ultrasound finding in the context of the overall clinical picture.

Redflags Panel
Clinically Enriched

Red Flags — Must Not Miss

Failure to screen for red flags in an abdominal pain history station is a documented cause of examination failure. Every candidate must ask about the following:

  • Haematemesis (vomiting blood) — indicates upper gastrointestinal haemorrhage; requires urgent endoscopy and hospital admission; may indicate bleeding peptic ulcer, oesophageal varices, or Mallory-Weiss tear

  • Melaena (black, tarry, offensive stools) — indicates upper gastrointestinal bleeding; haemoglobin is degraded to haematin in the upper gut; requires urgent investigation and hospital referral

  • Frank rectal bleeding (haematochezia) in a 40-year-old male — while more commonly lower GI in origin, warrants colonoscopy; must exclude colorectal malignancy

  • Unintentional weight loss — any significant involuntary weight loss (>5% body weight over 6 months) in the context of upper abdominal pain raises concern for upper GI malignancy (gastric, oesophageal, or pancreatic); mandates urgent investigation

  • Progressive dysphagia — difficulty swallowing, particularly progressing from solids to liquids, raises concern for oesophageal or gastric cardia malignancy; requires urgent upper GI endoscopy

  • Persistent vomiting — may indicate gastric outlet obstruction from malignancy, severe peptic ulceration, or pyloric stenosis; requires urgent assessment

  • Jaundice (icterus) — yellow discolouration of sclera or skin indicates bilirubin elevation; in this context suggests choledocholithiasis, cholangitis, hepatitis, or pancreatic head malignancy; requires urgent investigation

  • Fever and rigors with abdominal pain — suggests an infective/inflammatory process: cholecystitis, cholangitis (medical emergency), hepatic abscess, or peritonitis; requires urgent assessment

  • New-onset dyspepsia in a patient aged 45 years or over — in Australian practice, new epigastric symptoms in patients ≥45 years without a prior established diagnosis warrant early upper endoscopy to exclude malignancy

  • Palpable abdominal mass — any epigastric or abdominal mass discovered on examination raises concern for malignancy; requires urgent imaging and specialist referral

  • Anaemia symptoms (fatigue, pallor, dyspnoea) — may indicate chronic occult upper GI bleeding from peptic ulcer or malignancy; check full blood count

  • Chest pain, diaphoresis, or syncope with abdominal pain — raises concern for acute coronary syndrome presenting atypically; requires ECG and troponin as first priority

Management Flow
Clinically Enriched

Investigations

Bedside:
- Vital signs: heart rate, blood pressure, temperature, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation
- Blood glucose level (if diabetic or vascular risk factors present)
- Urine dipstick (to exclude urinary tract pathology as a contributor)
- ECG (if any features suggesting ACS or cardiac cause)
- Faecal occult blood test (if available; screens for occult GI bleeding)

Bloods:
- Full blood count (FBC): anaemia (occult bleeding, malignancy), leucocytosis (infection/inflammation)
- C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR): inflammatory markers
- Liver function tests (LFTs): bilirubin, ALT, AST, ALP, GGT — biliary obstruction, hepatitis, pancreatitis pattern
- Serum amylase and lipase: pancreatitis (lipase more sensitive and specific)
- Urea, electrolytes, creatinine (UEC): baseline renal function, particularly if considering NSAIDs or procedural sedation
- Fasting lipid profile and HbA1c: cardiovascular risk assessment given age and gender
- Coagulation studies (INR/PT): if anticoagulated or significant bleeding suspected
- Helicobacter pylori serology: if not already tested; note serology remains positive after eradication and has lower specificity than urea breath test
- CEA and CA 19-9: not first-line, but relevant if malignancy is suspected

Imaging:
- Ultrasound of the abdomen: already performed — confirms gallstones without features of cholecystitis; bile duct diameter should be assessed (if available on report)
- If ultrasound does not adequately visualise the biliary tree or pancreas: CT abdomen with IV contrast provides superior assessment of pancreas, liver, and excludes malignancy
- Chest X-ray: if cardiopulmonary cause considered, or if perforation suspected (free air under diaphragm)

Specialist / Procedural:
- Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (OGD — oesophago-gastro-duodenoscopy): the investigation of choice to confirm or exclude peptic ulcer disease, GORD, gastritis, and malignancy. Allows direct visualisation and biopsy. In a 40-year-old male with epigastric pain and risk factors, this should be organised promptly, particularly if red flag features are absent and empirical treatment fails, or present (immediate referral)
- Helicobacter pylori urea breath test or stool antigen test: non-invasive confirmation of active H. pylori infection; preferred over serology for diagnostic accuracy
- HIDA scan (hepatobiliary iminodiacetic acid scan): nuclear medicine study assessing gallbladder function and bile flow; relevant if biliary dyskinesia is suspected despite a structurally normal gallbladder on ultrasound
- Surgical referral for cholecystectomy: not indicated acutely in this case (no cholecystitis, no biliary obstruction, incidental gallstones); may be considered electively if symptomatic biliary colic is subsequently confirmed

Management

Acute Management in General Practice

  1. Analgesia: For moderate epigastric pain in a GP setting without red flags, begin with oral antacids (e.g., aluminium/magnesium hydroxide) or a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) — for example, omeprazole 20–40 mg orally once daily before breakfast. Do not provide NSAIDs, as these may worsen or cause peptic ulceration.

  2. Empirical acid suppression therapy: In a patient with classic features of an acid-related disorder (epigastric burning, antacid-responsive, food-related, no red flags), it is appropriate to commence a 4-week trial of a PPI (e.g., omeprazole 20 mg once daily or pantoprazole 40 mg once daily). If symptoms resolve completely with this treatment, this further supports the diagnosis of peptic acid disease.

  3. Test and treat for H. pylori: This is the evidence-based approach for all patients with dyspepsia or suspected peptic ulcer disease in Australia. A urea breath test or stool antigen test should be arranged. If positive, eradication therapy is prescribed:

  4. Standard first-line Australian regimen: triple therapy — PPI (standard dose twice daily) + amoxicillin 1 g twice daily + clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily for 7–14 days
  5. Alternative in penicillin allergy or clarithromycin resistance: PPI + metronidazole + clarithromycin, or quadruple therapy

  6. Confirm eradication 4–6 weeks after completing antibiotics with a repeat urea breath test (stop PPI 2 weeks prior)

  7. Lifestyle and dietary modification:

  8. Eliminate or reduce known dietary triggers: spicy foods, coffee, alcohol, carbonated beverages, acidic foods
  9. Cease smoking — provides clear benefit for peptic ulcer healing and recurrence prevention
  10. Avoid eating within 2–3 hours of lying down (relevant for GORD)
  11. Weight reduction if overweight (GORD)
  12. Elevate head of bed (GORD)

  13. Review and cease NSAIDs where possible; if NSAIDs are essential, co-prescribe PPI as gastroprotection

  14. Address the gallstones appropriately: Explain to the patient that the gallstones identified on ultrasound appear incidental at this stage, as the features of the pain do not fit a biliary cause and the ultrasound shows no inflammation. Asymptomatic gallstones in the absence of cholecystitis, biliary obstruction, or pancreatitis do not require immediate surgical intervention. The patient should be advised to report any episodes of severe right upper quadrant pain, fever, or jaundice promptly.

Ongoing Management

  • Upper endoscopy: Arrange within 4 weeks if symptoms fail to respond to empirical PPI therapy, or immediately if red flag features are identified at any point
  • Review at 4–6 weeks: Assess response to PPI, compliance, lifestyle modifications, and H. pylori eradication result
  • Maintenance PPI: Consider long-term maintenance therapy at lowest effective dose in confirmed reflux disease, particularly if the patient requires ongoing NSAID therapy
  • Surgical referral for gallstones: Revisit if the patient subsequently develops characteristic biliary colic episodes, cholecystitis, or biliary pancreatitis — elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy may then be indicated

Communication and Patient Education

Explaining the Diagnosis

The most challenging communication task in this consultation is re-framing the significance of the gallstones for a patient who may have arrived expecting that the scan has explained everything. The candidate must be empathic, clear, and non-dismissive:

"I can understand why you might have thought the gallstones were the cause of your pain — it would seem logical. But when I look at the features of your pain and what the scan actually shows, I think the gallstones are most likely just sitting there harmlessly. The pain you're describing — in the middle of your upper abdomen, brought on by spicy food and coffee, and helped by antacids — actually fits much better with a condition involving the stomach lining or a condition called acid reflux. I'd like to investigate this further and trial some medication, and then we'll reassess."

Key communication principles:
- Validate the patient's experience and the logic of their concern
- Explain clearly and simply, using lay language (e.g., "stomach ulcer," "acid reflux," rather than "peptic ulcer disease" or "GORD" alone)
- Avoid dismissing the gallstones entirely — they may become relevant later, and the patient must be educated about when to seek help related to them
- Collaborative decision-making — involve the patient in the investigation and management plan

Addressing Common Patient Concerns

  • "Do I need my gallbladder removed?" — Reassure that surgery is not indicated for incidental, asymptomatic gallstones. Explain the criteria that would prompt surgical consideration.
  • "Could this be cancer?" — Acknowledge the concern without dismissing it; explain that red flag features are absent and that investigation is being arranged to be thorough
  • "Why did the scan not find the real cause?" — Explain that ultrasound is very good at finding gallstones but is not the best test for the stomach or oesophagus; endoscopy will look at these directly

Written Resources and Follow-Up

  • Provide a written summary of the diagnosis, medications prescribed, and lifestyle advice
  • Supply the patient with the Helicobacter pylori eradication information sheet (available through Australian general practice resources, including NPS MedicineWise)
  • Arrange follow-up appointment in 4–6 weeks
  • Advise clearly when to seek urgent review (see Safety Net below)
  • Provide referral letter for upper endoscopy if appropriate

AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

  • Do not anchor to the imaging result. The most common reason candidates fail this station is premature diagnostic closure on the gallstones. Treat the ultrasound finding as one data point only. The history must drive the diagnosis, not the imaging.

  • SOCRATES must be completed in full. Candidates who skip domains — particularly exacerbating/relieving factors and associated symptoms — miss the clinical clues that redirect the diagnosis. In this case, antacid relief and food-related worsening are diagnostic pivot points.

  • Asking about antacid use is not routine — it is pivotal. The question "Have you tried anything for the pain? Does it help?" is essential here. A clear response that antacids relieve the pain is a critical finding that must redirect the candidate away from biliary pathology.

  • Red flags are mandatory and non-negotiable. In every AMC abdominal pain station, red flag screening must occur regardless of how clear the diagnosis appears. Missing this screen is a documented reason for failing. Ask explicitly about haematemesis, melaena, dysphagia, and weight loss in every case.

  • Biliary colic has a very specific pain pattern. If the candidate considers biliary colic, they must recognise that biliary colic is episodic, severe, fully resolving between attacks, and typically follows fatty meals. It is NOT a chronic, ongoing, dull ache. This distinction must be clear in the candidate's reasoning.

  • The gallstones are incidental — say so clearly in the formulation. Examiners reward candidates who explicitly address the ultrasound finding, acknowledge it, and explain clinically why it is unlikely to be the primary cause. Silence on this point is a missed opportunity.

  • ICE must be explored — the patient has already had a scan. A patient who arrives with an ultrasound result has already formed a cognitive framework about their illness. Exploring their Ideas (what they believe is wrong), Concerns (what worries them), and Expectations (what they hope to achieve) is clinically essential and marks-generating.

  • Structure your differential verbally when presenting. The examiner expects a candidate to state: "My most likely diagnosis is peptic ulcer disease / GORD based on [specific features]. The gallstones are likely an incidental finding. I need to exclude [red flag conditions] and will organise [investigations]." This demonstrates clinical reasoning, not just data collection.

  • Epigastric pain in a 40-year-old male warrants H. pylori testing. Always mention H. pylori in the context of peptic ulcer disease. This is standard Australian general practice: test and treat, then confirm eradication.

  • Do not forget ACS. In a 40-year-old male with upper abdominal pain, acute coronary syndrome must be on the differential and red flags for cardiac aetiology must be screened. Inferior MI classically presents as epigastric discomfort. Ask about chest tightness, diaphoresis, and cardiac risk factors.

  • Avoid the word "incidental" without explanation. If you tell a patient their gallstones are "incidental," explain what that means — that they appear to be present but not currently causing harm. Patients find this confusing and may feel dismissed.

  • Close the consultation explicitly. Summarise your working diagnosis, the immediate management plan (empirical PPI, H. pylori testing), the investigation plan (endoscopy), and the safety-net advice. A structured, confident close demonstrates clinical competence and scores communication marks.

Safety Net

Every patient leaving this consultation must receive clear, explicit safety-net advice regarding when to seek urgent medical attention. Document this in the clinical record and communicate it verbally.

Return immediately to the Emergency Department or call 000 if:
- Vomiting blood or material resembling coffee grounds (haematemesis)
- Passing black, tarry, foul-smelling stools (melaena) — indicating upper GI bleeding
- Sudden severe worsening of abdominal pain, especially if the abdomen becomes rigid or board-like (perforation)
- Severe right upper quadrant pain with fever and rigors (acute cholecystitis or cholangitis)
- Jaundice — yellowing of skin or eyes
- Chest tightness, pain, shortness of breath, or diaphoresis (possible ACS)
- Collapse, dizziness, or syncope

Return to the general practice within 1–2 weeks if:
- No improvement with the prescribed PPI therapy
- Symptoms are worsening despite medication
- New symptoms develop — particularly difficulty swallowing, unintentional weight loss, or persistent vomiting

Routine follow-up:
- Review appointment in 4–6 weeks to assess response to empirical PPI therapy and H. pylori eradication treatment
- Confirm receipt of urea breath test result and act accordingly
- Upper endoscopy review within 4–6 weeks of procedure

Regarding the gallstones specifically:
- "The gallstones found on your scan do not need treatment right now — they appear to be harmlessly sitting there. However, if you develop a sudden, severe pain in the right side of your upper abdomen that spreads to your right shoulder, or if you develop a fever or notice your eyes or skin turning yellow, please seek urgent medical attention, as this could mean the gallstones are causing a problem."

Written documentation: Provide a patient information sheet summarising:
1. Working diagnosis (suspected peptic acid disease / GORD)
2. Current treatment (PPI name, dose, duration)
3. Lifestyle advice
4. H. pylori test details and next steps
5. Safety-net symptoms requiring urgent return
6. Follow-up appointment date and purpose

Key Facts & Clinical Priorities

Red Flags — Must Not Miss

  • Haematemesis (vomiting blood)
  • Melaena (black, tarry, offensive stools)
  • Frank rectal bleeding (haematochezia) in a 40-year-old male
  • Unintentional weight loss
  • Progressive dysphagia
  • Persistent vomiting
  • Jaundice (icterus)
  • Fever and rigors with abdominal pain

Differential Diagnoses

  • Peptic ulcer disease
  • Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD)
  • Functional dyspepsia
  • Gastritis (including *H. pylori*)
  • Biliary colic
  • Chronic pancreatitis
  • Acute pancreatitis
  • Mesenteric ischaemia

AMC Exam Key Points

  • Do not anchor to the imaging result. The most common reason candidates fail this station is premature diagnostic closure on the gallstones. Treat the ultrasound finding as one data point only. The history must drive the diagnosis, not the imaging.
  • SOCRATES must be completed in full. Candidates who skip domains
  • Asking about antacid use is not routine
  • Red flags are mandatory and non-negotiable. In every AMC abdominal pain station, red flag screening must occur regardless of how clear the diagnosis appears. Missing this screen is a documented reason for failing. Ask explicitly about haematemesis, melaena, dysphagia, and weight loss in every case.
  • Biliary colic has a very specific pain pattern. If the candidate considers biliary colic, they must recognise that biliary colic is episodic, severe, fully resolving between attacks, and typically follows fatty meals. It is NOT a chronic, ongoing, dull ache. This distinction must be clear in the candidate's reasoning.
  • The gallstones are incidental
  • ICE must be explored
  • Structure your differential verbally when presenting. The examiner expects a candidate to state: *"My most likely diagnosis is peptic ulcer disease / GORD based on [specific features]. The gallstones are likely an incidental finding. I need to exclude [red flag conditions] and will organise [investigations]."* This demonstrates clinical reasoning, not just data collection.
