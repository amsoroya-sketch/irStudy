# Right Lower Quadrant Abdominal Pain — Physical Examination and History Taking Station

## Clinical Presentation

A 27-year-old woman presents to a general practice setting with a four-day history of right lower quadrant (RLQ) abdominal pain, worsening over the preceding 24 hours. She reports feeling feverish and has vomited on multiple occasions. This presentation encapsulates one of the highest-yield clinical scenarios in the AMC Clinical Examination: the young woman with RLQ pain requiring systematic exclusion of life-threatening gynaecological, surgical, and urological pathology.

The clinical importance of this station lies in its diagnostic complexity. The RLQ pain differential in a woman of reproductive age spans at least three organ systems — gastrointestinal, urological, and gynaecological — and includes conditions ranging from the benign (Mittelschmerz) to the immediately life-threatening (ectopic pregnancy with haemodynamic compromise). The AMC examiner is specifically testing the candidate's ability to maintain a structured, system-based approach in the face of multiple convergent findings that may point simultaneously towards several diagnoses.

A defining feature of this station type is the phenomenon of multiple positive findings — a deliberate AMC examination design in which candidates who systematically rule out a broad differential will encounter positive findings across several diagnostic categories. This is not an error in the examination design; it is a quality marker. Candidates who narrow to a single diagnosis early and abandon their structured approach will miss critical findings. The ability to gather multiple positive findings, integrate them coherently, and arrive at a reasoned provisional diagnosis with an articulate differential is precisely what distinguishes a passing candidate from a failing one.

---

## Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

This station tests both history-taking and focused physical examination skills within a single integrated consultation. The examiner assesses the candidate's ability to:

1. **Open the consultation** professionally with appropriate introduction, consent, and pain assessment
2. **Conduct systematic history-taking** using an organised, system-based structure — not a scatter-gun approach
3. **Perform a targeted physical examination** following the inspection–palpation–percussion–auscultation (IPPA) sequence, including relevant special tests
4. **Integrate findings** to form a provisional diagnosis with an articulate, ranked differential
5. **Communicate the diagnosis** in accessible, jargon-free language with clear explanation and appropriate safety netting

The examiner will provide vital signs, examination findings, and investigation results verbally when specifically requested. Candidates who fail to explicitly request findings — for example, asking only about tenderness without asking about guarding, rigidity, and rebound tenderness — will not receive those findings volunteered. Every finding in this station must be **actively requested** by the candidate.

The examination sequence expected in this station is:

- **Introduction and consent** — confirm patient identity, introduce self, obtain consent for examination
- **Vital signs** — temperature, blood pressure, pulse rate, respiratory rate
- **Abdominal inspection** — general appearance, abdominal contour, skin changes, visible peristalsis, movement with respiration
- **Superficial then deep palpation** — tenderness, guarding, rigidity
- **Percussion** — percussion tenderness in RLQ
- **Auscultation** — bowel sounds
- **Special tests for appendicitis** — Rovsing sign, obturator sign, psoas sign
- **Pelvic examination** — inspection of external genitalia, speculum examination (vaginal walls, cervix), bimanual examination (cervical motion tenderness, uterine size and tenderness, adnexal tenderness and masses)
- **Bedside investigations** — urine pregnancy test, urine dipstick

## Systematic Approach

### History-Taking Structure: Pain Assessment Using SOCRATES

The history begins with an open-ended invitation — *"Can you tell me more about the pain you have been experiencing?"* — before transitioning to systematic SOCRATES questioning.

**Site:** Right lower quadrant. The crucial follow-up question is: *"Did the pain start where it is now, or did it begin somewhere else and move here?"* This screens for the classical migration of appendicitis (periumbilical pain shifting to McBurney's point). In this case, the pain began and remained in the RLQ — absence of shift reduces the likelihood of classic appendicitis, though it does not exclude it.

**Onset:** Four days, worsening in the past 24 hours. Clarify whether onset was sudden or gradual. Sudden-onset severe pain raises the possibility of ovarian torsion or a ruptured ectopic pregnancy.

**Character:** Colicky versus constant. Constant, progressive pain suggests an inflammatory or ischaemic process (appendicitis, torsion, ectopic). Colicky pain suggests hollow viscus involvement (ureteric colic, bowel obstruction).

**Radiation:** To the back or flank suggests renal or ureteric origin. Shoulder-tip pain (diaphragmatic irritation from haemoperitoneum) is a critical red flag for ruptured ectopic pregnancy.

**Associations:** Nausea, vomiting (note colour — bilious green vomiting suggests obstruction; haematemesis requires urgent escalation), fever and chills, change in bowel habit, anorexia, urinary symptoms.

**Timing:** Continuous versus intermittent, relationship to the menstrual cycle.

**Exacerbating/Relieving factors:** Movement, eating, micturition, defecation.

**Severity:** Numerical pain score 0–10; functional impact.

---

### System-Based Differential Screening Questions

Following pain characterisation, history-taking should proceed through three distinct groups of screening questions, each targeting a specific organ system:

**Group 1 — Gastrointestinal**
- Nausea and vomiting (if present: colour of vomit — specifically rule out bilious vomiting)
- Anorexia
- Change in bowel habit — diarrhoea or constipation
- Blood in stool
- Bloating and distension
- Lumps or swellings in the groin (screen for inguinal hernia)
- Previous abdominal surgeries
- Current medications (NSAIDs, immunosuppressants)
- Family history of colorectal or bowel disease

**Group 2 — Renal and Urological (KUB)**
- Dysuria — burning or stinging on urination
- Urinary frequency and urgency
- Haematuria
- Loin or flank pain

**Group 3 — Gynaecological and Obstetric (Pelvic)**
- **Last menstrual period (LMP):** Ask the exact date. If greater than four weeks, calculate gestational age and assess regularity of cycle. Irregularity (e.g., cycles every four to six weeks) complicates interpretation but does not eliminate the possibility of pregnancy.
- **Menstrual regularity:** Regular or irregular? How often? This contextualises the significance of a delayed period.
- **Sexual history:** Is the patient sexually active? Does she use contraception? Has she ever been diagnosed with a sexually transmitted infection (STI)? A history of prior STI substantially raises the risk of pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) and tubal damage predisposing to ectopic pregnancy.
- **Vaginal discharge:** Character, colour, odour, volume
- **Dyspareunia:** Superficial versus deep

**Fever clarification:** When the patient reports feeling "feverish" or "a bit hot," the candidate must follow this with:
1. *"Have you checked your temperature at home?"*
2. *"Have you had any rigors or chills?"*

This approach demonstrates clinical rigour. The AMC commonly provides fever in colloquial terms rather than an objective temperature reading; failure to probe for objective measurement is a missed opportunity to demonstrate clinical reasoning.

### Physical Examination Sequence

**Step 1 — Vital Signs (Always request explicitly)**

Ask: *"Examiner, can I have the vital signs please — temperature, blood pressure, pulse rate, and respiratory rate?"*

In this case, the temperature is 37.5°C. While this sits at the lower threshold for fever (variously defined as ≥37.4–38.0°C depending on measurement site), in the context of this clinical presentation it should be treated as clinically significant. It does not exclude ectopic pregnancy — fever and flu-like symptoms appear on the symptom spectrum of ectopic pregnancy — and should not redirect the differential towards an infective process alone.

**Step 2 — General Inspection**

Observe: general appearance, pallor (anaemia from haemoperitoneum), diaphoresis, distress level, jaundice, cachexia. Note whether the patient is lying still (peritonism) versus writhing (colic).

**Step 3 — Abdominal Inspection**

Observe: abdominal contour (flat, distended, scaphoid), visible peristalsis, skin changes (erythema, bruising — Cullen's sign at umbilicus or Grey Turner's sign in the flanks suggest retroperitoneal haemorrhage), surgical scars, abdominal movement with respiration (reduced movement suggests peritonism).

**Step 4 — Palpation (Superficial, then Deep)**

Begin with superficial palpation in all four quadrants, moving away from the site of pain. Assess for:
- **Tenderness** — location and distribution
- **Guarding** — voluntary muscle contraction in response to palpation
- **Rigidity** — involuntary board-like muscle rigidity indicating peritonism

Progress to deep palpation, then specifically assess the RLQ for:
- **Tenderness at McBurney's point** (one-third the distance from the right anterior superior iliac spine to the umbilicus)
- **Guarding** — ask explicitly: *"Examiner, is there guarding in the right lower quadrant?"*
- **Rigidity** — ask explicitly: *"Is there rigidity?"*
- **Rebound tenderness** — ask explicitly: *"Is there rebound tenderness?"*

> **Critical examination point:** Each of guarding, rigidity, and rebound tenderness must be asked about **individually**. Candidates who ask only about "tenderness" will not be volunteered findings of guarding and rigidity. In this case, guarding and rigidity are positive, and rebound tenderness is positive — findings that represent peritoneal irritation and are clinically significant regardless of the underlying diagnosis.

**Step 5 — Percussion**

Percuss all quadrants. Ask: *"Examiner, is there percussion tenderness in the right lower quadrant?"* Percussion tenderness is an alternative, less painful method of eliciting peritoneal irritation.

**Step 6 — Auscultation**

Bowel sounds: normal, hypoactive (ileus, peritonitis), hyperactive (early obstruction, gastroenteritis), or absent (late obstruction, generalised peritonitis). Ask specifically in the RLQ.

**Step 7 — Special Tests for Appendicitis**

These must be requested explicitly by name:

- **Rovsing's sign:** Palpation in the left iliac fossa produces pain in the right iliac fossa — indicates peritoneal irritation at the appendix. In this case: **negative.**
- **Obturator sign:** Flexion and internal rotation of the right hip with the knee bent — pain in the RLQ suggests an inflamed appendix (or pelvic structure) in contact with the obturator internus. In this case: **negative.**
- **Psoas sign (Iliopsoas sign):** Pain on extension of the right hip against resistance, or on passive extension with the patient in left lateral position — suggests a retrocaecal appendix irritating the psoas muscle.

Negative Rovsing and obturator signs in this context meaningfully reduce the probability of appendicitis and support the direction toward a gynaecological diagnosis.

**Step 8 — Pelvic Examination**

State: *"Examiner, I would like to perform a pelvic examination with a female chaperone present, with the patient's consent."*

*Inspection of external genitalia:*
- Erythema or rash
- Abnormal discharge
- Bleeding
- Swellings or masses

*Speculum examination:*
Ask: *"Examiner, on speculum examination, are there any abnormalities of the vaginal walls or cervix?"*
- Cervical ectropion, cervicitis, discharge at the os
- In this case: normal vaginal walls and cervix

*Bimanual examination:*
This is the critical component of the pelvic examination in this station. Request each finding individually:

1. *"Is cervical motion tenderness present?"* — **Positive.** Cervical motion tenderness (CMT) is elicited by gently moving the cervix laterally during bimanual examination. A positive CMT (the so-called "chandelier sign") indicates irritation of the pelvic peritoneum and is found in both PID and ectopic pregnancy. It must not be attributed solely to PID.

2. *"Can you tell me the size, shape, and tenderness of the uterus?"* — **Uterus is normal size, anteverted (or retroverted — note the finding), with no tenderness of the uterine body.** Importantly, the uterus is **empty** — a finding consistent with ectopic pregnancy.

3. *"Is there right adnexal tenderness?"* — **Positive.**

4. *"Are there any adnexal masses?"* — Request specifically. An adnexal mass in the context of a positive pregnancy test and RLQ pain should be presumed an ectopic pregnancy until proven otherwise.

---

### Bedside Investigations

Request these explicitly during the examination:

**Urine pregnancy test (UPT):** *"Examiner, I would like to perform a urine pregnancy test."*
- Result: **Positive.**
- This is the single most important investigation in this clinical scenario. A positive UPT in the context of RLQ pain, adnexal tenderness, CMT, and an empty uterus on bimanual examination constitutes a working diagnosis of ectopic pregnancy until excluded by imaging.

**Urine dipstick:** *"Examiner, I would like to perform a urine dipstick."*
- Result: **Normal** (no leucocytes, nitrites, haematuria)
- Reduces the probability of urinary tract infection and urolithiasis as the primary diagnosis, though does not exclude these entirely.

## Differential Diagnosis

| Category | Diagnosis | Key Distinguishing Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Must Not Miss** | **Ectopic pregnancy** | Positive UPT, positive CMT, right adnexal tenderness, empty uterus on bimanual, missed or delayed period, history of STI |
| **Must Not Miss** | **Ovarian torsion** | Sudden-onset severe pain, nausea and vomiting, adnexal mass, negative UPT — requires urgent Doppler ultrasound |
| **Must Not Miss** | **Appendicitis** | RLQ pain with shift from periumbilical origin, positive Rovsing/obturator/psoas signs, fever >38°C, anorexia — negative special tests in this case reduce probability |
| **Important** | **Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)** | Positive CMT, vaginal discharge, history of STI, bilateral adnexal tenderness, negative UPT — discharge absent in this case; UPT positive argues against |
| **Important** | **Ovarian cyst** (corpus luteum, follicular) | Mild unilateral pain, mid-cycle timing, previous episodes, UPT negative — bilateral or enlarging cysts may torse |
| **Important** | **Urinary tract infection / Pyelonephritis** | Dysuria, frequency, loin pain, haematuria, positive dipstick (leucocytes, nitrites) |
| **Important** | **Ureteric colic** | Severe colicky loin-to-groin radiation, haematuria on dipstick, writhing in pain |
| **Common** | **Mittelschmerz (mid-cycle pain)** | Day 14 of regular 28-day cycle, mild unilateral LQ pain, previous self-resolving episodes, negative UPT, no fever |
| **Common** | **Gastroenteritis** | Vomiting and diarrhoea, cramping pain, systemic viral illness features |
| **Common** | **Irritable bowel syndrome** | Chronic relapsing, associated with bowel habit change, relieved by defecation, no systemic features |
| **Consider** | **Incarcerated inguinal hernia** | Groin lump, pain on Valsalva, absent bowel sounds, obstruction features |
| **Consider** | **Bowel obstruction** | Absolute constipation, distension, colicky pain, high-pitched or absent bowel sounds, surgical history |
| **Consider** | **Crohn's disease** | Young patient, chronic diarrhoea, weight loss, perianal disease, systemic features |

## Red Flags — Must Not Miss

- **Positive urine pregnancy test with unilateral adnexal pain** — Must be treated as ectopic pregnancy until excluded by transvaginal ultrasound. Do not reassure the patient. Urgent gynaecological referral and bedside quantitative β-hCG required.

- **Haemodynamic instability (hypotension, tachycardia, pallor, syncope)** — Indicates possible ruptured ectopic pregnancy with haemoperitoneum. This is a surgical emergency requiring immediate resuscitation, IV access, cross-match, and emergency theatre activation.

- **Shoulder-tip pain** — Diaphragmatic irritation from haemoperitoneum (free blood tracking to subphrenic space). In the context of a positive UPT, this mandates immediate emergency referral.

- **Peritoneal signs (guarding, rigidity, rebound tenderness)** — Even in the absence of fever or haemodynamic compromise, peritonism mandates urgent surgical or gynaecological review. Do not discharge a patient with these findings from general practice.

- **Fever >38°C with cervical motion tenderness and adnexal tenderness** — May represent septic ectopic pregnancy or severe PID. Requires inpatient management, intravenous antibiotics, and imaging.

- **Bilious (green) vomiting** — Implies distal obstruction at or beyond the duodenum. Requires urgent surgical review and imaging to exclude bowel obstruction or volvulus.

- **Severe acute-onset pain ('worst pain of my life')** — Suggests catastrophic event: torsion, rupture, or perforation. Requires emergency assessment.

- **History of prior STI with bilateral adnexal tenderness and fever** — Even with a positive UPT, concurrent PID cannot be excluded. Both diagnoses may coexist in the context of a recently infected pelvis.

- **Absence of bowel sounds with abdominal rigidity** — Generalised peritonitis requiring immediate surgical escalation.

## Investigations

**Bedside:**
- Urine pregnancy test (UPT) — mandatory in all women of reproductive age with RLQ pain
- Urine dipstick — leucocytes, nitrites, haematuria, protein
- Temperature, blood pressure, pulse rate, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation
- Capillary blood glucose (if clinically indicated)

**Bloods:**
- Full blood count (FBC) — leucocytosis in appendicitis, PID, ruptured ectopic; anaemia in haemorrhage
- C-reactive protein (CRP) — elevated in appendicitis, PID
- Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR)
- Quantitative serum β-human chorionic gonadotrophin (β-hCG) — serial measurements (48-hourly) distinguish viable intrauterine pregnancy (doubling time approximately 48 hours) from ectopic pregnancy (suboptimally rising or plateauing β-hCG)
- Urea, electrolytes and creatinine (UEC)
- Liver function tests (LFTs)
- Coagulation studies (if surgical intervention anticipated)
- Group and hold / crossmatch (if haemodynamic compromise suspected)
- Blood cultures (if sepsis suspected)
- Cervical swabs for *Chlamydia trachomatis* and *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* (NAAT)

**Imaging:**
- **Transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS)** — first-line investigation for suspected ectopic pregnancy; identifies intrauterine gestational sac, adnexal mass, free fluid in the pouch of Douglas. A discriminatory β-hCG zone of approximately 1,500–2,000 IU/L is used: an intrauterine pregnancy should be visible on TVUS above this threshold; absence suggests ectopic or failed intrauterine pregnancy.
- Transabdominal ultrasound — adjunct to TVUS; useful for larger adnexal masses and haemoperitoneum assessment
- CT abdomen and pelvis (with contrast) — if appendicitis or other surgical pathology suspected and ultrasound inconclusive; radiation consideration in young women of reproductive age
- MRI pelvis — alternative to CT for complex adnexal pathology without radiation exposure

**Specialist Referral:**
- Emergency gynaecology referral — all confirmed or suspected ectopic pregnancies
- Emergency surgery consultation — if appendicitis or surgical cause strongly suspected
- Diagnostic laparoscopy — gold standard for definitive diagnosis and surgical management of ectopic pregnancy

## Management

### Ectopic Pregnancy — Acute Management

**Immediate stabilisation:**
- IV access (two large-bore cannulae)
- IV crystalloid resuscitation if haemodynamically compromised
- Continuous monitoring: pulse, blood pressure, oxygen saturation
- Cross-match and group and hold
- Nothing by mouth (NBM) in anticipation of surgical intervention
- Analgesia — IV morphine titrated to pain, with antiemetic

**Definitive management options (gynaecology-directed):**

1. **Expectant management** — appropriate only for haemodynamically stable women with a small ectopic (typically <3 cm), declining β-hCG, and no cardiac activity on ultrasound. Requires close outpatient monitoring with serial β-hCG and repeat ultrasound.

2. **Medical management — Methotrexate (MTX)** — appropriate for haemodynamically stable, unruptured ectopic with β-hCG typically <5,000 IU/L, no foetal cardiac activity, and no contraindications (normal renal, hepatic, and haematological function; no breastfeeding; not immunocompromised). Single-dose IM methotrexate is the standard regimen. Serial β-hCG monitoring required post-treatment.

3. **Surgical management** — laparoscopic salpingotomy (tube-conserving, if future fertility desired and contralateral tube is damaged) or laparoscopic salpingectomy (tube removal, preferred if contralateral tube is healthy or in unstable patients). Open laparotomy reserved for haemodynamically unstable or ruptured cases.

**Rhesus status:** All women undergoing management for ectopic pregnancy must have their blood group checked; Rh-negative women require anti-D immunoglobulin administration.

**Ongoing management:**
- Emotional and psychological support — pregnancy loss counselling
- Discussion of future fertility implications
- Contraception counselling post-recovery
- Follow-up with quantitative β-hCG until undetectable
- STI screening and contact tracing if concurrent PID suspected

## Communication and Patient Education

### Explaining the Diagnosis

When communicating a diagnosis of ectopic pregnancy, the following structure is recommended:

1. **Name the diagnosis clearly:** *"Based on your symptoms, examination findings, and the positive pregnancy test, I believe you have a condition called an ectopic pregnancy."*

2. **Explain in accessible language:** *"In a normal pregnancy, the fertilised egg travels down the fallopian tube and settles in the womb to grow. In an ectopic pregnancy, the fertilised egg begins growing inside the tube itself rather than reaching the womb. The tube is not designed to accommodate a growing pregnancy, and this can cause it to stretch and rupture, which can be very serious."*

3. **Link the diagnosis to findings:** Explain that the positive pregnancy test confirmed pregnancy; the absence of a pregnancy within the uterus on internal examination (empty womb), combined with tenderness at the right adnexa and a painful cervix on examination, strongly suggests the pregnancy is located in the right tube.

4. **Address the fever and vomiting:** Reassure the patient that feeling feverish and nauseous can be features of ectopic pregnancy itself, and that morning sickness is a natural early pregnancy symptom. This prevents the patient from minimising her symptoms as "just pregnancy."

5. **Convey urgency without alarm:** *"This is a condition that requires urgent attention from a specialist today. I am going to refer you immediately to the hospital gynaecology team. I want to be clear — this is a potentially serious situation and needs to be assessed urgently."*

### Explaining Included Differentials (Briefly)

**Appendicitis:** *"One other possibility I considered was appendicitis — inflammation of a small pouch attached to the bowel. However, the special examination tests I performed were negative, which makes this less likely."*

**Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID):** *"I also considered an infection in the womb and tubes, called pelvic inflammatory disease. While you do have some features consistent with this, you did not have any unusual vaginal discharge, and importantly, your pregnancy test is positive — this changes the picture and makes ectopic pregnancy the priority diagnosis."*

### Questions to Anticipate

- *"Am I going to lose the pregnancy?"* — Sensitive, honest response: the pregnancy, unfortunately, cannot be saved in an ectopic location and must be removed to protect the patient's health and life.
- *"Will I be able to get pregnant again?"* — Reassure that many women have successful subsequent pregnancies after ectopic pregnancy; the outcome depends on the treatment chosen and the health of the remaining tube.
- *"Did my STI cause this?"* — Explain factually that prior STIs can cause tubal scarring which predisposes to ectopic pregnancy; avoid language that sounds accusatory.
- *"Is this my fault?"* — Directly address guilt: *"This is not your fault. This is something that can happen to any woman."*

## AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

- **Explicitly request guarding, rigidity, and rebound tenderness as three separate questions.** The AMC examiner will only provide these findings when directly asked. A candidate who asks only "is there tenderness?" will not be awarded marks for identifying peritoneal signs, even if they are present.

- **Never narrow to a single diagnosis early.** The AMC deliberately designs cases with positive findings across multiple diagnostic categories to test whether candidates maintain a structured, comprehensive approach. Encountering multiple positive findings is a sign you are performing well — not a sign the case is broken.

- **The urine pregnancy test is mandatory in every woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain.** Failure to request a UPT in this demographic is a critical omission. Even if the examiner does not volunteer it, the candidate must actively request it.

- **Cervical motion tenderness must be requested by name during bimanual examination.** It is not volunteered spontaneously. Ask: *"Examiner, is cervical motion tenderness present?"* A positive result in the context of a positive UPT is highly significant.

- **Do not anchor on fever as evidence of infection alone.** Fever and flu-like symptoms are documented features of ectopic pregnancy. A temperature of 37.5°C should not redirect the differential away from ectopic towards an infective diagnosis without corroborating evidence.

- **The pain shift question is non-negotiable in RLQ presentations.** Always ask: *"Did the pain start where it is now, or did it begin somewhere else?"* The presence or absence of a classical periumbilical-to-RLQ shift significantly modifies the appendicitis probability and must be documented in your reasoning.

- **Request a chaperone and patient consent before any pelvic examination.** State this explicitly to the examiner. The AMC assesses professionalism alongside clinical competence. Omitting consent and chaperone is a communication mark deduction.

- **Name your special tests explicitly.** Do not say "can I do some tests on the abdomen." Say: *"I would like to perform Rovsing's sign — is it positive? I would like to perform the obturator sign — is it positive?"* Each named test, when correctly requested, attracts individual marks.

- **Do not perform a pelvic examination without first completing the abdominal examination.** The IPPA sequence with special abdominal tests must precede the pelvic examination. Jumping directly to pelvic examination before completing the abdominal examination demonstrates poor structure.

- **Probe vomiting for colour.** When a patient reports vomiting, the single most important follow-up question is the colour of the vomit. Bilious (green) vomiting is a red flag requiring specific documentation and action. Blood-stained vomiting (haematemesis) mandates urgent escalation.

- **When LMP is delayed and cycles are irregular, do not dismiss pregnancy.** In a patient with cycles of four to six weeks, an LMP five weeks ago does not exclude pregnancy. The correct response is to proceed to a pregnancy test regardless.

- **In the diagnosis explanation, link every finding to the diagnosis.** The AMC expects candidates to explain the reasoning behind their diagnosis — not just state it. Connect the positive UPT, the empty uterus, the adnexal tenderness, and the CMT to the diagnosis of ectopic pregnancy in language the patient can understand.

## Safety Net

The following safety-netting instructions must be provided to the patient in clear, unambiguous language. In the case of suspected ectopic pregnancy, the safety net is framed within the context of **same-day urgent referral** — the patient should not leave the practice without a confirmed specialist appointment or direct transfer to hospital.

**Immediate return to the emergency department (or call triple-zero) if any of the following occur:**
- Sudden severe worsening of abdominal pain
- Feeling faint, dizzy, or collapsing
- Pallor, sweating, or rapid heart rate
- Shoulder-tip pain (pain at the tip of the right or left shoulder)
- Heavy vaginal bleeding
- Inability to keep fluids down

**Follow-up plan:**
- Same-day emergency gynaecology referral — do not delay
- Serial quantitative serum β-hCG measurements as directed by the specialist (typically at 0, 48, and 72 hours)
- Transvaginal ultrasound to be arranged urgently by gynaecology
- If methotrexate is administered: strict follow-up protocol with β-hCG monitoring at day 4 and day 7 post-treatment; avoid alcohol, NSAIDs, folic acid supplements, and sun exposure; use reliable contraception for at least three months post-treatment
- Written information on ectopic pregnancy (e.g., Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists [RANZCOG] patient information leaflets)
- Emotional support: provide information about pregnancy loss support services; validate that this is a frightening and distressing experience

**Key message to communicate clearly:** *"You must go to hospital today. This condition requires urgent specialist assessment, and if it gets worse before you reach hospital — particularly if you develop severe pain, dizziness, or shoulder pain — call an ambulance immediately."*
