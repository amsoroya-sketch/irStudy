AMC Clinical Examination Scoring System and Performance Framework

Clinical Presentation

The AMC Clinical Examination is a high-stakes objective structured clinical examination (OSCE) designed to assess whether an international medical graduate (IMG) meets the standards required for general registration in Australia. Understanding the examination's scoring architecture is essential for any candidate undertaking this assessment, as it directly determines the pass or fail outcome of each station and of the examination as a whole. The examination comprises 16 stations, of which 14 are scored and 2 are pilot stations; candidates are not informed which stations are pilots, and the AMC advises treating all 16 stations with equal commitment and rigour.

The passing threshold requires a candidate to achieve satisfactory performance on a minimum of nine of the 14 scored stations. Failure to reach this threshold — that is, achieving a satisfactory global rating on eight or fewer scored stations — results in a failed examination. The AMC does not impose a limit on the number of attempts a candidate may make at the clinical examination, though repeated attempts carry significant personal, financial, and professional costs. Historically, passing rates have fluctuated substantially following changes to the marking framework, most notably dropping from above 60 percent to approximately 20–25 percent following scoring system revisions, underscoring how fundamentally the marking architecture shapes candidate outcomes.

This clinical reference addresses the structural mechanics of the AMC Clinical Examination scoring system, the role of the predominant assessment area, the concept of critical key points, the importance of differential diagnosis, time management discipline, and the distinction between borderline and clearly passing performance. Mastery of this framework is not peripheral preparation — it is a prerequisite for strategic, targeted, and ultimately successful examination performance.

Counselling Pathway

Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

The Seven-Point Global Rating Scale

Each AMC Clinical Examination station is assessed using a seven-point Likert-type scale across multiple assessed domains (tasks), and the examiner assigns a separate, overarching global rating score at the conclusion of the station. The global rating — not the arithmetic sum or average of individual domain scores — is the sole determinant of whether a candidate passes or fails that station.

  • Score of 4 or above on the global rating = Station passed
  • Score of 3 or below on the global rating = Station failed

Individual domain scores (history taking, physical examination, diagnostic formulation, management and counselling, approach to the patient) are recorded and reported to the candidate in post-examination feedback, but these scores do not numerically or directly determine the pass/fail outcome. The AMC is explicit on this point: a candidate may receive passing scores across the majority of assessed domains and still receive a failing global rating. Conversely, a candidate with one low domain score may still receive a passing global rating if the examiner's holistic assessment of the overall clinical performance is satisfactory.

The Three Assessed Domains Most Commonly Encountered

Most standard diagnostic formulation stations (which constitute approximately 90 percent of face-to-face examination cases) carry three assessed tasks:

  1. History taking — systematic, structured, clinically relevant information gathering
  2. Physical examination — choice of examination, technique, and performance of examination
  3. Diagnosis and differential diagnosis — formulation of a primary diagnosis and a clinically credible, specific, and broad differential diagnosis list

Additionally, all stations carry an approach to the patient domain, which assesses the candidate's interpersonal communication, professionalism, empathy, and rapport throughout the encounter. Counselling stations and examination-only stations may present with two assessed tasks, while five-task stations — though less common — do occur and require heightened time discipline from candidates.

Systematic Approach

The Predominant Assessment Area — The Decisive Domain

The most operationally significant concept in the AMC Clinical Examination scoring framework is the predominant assessment area (PAA). When an examiner assigns the global rating, AMC instructs examiners to weight their global assessment in favour of how well the candidate performed in the station's designated PAA. The PAA is reported to the candidate in post-examination feedback.

The four defined predominant assessment areas are:

PAA What It Means for the Candidate
History Taking The systematic, structured clinical history is the primary performance indicator for this station
Physical Examination The choice, technique, and performance of clinical examination determines the global rating
Diagnostic Formulation The primary diagnosis and differential diagnosis list are weighted most heavily
Management and Counselling The management plan, patient education, and counselling competency drive the global outcome

Practical implication: A candidate who performs acceptably across all other domains but fails the PAA domain will almost invariably receive a failing global rating, regardless of other domain scores. Conversely, a candidate with a low score in a non-PAA domain, but who demonstrates clearly satisfactory performance in the PAA, has a reasonable prospect of receiving a passing global rating.

Consider the following representative patterns drawn from real candidate feedback:

  • History PAA, History score = pass, Differentials = fail → Global pass (examiner overlooks weak differentials in a history-dominant case)
  • Examination PAA, Examination score = fail, History = pass → Global fail (no other domain compensates for failure in the examination PAA)
  • Diagnostic Formulation PAA, Diagnosis/Differentials = pass, Examination technique = fail → Global pass
  • Management PAA, Management/Counselling = fail, History = pass → Global fail

This asymmetry is not arbitrary. It reflects the clinical reality that the AMC is testing a specific competency per station. A history-taking station is a test of history-taking ability; strong physical examination skills cannot substitute for deficient history-taking in that context.

Critical Key Points — The Non-Negotiable Performance Elements

Beyond the domain scoring and global rating framework, the AMC examination incorporates critical key points (also historically described in older marking frameworks as critical errors). These are specific, clinically essential actions, decisions, or pieces of information that the examiner's mark sheet identifies as mandatory for satisfactory performance. Missing a critical key point may result in a failing global rating even when all domain scores appear at passing level.

The AMC specification acknowledges that each station contains between two and five key steps that the candidate is expected to demonstrate, recorded as observed or not observed. The clinical significance of this structure is substantial:

  • A candidate who scores 5, 4, 4 across domains and misses a critical key point may still fail the station.
  • A candidate who scores 6, 5, 5, 5 across all domains but omits one clinically decisive step may fail the station.
  • These outcomes are not scoring anomalies; they reflect the intended design of the examination.

Clinical examples of critical key points:

  • DVT counselling station: Mandatory cessation of the combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP) following a confirmed episode of deep venous thrombosis. Failure to address COCP cessation constitutes a critical omission that will result in a failing global rating irrespective of otherwise thorough anticoagulation counselling.
  • Meningitis history station: Identifying neck stiffness, photophobia, and non-blanching rash as an emergency requiring immediate escalation.
  • Stroke or TIA presentation: Recognising and communicating the urgency of the presentation; initiating appropriate acute workup and neurology referral.

Critical key points are not always predictable from first principles, but they are consistently associated with:
1. Safety-critical clinical decisions (stopping a harmful medication, escalating an emergency)
2. Diagnostic pivots that alter management entirely
3. Mandatory investigations or referrals specific to the clinical context

The strategic implication is that preparation for any AMC station must include explicit identification of the two to five most clinically critical, non-negotiable steps for that station — the actions that, if missed, signal to the examiner that the candidate lacks the fundamental clinical competency the station is designed to assess.

Differential Diagnosis — The Foundation of Clinical Competency

Differential diagnosis is consistently the most common source of preventable failure across all station types in the AMC Clinical Examination. Analysis of candidate feedback across multiple examination cycles reveals a recurring pattern: candidates who perform well across history taking, examination, and communication domains nonetheless fail because of an inadequate, non-specific, or truncated differential diagnosis list.

Why differentials fail candidates:

  1. Non-specific terminology — Stating "infection" rather than "sinusitis," "meningitis," or "community-acquired pneumonia." Stating "tumour" rather than "non-small cell lung carcinoma," "renal cell carcinoma," or "hepatocellular carcinoma." The examiner's mark sheet lists specific diagnostic entities; broad categorical terms are not scored.

  2. Insufficient list length — The examiner's mark sheet for any given station typically contains six to eight specific differential diagnoses. A candidate who lists three differentials, even if all three appear on the mark sheet, cannot score as highly as one who lists six to seven. In a diagnostic formulation PAA station, a differential score of three out of seven possible points will almost certainly result in a failing global rating.

  3. Time exhaustion — Candidates who spend disproportionate time on history taking or physical examination frequently exhaust their allocated time before reaching the differential diagnosis task. Running out of time before completing the differential diagnosis list is a leading cause of otherwise capable candidates failing examination stations.

  4. Recall-only preparation — Candidates who have memorised two or three "classic" differentials for common presentations lack the breadth to score adequately. The examination is designed to differentiate candidates who understand pathophysiology and clinical reasoning from those who have memorised stereotyped answers.

Worked example — Dizziness in a 50-year-old man with left eyelid ptosis:

The examiner's mark sheet for this case would typically include the following specific differential diagnoses:
- Stroke / cerebrovascular accident (primary diagnosis, alongside TIA — note: both may attract a single combined score)
- Transient ischaemic attack (if not combined with stroke above)
- Labyrinthitis
- Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) — noting that this presentation has non-positional features that argue against it, but it remains a plausible differential for a dizzy patient
- Vestibular neuritis
- Migraine (vestibular migraine)
- Cerebral tumour / intracranial mass
- Multiple sclerosis

A candidate who lists only "stroke, TIA, and vertigo" has three differentials of which one is non-specific. A candidate who lists seven of the eight above, using precise diagnostic terminology, has maximally demonstrated differential diagnostic competency.

Worked example — Acute chest pain with ECG:

Minimum expected differential list, organised by life-threatening priority:
- Acute myocardial infarction (STEMI/NSTEMI)
- Pericarditis / myocarditis
- Pulmonary embolism
- Pneumothorax (tension or spontaneous)
- Aortic dissection
- Oesophageal rupture (Boerhaave syndrome)
- Unstable angina / stable angina exacerbation
- Heart failure with acute decompensation
- Lung malignancy (less acute but clinically relevant)

Candidates should organise their differential lists using a systematic framework (cardiovascular, respiratory, gastrointestinal, musculoskeletal, other) and rehearse them until they can be delivered rapidly and fluently under examination conditions.

Time Management — The Silent Determinant of Differential Diagnosis Performance

Time management in the AMC Clinical Examination face-to-face format is qualitatively different from time management in the online examination. In the face-to-face format, most standard cases carry three tasks and a single prompt time allocation. This means the candidate must internally partition the available time across history taking, physical examination, and differential diagnosis without external prompting. In the online format, physical examination was often presented as on-screen text, removing its time burden; in the face-to-face setting, the candidate must physically perform the examination and manage the time cost of doing so.

The critical time management failure pattern is consistent across candidate feedback:

The candidate performs a high-quality, thorough history (often exceeding the optimal two to two-and-a-half minutes), then performs a thorough physical examination, and arrives at the differential diagnosis task with insufficient time to articulate a complete list. Even a candidate with strong differential diagnosis knowledge cannot compensate for thirty seconds of available time.

In cases with five assessed tasks — which do occur in the AMC Clinical Examination — candidates must adopt an accelerated pace from the moment they read the station stem. A five-task station signals to the prepared candidate: compress each task, do not exceed the minimum necessary standard in any one area, and ensure all five domains receive attention before time expires.

The differential diagnosis is almost always the final task. Running out of time for the final task means delivering an incomplete performance in the domain that is most commonly the primary differentiator of pass and fail outcomes. The strategic response is to practise delivering differential diagnosis lists under strict time pressure, beginning with the most specific and clinically significant diagnoses and working through the list rapidly.

The Examiner's Global Rating Decision — The Borderline Candidate

When a candidate's performance is borderline — demonstrating neither clearly satisfactory nor clearly unsatisfactory competency — the examiner is required to make a binary decision: pass or fail. There is no partial pass, no borderline pass category, and no mechanism for conditional advancement. The examiner's reasoning in this moment draws on:

  • The overall impression of the candidate's clinical reasoning, structure, and organisation
  • Whether critical key points were demonstrated
  • The breadth and specificity of the differential diagnosis
  • Whether the candidate actively engaged with and addressed the patient's ideas, concerns, and expectations
  • Whether the candidate demonstrated a coherent line of clinical thinking throughout the encounter
  • Whether the candidate probed positive findings rather than eliciting them superficially and moving on

Factors that push a borderline candidate toward a pass:
- One additional, specific, clinically relevant differential
- A structured, logical sequence through the history rather than a disorganised scatter of questions
- Demonstrably addressing the patient's primary concern
- Tidying a three-point key point list by completing the third point clearly
- Maintaining calm, professional communication even when time pressure is evident

Factors that push a borderline candidate toward a fail:
- Non-specific differentials (categories rather than diagnoses)
- Running out of time before completing the differential diagnosis task
- Missing a critical key point specific to the clinical scenario
- Disorganised, reactive history taking without evident structure
- Failing to acknowledge or respond to patient distress or concerns during the encounter

The Approach to the Patient Domain — Accurate Weighting

The approach to the patient domain — encompassing interpersonal communication, empathy, rapport, active listening, and professionalism — is assessed at every station and contributes to the overall impression forming the global rating. However, it is important to calibrate the weight of this domain accurately.

No AMC Clinical Examination station has "approach to the patient" as its designated predominant assessment area. This means that, by definition, this domain is never the primary determinant of the global rating. A candidate who communicates warmly and empathically but fails to take an adequate history in a history PAA station will not be passed on the basis of communication skills. Conversely, a candidate who is clinically systematic but communicates with only adequate (not excellent) rapport, and who performs well in the PAA domain, may well pass the station.

The practical implication is not that communication skills are unimportant — they contribute to the overall examiner impression and are evaluated — but that they cannot substitute for clinical competency in the PAA domain, knowledge of key points, or the ability to generate a specific and broad differential diagnosis list. Candidates who invest disproportionate preparation time in "bedside manner" coaching at the expense of clinical knowledge and differential diagnosis preparation are misdirecting their preparation effort.

Red Flags — Must Not Miss

The following represent the critical clinical red flags that constitute key points in AMC Clinical Examination stations. Omission of these in the examination context is likely to result in a critical error and failing global rating:

  • Neurological deficit in a dizzy patient — Ptosis, diplopia, facial droop, limb weakness, or ataxia in association with dizziness mandates immediate stroke protocol activation (CT brain, neurology referral, thrombolysis consideration within the treatment window); do not attribute to peripheral vestibular cause without excluding central pathology.

  • Haemodynamic instability in chest pain — Hypotension, diaphoresis, tachycardia, and pallor accompanying chest pain indicate a cardiovascular emergency (STEMI, aortic dissection, massive PE, tension pneumothorax); immediate resuscitation and specialist involvement is mandatory.

  • Signs of meningism — Neck stiffness, photophobia, non-blanching purpuric rash, and altered consciousness constitute a medical emergency; empirical antibiotics must not be delayed pending lumbar puncture.

  • Failure to cease causative medications — In DVT counselling, failure to counsel the patient to cease the combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP) immediately is a critical error. Similarly, in appropriate clinical contexts, cessation of antithrombotic agents or anticoagulants must be addressed explicitly.

  • Signs of raised intracranial pressure — Headache that is worst on waking, progressive, or associated with vomiting and visual changes requires urgent neuroimaging; do not dismiss as tension headache without adequate red flag screen.

  • Sudden severe headache — "Thunderclap" headache or "worst headache of life" mandates subarachnoid haemorrhage exclusion (CT head then lumbar puncture if CT negative); this is a non-negotiable critical key point in any headache station.

  • Functional decline in a cancer patient — New pain, unexpected weight loss, night sweats, or change in bowel/bladder function in a patient with known malignancy requires urgent oncology review and reassessment for disease progression or new complication.

  • Cardiac sounding chest pain in women and diabetic patients — Atypical presentations (dyspnoea, fatigue, nausea without classic chest pressure) must not lead to dismissal of an acute coronary syndrome; these populations frequently present atypically, and omission of this consideration is clinically and examinationally significant.

Investigations

For AMC Clinical Examination stations in which investigations are an assessed task or are clinically relevant to the case:

Bedside:
- 12-lead ECG (mandatory in all chest pain, palpitation, syncope, and unexplained dyspnoea presentations)
- Blood glucose level (BGL) — altered consciousness, first seizure, dizziness
- Urinalysis — abdominal pain, flank pain, suspected urinary tract pathology
- Pulse oximetry — respiratory and cardiac presentations
- Orthostatic blood pressure measurements — dizziness, syncope

Bloods:
- Full blood count (FBC) — anaemia, infection, haematological malignancy
- Urea, electrolytes, and creatinine (UEC) — renal function, fluid status
- Liver function tests (LFTs) — right upper quadrant pain, jaundice, alcohol-related presentations
- Troponin (high-sensitivity) — chest pain, possible ACS; repeat at 3 hours
- D-dimer — low-to-intermediate probability PE (Wells criteria guided)
- C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) — infection, inflammation
- Thyroid function tests (TFTs) — palpitations, weight change, fatigue
- Coagulation studies — bleeding, anticoagulation monitoring, suspected PE
- Lipid profile and HbA1c — cardiovascular risk assessment

Imaging:
- CT head (non-contrast) — acute headache, suspected stroke or haemorrhage
- CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) — moderate-to-high probability pulmonary embolism
- Chest X-ray — respiratory and cardiac presentations, suspected pneumothorax, aortic dissection
- Duplex ultrasound of lower limbs — suspected deep vein thrombosis
- CT abdomen and pelvis — abdominal pain with diagnostic uncertainty
- MRI brain — posterior fossa pathology, suspected multiple sclerosis, equivocal CT

Specialist Referral:
- Neurology — stroke, TIA, multiple sclerosis, complex headache
- Cardiology — ACS, heart failure, significant arrhythmia, structural heart disease
- Haematology — haematological malignancy, complex anticoagulation
- Respiratory / Thoracic surgery — PE, pneumothorax, lung malignancy
- General surgery — surgical abdominal emergencies

Management

Management responses in AMC Clinical Examination stations must be systematic, demonstrating that the candidate understands both the immediate clinical priorities and the longer-term management framework. The following principles apply across station types:

Immediate management (acute presentations):
- Address any immediate life-threatening concern (airway, breathing, circulation, disability)
- Initiate appropriate monitoring (cardiac monitor, pulse oximetry, IV access where indicated)
- Commence empirical treatment where delay is clinically unsafe (antibiotics for suspected meningitis; aspirin and anticoagulation for STEMI)
- Escalate to appropriate specialist immediately when the condition exceeds primary care management

Intermediate management:
- Confirm diagnosis with appropriate investigations before committing to definitive treatment where time permits
- Initiate condition-specific pharmacotherapy with attention to contraindications (e.g., COCP must be ceased in DVT before commencing anticoagulation counselling)
- Arrange appropriate imaging and specialist consultation within a timeframe commensurate with clinical urgency

Ongoing management:
- Duration of treatment (e.g., three to six months of anticoagulation for provoked DVT; indefinite for unprovoked or recurrent DVT)
- Lifestyle modification counselling (smoking cessation, weight management, physical activity, dietary modification for cardiovascular risk reduction)
- Medication adherence counselling and common side effect education
- Monitoring parameters and follow-up schedule
- Specialist outpatient referral where ongoing supervision is required

Communication and Patient Education

Communication competency in the AMC Clinical Examination is assessed across all station types through the approach to the patient domain, and as the predominant assessment area in counselling stations. The following framework applies:

Opening the consultation:
Introduce by name and role, confirm the patient's identity and preferred form of address, establish an open and non-threatening consultation environment. In counselling stations, begin with an open invitation: "I understand you have some questions today — please tell me what's on your mind," or "I have some information to share with you and I'd like to make sure we have time to address any concerns you have."

Eliciting ideas, concerns, and expectations (ICE):
- Ideas: "What do you think might be causing your symptoms?" / "Has anyone mentioned a possible diagnosis to you?"
- Concerns: "Is there something specific you've been worried about?" / "What concerns you most about what's been happening?"
- Expectations: "What were you hoping we could do today?" / "Is there a particular outcome you were hoping for from this consultation?"

ICE elicitation must precede information giving in any communication station. Providing information without first understanding the patient's perspective is a communication error that will be noted by the examiner.

Information giving:
- Use plain language calibrated to the patient's apparent health literacy
- Introduce the diagnosis with empathy before discussing management: "I need to share some important findings with you, and I want you to know I'll be with you every step of the way."
- Use the chunking and checking technique: provide a segment of information, then pause and check understanding before proceeding
- Avoid medical jargon; where technical terms are unavoidable, define them immediately
- Use visual aids, diagrams, or written summaries where available

Shared decision making:
- Offer treatment options where clinically appropriate, explaining the benefits and risks of each
- Invite the patient's preference while providing a clear clinical recommendation
- Confirm the patient's understanding and agreement before finalising the management plan

Addressing specific patient concerns:
- Acknowledge the concern explicitly before responding to it: "That's a very important concern and I'm glad you've raised it."
- Respond directly and honestly, avoiding minimisation or false reassurance
- For serious diagnoses, allow silence and emotional space before proceeding with medical information

AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

  • Identify the predominant assessment area immediately upon reading the station stem. Direct at least 60 percent of your cognitive effort toward ensuring excellence in the PAA domain. A brilliant history in an examination PAA station does not compensate for poor examination technique.

  • Prepare an explicit critical key points list for every common AMC station type. For DVT: cease COCP. For meningitis: do not delay antibiotics. For chest pain: perform and interpret ECG. Know your case-specific non-negotiables before entering the examination room.

  • Never list a broad category as a differential diagnosis. "Infection," "cancer," "tumour," and "inflammatory condition" are not differential diagnoses — they are categories. Every differential you name must be a specific diagnostic entity (e.g., "community-acquired pneumonia," "hepatocellular carcinoma," "Crohn's disease").

  • Prepare differential diagnosis lists that are longer than you expect to need. The examiner's mark sheet typically contains six to eight specific diagnoses. If you can only name three, you cannot score above three out of eight, regardless of how accurate your primary diagnosis is. Prepare eight to ten condition-specific differentials for every common presentation cluster.

  • Manage time actively, not reactively. In a three-task station, allocate approximately two to two-and-a-half minutes to history taking, two minutes to physical examination, and ensure a minimum of ninety seconds to two minutes for differential diagnosis. In a five-task station, reduce every task allocation and commit to speed without sacrificing structure.

  • The differential diagnosis task is almost always last — protect its time allocation. If you sense you are running over time in history taking or examination, deliberately compress. Arriving at differential diagnosis with thirty seconds remaining and delivering two incomplete diagnoses is one of the most common and preventable causes of station failure.

  • Structure is not a stylistic preference — it is a scoring mechanism. A structured history (presenting complaint, SOCRATES or equivalent systematic exploration, associated symptoms, relevant negatives, past medical history, medications, allergies, family history, social history, systems review) signals to the examiner a coherent, reproducible clinical reasoning process. An unstructured, reactive history signals the opposite.

  • The approach to the patient domain matters at the margin, not at the centre. Empathy and communication skills contribute to the examiner's overall impression and to borderline candidate outcomes. However, they cannot substitute for clinical competency in the PAA, completion of key points, or an adequate differential diagnosis list. Allocate preparation time proportionately.

  • Understand that the global rating is the examiner's holistic clinical impression, not an arithmetic calculation. A candidate who scores 5, 4, 4, 3 across domains and misses a critical key point may still fail. A candidate who scores 4, 4, 3, 4 but performs clearly in the PAA and demonstrates good clinical reasoning may still pass. Optimise for the examiner's overall impression of clinical competency, not for individual domain averages.

  • Practise differential diagnosis delivery at speed under time pressure. The examination environment introduces cognitive load that degrades recall. Practise saying eight differentials for common presentations aloud, rapidly, using correct medical terminology, until the list is automatic and does not require active effortful retrieval during the station.

  • Never assume a station is a pilot station. Pilot stations are indistinguishable from scored stations. Candidates who mentally classify a station as "probably a pilot" and reduce their performance effort risk failing a scored station. Treat all 16 stations with identical commitment.

  • Borderline performances are resolved by the examiner in favour of whichever impression is marginally stronger. The single most reliable way to shift a borderline performance toward a pass is to add one more specific, clinically relevant differential; complete the final task before time expires; and demonstrate explicit engagement with the patient's primary concern. These are the three most commonly cited differentiating factors in examiner decision making at the borderline.

Safety Net

The concept of safety netting in the AMC Clinical Examination applies both to clinical management within a station scenario and to candidate examination strategy. In clinical communication stations, safety netting must be provided explicitly to the patient before the consultation concludes.

Clinical safety netting for patients in AMC station scenarios:

  • Provide the patient with clear, specific instructions about symptoms that should trigger immediate return to medical care. Avoid generic instructions such as "come back if you're worried" — instead specify: "If you develop severe headache, weakness on one side of your body, difficulty speaking, or chest pain, you must call 000 immediately."

  • Provide a specific follow-up appointment or timeframe: "I'd like to see you again in two weeks to review your blood results and assess how you are responding to treatment."

  • Provide written information where available, particularly for complex diagnoses, new medications, or significant lifestyle modifications.

  • Confirm the patient's understanding of the safety net instructions using the teach-back method: "Just to make sure I've explained this clearly, could you tell me in your own words which symptoms would mean you need to come back urgently?"

  • In mental health stations or consultations involving significant distress, ensure the patient has access to crisis support resources: "If you find yourself feeling overwhelmed or having thoughts of harming yourself, please contact Lifeline on 13 11 14 immediately, or present to your nearest emergency department."

Candidate examination safety netting — strategic preparation principles:

  • If you complete a station early, use remaining time to expand your differential diagnosis list or to provide more detailed management counselling — do not exit the station early.
  • If you run out of time before completing all tasks, prioritise completing the differential diagnosis task even partially over perfect completion of any other task — an incomplete differential is preferable to no differential.
  • If the examiner appears dissatisfied or provides limited role-player responses, do not disengage from the clinical task — maintain structure, continue through your key points, and deliver your differential diagnosis regardless of environmental cues.
  • After a difficult station, reset completely before entering the next station. The global rating of each station is independent; a poor performance in one station does not affect scoring in another.
