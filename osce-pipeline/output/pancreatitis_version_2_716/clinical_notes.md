# Acute Pancreatitis — History Taking Station with Investigation Interpretation

## Clinical Presentation

A 25-year-old male presents to the emergency department with upper abdominal pain. Unlike a standard history-taking station, the examination stem provides pre-ordered investigation results including an elevated serum lipase, a raised white cell count, and mildly elevated liver transaminases. The candidate's task is tripartite: conduct a structured, systematic history; interpret and explain the provided investigations in plain language; and communicate a diagnosis and differential diagnoses to the patient. This station design is deliberately constrained — the elevated lipase effectively pre-confirms acute pancreatitis as the working diagnosis — and the examiner's scoring emphasis shifts accordingly.

The clinical importance of this station lies not in the diagnostic uncertainty that characterises most history-taking stations, but in its demand for aetiological precision. Because the diagnosis is effectively established by the investigation results, the candidate who scores highest is the one who can methodically enumerate and clinically explore the known causes of acute pancreatitis. In a 25-year-old male presenting with an acute episode, gallstones and alcohol are the two most probable aetiologies, and the history must systematically evaluate both, alongside the less common but clinically important causes. Failing to do this — instead spending the station pursuing broad abdominal pain differentials — is the most common reason candidates underperform in this station type.

This station is also clinically important because it tests the candidate's ability to integrate investigation results into a coherent clinical narrative and communicate that narrative accessibly. Patients presenting with acute pancreatitis are often frightened, in pain, and unfamiliar with pancreatic anatomy and physiology. The examiner assesses not only clinical knowledge but the candidate's capacity to translate laboratory values and pathophysiology into language a lay person can understand, without sacrificing accuracy or appropriate clinical gravity.

---

## Station Framework — What the Examiner Expects

The examiner for this station will be assessing across three domains: history taking (heavily weighted at 11 marks), communication (5 marks), and a brief physical examination acknowledgement (1 mark). The candidate must demonstrate a structured, systematic approach that does not collapse into a disorganised interrogation of symptoms, nor into premature closure around the pre-provided diagnosis.

The recommended framework for this station is as follows:

**Opening:** Introduce yourself professionally, confirm the patient's identity, establish rapport, and acknowledge that the patient is unwell. A brief statement of purpose — "I'd like to ask you some questions about your pain, and then I'll go through your test results with you and explain what we think is happening" — sets expectations and demonstrates communication competence from the outset.

**Systematic History:** Begin with SOCRATES to characterise the presenting pain fully, then transition to a systematic aetiological screen for causes of acute pancreatitis. This is the dominant scoring domain. Move efficiently through alcohol use, gallstone risk factors, medications, metabolic causes, and recent infections or procedures.

**ICE Elicitation:** Ideas, Concerns, and Expectations must be formally explored. In a young man with acute pancreatitis, concerns about recurrence, alcohol dependency, long-term pancreatic insufficiency, and malignancy are common and clinically appropriate.

**Investigation Explanation:** Translate the white cell count, lipase, and liver transaminases into accessible language. Explain the mild transaminase elevation in the context of pancreatitis — shared ductal anatomy — rather than allowing the patient to interpret this as a separate liver disease.

**Diagnosis and Differential Communication:** Deliver the diagnosis of acute pancreatitis clearly and compassionately. Provide a brief, structured list of alternative diagnoses that were considered. Outline the clinical and investigative reasoning behind the working diagnosis.

**Closing and Safety Netting:** Summarise the plan, confirm the patient's understanding, address remaining concerns, and provide explicit red flag advice for deterioration.

## Systematic Approach

### SOCRATES Pain Assessment

A thorough SOCRATES assessment remains the essential starting point, even when the diagnosis is pre-suggested by investigation results. The history anchors the diagnosis to the clinical narrative and identifies aetiological clues.

**Site:** "Where exactly is the pain? Can you point to it with one finger?" — Acute pancreatitis classically produces epigastric pain, often with a central upper abdominal distribution. Pain localised to the right upper quadrant favours cholelithiasis or cholecystitis; left upper quadrant pain may suggest splenic pathology.

**Onset:** "Did the pain come on suddenly or gradually? What were you doing when it started?" — Acute pancreatitis typically has a subacute onset over hours, often following a large meal or alcohol intake. Sudden onset, maximal-at-onset pain raises the critical differential of a perforated viscus or a vascular catastrophe.

**Character:** "How would you describe the pain — is it sharp, dull, crampy, burning?" — Pancreatic pain is typically described as a deep, dull, or boring pain. Colicky pain suggests biliary or ureteric obstruction. Burning epigastric pain favours peptic ulcer disease or gastro-oesophageal reflux disease.

**Radiation:** "Does the pain travel anywhere — to your back, your shoulder, your chest?" — Radiation to the back in a band-like or penetrating pattern is a hallmark of acute pancreatitis, reflecting retroperitoneal inflammation. Radiation to the right shoulder tip raises concern for diaphragmatic irritation from biliary or hepatic pathology.

**Associated symptoms:** "Do you have nausea or vomiting? Fevers or chills? Jaundice — any yellowing of the skin or eyes? Changes in your bowel habits or the colour of your stools or urine?" — Nausea and vomiting are near-universal in acute pancreatitis. Jaundice, dark urine, and pale stools suggest biliary obstruction and must be explored carefully, as they may indicate choledocholithiasis or, in the differential, malignancy of the pancreatic head or bile duct.

**Timing:** "How long have you had this pain? Is it constant or does it come and go?" — Acute pancreatitis pain is typically constant, differentiating it from the episodic biliary colic of uncomplicated cholelithiasis.

**Exacerbating and relieving factors:** "Does anything make the pain better or worse — lying flat, leaning forward, eating, drinking?" — A characteristic and examiner-pleasing feature of acute pancreatitis is relief on leaning forward (reducing retroperitoneal tension) and worsening on lying supine. Pain worsening after fatty meals is more consistent with biliary disease.

**Severity:** "On a scale of zero to ten, with zero being no pain and ten being the worst imaginable, where would you rate your pain?" — Severity guides triage and analgesia urgency. Severe pain (7–10/10) in acute pancreatitis is typical and should prompt immediate analgesia and senior review.

### Aetiological History — Causes of Acute Pancreatitis

This section carries the highest clinical weight in this station. Because the lipase confirms pancreatitis, the examiner is specifically testing whether the candidate can systematically evaluate the recognised causes. The mnemonic **I GET SMASHED** provides a reliable aetiological framework:

**I — Idiopathic:** After thorough assessment, approximately 10–20% of cases have no identifiable cause. This is a diagnosis of exclusion and should not be volunteered early.

**G — Gallstones (cholelithiasis):** This is the most common cause of acute pancreatitis in the general population, and must be specifically explored in a 25-year-old.
- "Have you ever been told you have gallstones or had problems with your gallbladder?"
- "Have you had similar episodes of pain before, particularly after fatty meals?"
- "Any recent jaundice, pale stools, or dark urine?"
- Gallstone pancreatitis is suggested by elevated ALP and bilirubin, right upper quadrant tenderness, and a history of biliary colic.

**E — Ethanol (alcohol):** This is the most common cause in young adults and must be explored with sensitivity but directness.
- "I need to ask about alcohol use — how much do you drink in a typical week? Have you had a heavier drinking occasion recently, such as in the last 24–48 hours?"
- A binge episode of heavy drinking is a classic precipitant, and in a 25-year-old male, this aetiology must be placed at the top of the differential for causes.
- Use a validated screening approach: AUDIT-C, or simply ask about quantity, frequency, and any recent escalation.
- Avoid judgemental framing; normalise the question as routine clinical assessment.

**T — Trauma:** Blunt abdominal trauma, including that sustained in motor vehicle accidents or contact sport, can cause direct pancreatic injury.
- "Have you had any injury to your abdomen recently — a fall, an accident, or contact sport?"

**S — Steroids:** Corticosteroid use is a recognised iatrogenic cause.
- "Are you taking any medications regularly, including steroids such as prednisolone or cortisone?"

**M — Mumps and other infections:** Viral pancreatitis — caused by mumps, Coxsackievirus B, CMV, or EBV — is a relevant consideration in a young, unimmunised patient.
- "Have you had any recent viral illness — swollen glands, fever, sore throat?"
- "Are you up to date with your vaccinations, including MMR?"

**A — Autoimmune:** Autoimmune pancreatitis (AIP Type 1, associated with IgG4-related disease) typically presents in older adults but should be considered in refractory cases.

**S — Scorpion stings and Snake venom:** Relevant in tropical Australian and remote areas; enquire about recent travel or outdoor activity.

**H — Hyperlipidaemia and Hypercalcaemia:** Severe hypertriglyceridaemia (>11 mmol/L) is a well-recognised metabolic cause of pancreatitis. Hypercalcaemia from hyperparathyroidism, malignancy, or sarcoidosis can also precipitate it.
- "Has anyone ever told you your cholesterol or triglycerides are high? Any history of kidney stones, bone pain, or calcium problems?"

**E — ERCP (post-procedural):** Post-ERCP pancreatitis is iatrogenic; relevant if the patient has undergone recent endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography.
- "Have you had any recent procedures involving a camera or scope down your throat into your stomach or bile ducts?"

**D — Drugs:** Numerous medications are associated with drug-induced pancreatitis, including azathioprine, valproate, thiazide diuretics, metronidazole, and tetracyclines.
- "What medications are you currently taking, including any over-the-counter medicines, herbal preparations, or supplements?"

### Past Medical, Family, and Social History

**Past medical history:**
- Prior episodes of pancreatitis (risk of chronic pancreatitis with recurrent episodes)
- Known gallbladder disease, peptic ulcer disease, or inflammatory bowel disease
- Diabetes mellitus or metabolic syndrome
- Known malignancy

**Medications and allergies:** Full medication reconciliation as described under aetiological causes above. Document allergies and adverse drug reactions.

**Family history:**
- Hereditary pancreatitis (mutations in PRSS1, SPINK1, or CFTR genes) should be explored if the patient has recurrent episodes or a positive family history
- Familial hypertriglyceridaemia
- Pancreatic or gastrointestinal malignancy

**Social history:**
- Alcohol and recreational drug use (IVDU is associated with hepatitis and indirect pancreatitis risk)
- Smoking history (independent risk factor for chronic pancreatitis and pancreatic cancer)
- Occupation and social support — particularly relevant for follow-up compliance and alcohol cessation support
- Diet, specifically high-fat diet and relevance to hypertriglyceridaemia

### ICE — Ideas, Concerns, and Expectations

ICE must be formally elicited, not token-gestured. In a young adult with acute pancreatitis and elevated liver enzymes, the following ICE domains are particularly important:

**Ideas:** "What do you think might be causing your pain?" — Young patients may attribute their symptoms to muscle strain, food poisoning, or gastritis. Understanding their baseline concept guides how you frame the diagnosis.

**Concerns:** "Is there anything in particular that's worrying you about this?" — Common and clinically valid concerns include:
- Fear of malignancy ("Could this be cancer of the pancreas?")
- Concern about the liver enzyme elevation ("Does this mean my liver is damaged?")
- Fear of diabetes if the pancreas is damaged
- Concern about the implications of a diagnosis linked to alcohol use — social stigma, employment, and relationships
- Recurrence risk and long-term prognosis

**Expectations:** "What were you hoping we might be able to do for you today?" — Some patients expect immediate discharge once pain is controlled; others are anxious and expect extensive workup. Aligning management with patient expectations improves adherence and reduces dissatisfaction.

## Differential Diagnosis

| Category | Diagnosis | Key Distinguishing Features |
|----------|-----------|----------------------------|
| Common | Acute pancreatitis (alcohol-induced) | Epigastric pain radiating to back, history of binge drinking, elevated lipase >3× ULN, leaning-forward relief |
| Common | Acute pancreatitis (gallstone-induced) | Colicky onset, post-fatty meal, elevated ALP/bilirubin, stones on ultrasound |
| Common | Acute cholecystitis | Right upper quadrant pain, Murphy's sign positive, fever, mildly elevated ALP, ultrasound findings |
| Common | Peptic ulcer disease | Epigastric pain, may be relieved by antacids, endoscopic confirmation, H. pylori testing |
| Common | Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease | Burning retrosternal and epigastric pain, worse post-prandially, no fever, lipase normal |
| Important | Choledocholithiasis / cholangitis | Charcot's triad (jaundice, fever, RUQ pain), obstructive LFTs, dilated CBD on imaging |
| Important | Hepatitis (viral or alcoholic) | Marked transaminase elevation (>5–10× ULN), positive viral serology, jaundice, hepatomegaly |
| Important | Peptic ulcer perforation | Sudden onset severe pain, peritonism, free air on erect CXR — surgical emergency |
| Important | Acute coronary syndrome | Central/epigastric pain, diaphoresis, ECG changes, troponin elevation — must not be missed |
| Important | Pneumonia (lower lobe) | Pleuritic pain referred to upper abdomen, fever, cough, consolidation on CXR |
| Must Not Miss | Pancreatic carcinoma | Weight loss, painless jaundice, back pain, older age, CA 19-9 elevation, CT/MRCP findings |
| Must Not Miss | Aortic dissection or ruptured AAA | Tearing pain radiating to back, haemodynamic instability, pulsatile mass, CT angiography |
| Must Not Miss | Mesenteric ischaemia | Severe pain out of proportion to examination, AF or vascular risk factors, elevated lactate |

## Red Flags — Must Not Miss

- **Haemodynamic instability (tachycardia, hypotension)** — Indicates severe pancreatitis with systemic inflammatory response or haemorrhagic pancreatitis (Vascular involvement of splenic or mesenteric vessels). Requires immediate ICU-level management and urgent senior review. Classified as severe pancreatitis by Atlanta Criteria.

- **Signs of peritonism (rigid abdomen, guarding, rebound tenderness)** — Raises concern for perforation of a viscus (perforated duodenal ulcer, perforated peptic ulcer) as a comorbid or alternative diagnosis. Requires surgical consultation and erect chest X-ray urgently.

- **Jaundice with fever and rigors (Charcot's triad)** — Suggests ascending cholangitis, which carries high mortality if untreated. Mandates urgent biliary decompression (ERCP) and broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics. Life-threatening if Reynolds' pentad (addition of confusion and shock) develops.

- **Unexplained weight loss, anorexia, or new-onset diabetes mellitus** — In the context of upper abdominal pain and an elevated lipase, these features raise concern for pancreatic carcinoma presenting with obstructive or mass-effect pancreatitis. CT pancreas with contrast and specialist referral required.

- **Grey Turner's sign (flank bruising) or Cullen's sign (periumbilical bruising)** — Ecchymosis indicating retroperitoneal haemorrhage from haemorrhagic pancreatitis. Rare but indicates severe, necrotising pancreatitis. Critical care escalation required.

- **Severe hypertriglyceridaemia (serum triglycerides >11 mmol/L)** — Requires specific treatment (insulin infusion, plasmapheresis in refractory cases, gemfibrozil) in addition to pancreatitis management. Recurrence prevention depends on lipid management.

- **Markedly elevated liver transaminases (>5–10× upper limit of normal)** — In the context of pancreatitis, this level of transaminase elevation should raise concern for a hepatocellular process (acute viral hepatitis, alcoholic hepatitis) rather than the mild reactive elevation expected with uncomplicated pancreatitis. Requires viral hepatitis serology and hepatology review.

- **Confusion or altered mental state** — May indicate Wernicke's encephalopathy in a patient with heavy alcohol use, septic encephalopathy in complicated pancreatitis, or hypoglycaemia from pancreatic endocrine dysfunction. Urgent assessment required.

- **Inability to tolerate any oral intake with clinical dehydration** — Indicates requirement for hospital admission and intravenous fluid resuscitation. Outpatient management is inappropriate.

## Investigations

**Bedside:**
- Vital signs and early warning score (HR, BP, RR, SpO₂, temperature, GCS)
- Urine dipstick (glycosuria may suggest pancreatic endocrine involvement; haematuria may suggest renal calculi as a coexisting cause of flank pain)
- Urinary beta-hCG in females of reproductive age (to exclude ectopic pregnancy in the differential for acute abdominal pain)
- Blood glucose level (BSL) — pancreatic dysfunction may impair insulin secretion
- ECG — exclude acute coronary syndrome in the differential for epigastric pain

**Bloods:**
- Full blood count (FBC) — leucocytosis confirms systemic inflammatory response; haemoconcentration (haematocrit >44%) is a marker of severity in Atlanta Criteria
- Urea, electrolytes, and creatinine (UEC) — assess renal function and hydration status; elevated urea (BUN >7.14 mmol/L) is a prognostic marker
- Liver function tests (LFTs) including bilirubin, ALP, ALT, AST, GGT — differentiate gallstone pancreatitis (ALP and bilirubin elevation) from alcoholic pancreatitis (GGT and AST disproportionate elevation), and assess severity of transaminase elevation
- Serum lipase — gold standard enzyme marker; elevation >3× upper limit of normal is diagnostic in appropriate clinical context (note: amylase is less specific and less sensitive)
- Calcium (hypercalcaemia as aetiology)
- Serum triglycerides (hypertriglyceridaemia as aetiology, particularly if lipid-induced pancreatitis suspected)
- C-reactive protein (CRP) — elevated CRP >150 mg/L at 48 hours is the strongest single biochemical predictor of severe pancreatitis
- Coagulation screen (APTT, PT/INR) — if clinical concern about haemorrhagic or DIC complication
- Blood cultures — if fever present, to exclude infected necrosis or cholangitis
- Glucose — hyperglycaemia indicates pancreatic endocrine compromise

**Imaging:**
- Transabdominal ultrasound (first-line imaging) — assess for gallstones, biliary dilation, CBD diameter, cholecystitis; note that the pancreas is often poorly visualised in the acute setting due to overlying bowel gas
- Erect chest X-ray (CXR) — exclude free air under the diaphragm (perforation), pleural effusions (left-sided pleural effusion is a recognised complication of acute pancreatitis)
- CT abdomen with intravenous contrast (CT pancreas protocol) — indicated if diagnosis uncertain, clinical deterioration, or suspected necrotising pancreatitis; should be performed 48–72 hours after onset for optimal assessment of pancreatic necrosis (CT Severity Index / Balthazar Score)
- MRCP (Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography) — preferred non-invasive imaging of the biliary tree and pancreatic duct, particularly to detect choledocholithiasis and pancreatic duct disruption, avoiding radiation
- Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) — indicated for suspected microlithiasis not detected on standard ultrasound, or to assess pancreatic duct anatomy in recurrent pancreatitis

**Specialist:**
- Gastroenterology or hepatobiliary surgery consultation for severe, complicated, or recurrent cases
- ERCP — therapeutic intervention for choledocholithiasis-associated pancreatitis with biliary obstruction; performed within 24–72 hours in cases of concurrent cholangitis
- Dietitian referral for nutritional support, alcohol cessation counselling integration
- Alcohol and Drug Service (ADS) referral if alcohol aetiology confirmed

## Management

### Acute In-Hospital Management

**Resuscitation and monitoring:**
- Intravenous fluid resuscitation with crystalloid (Hartmann's solution preferred over normal saline; evidence supports lactated Ringer's reducing SIRS and necrosis risk in acute pancreatitis): 250–500 mL/hour initially, titrated to urine output >0.5 mL/kg/hour
- Continuous vital sign monitoring with 4-hourly observations minimum; ICU admission for organ dysfunction (respiratory, renal, or haemodynamic failure)
- Nil by mouth in the acute phase if nausea, vomiting, or ileus is present; early enteral nutrition (nasojejunal feeding within 24–48 hours) preferred over total parenteral nutrition for moderate-to-severe pancreatitis — reduces infective complications and mortality

**Analgesia:**
- Adequate analgesia is a priority and does not mask the diagnosis once established
- IV morphine or hydromorphone (Patient-Controlled Analgesia in severe cases); avoid NSAIDs initially given renal impairment risk in dehydrated patients
- Antiemetics: ondansetron or metoclopramide for nausea management

**Aetiological management:**
- Gallstone pancreatitis: cholecystectomy should be performed during the same hospital admission (or within 2–4 weeks) to prevent recurrence; ERCP if concurrent bile duct obstruction or cholangitis
- Alcohol-induced pancreatitis: alcohol cessation counselling, thiamine supplementation (Wernicke's prophylaxis), referral to Alcohol and Drug Services; AUDIT-C or CAGE screening for dependency
- Hypertriglyceridaemia: insulin infusion (drives triglycerides into adipose tissue), low-fat diet, fibrate therapy on discharge
- Drug-induced pancreatitis: cease the offending agent

**Complications to anticipate and manage:**
- Pancreatic necrosis: sterile (conservative management) vs. infected (requires minimally invasive or surgical debridement — step-up approach)
- Pseudocyst formation: most resolve spontaneously; EUS-guided drainage if symptomatic or enlarging
- Pancreatic fistula, splenic vein thrombosis, splenic artery pseudoaneurysm — specialist-guided management
- Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS): early HDU/ICU, lung-protective ventilation
- Acute kidney injury (AKI): aggressive fluid resuscitation, nephrology review for renal replacement therapy if required

### Ongoing and Outpatient Management

- Follow-up with gastroenterology at 4–6 weeks post-discharge
- Repeat transabdominal ultrasound if gallstones not excluded in-hospital
- Fasting lipid profile and triglycerides in convalescence
- Monitoring for development of exocrine insufficiency (steatorrhoea, fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies) and endocrine insufficiency (new-onset diabetes mellitus)
- Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) if exocrine insufficiency develops
- Long-term alcohol cessation support and mental health review as indicated

## Communication and Patient Education

### Explaining Investigation Results

The candidate must translate laboratory results into accessible, non-technical language without being dismissive or overly simplistic. A structured approach:

**White cell count:** *"Your white blood cells are elevated. These are cells that form part of your immune system. When your body has inflammation — which is when tissue becomes irritated or injured — your white blood cell count rises as part of the response. This is what we are seeing here."*

**Lipase:** *"Your lipase level is elevated. Lipase is an enzyme — a type of protein — that is produced by your pancreas. The pancreas is an organ that sits behind your stomach and helps digest the food you eat. When the pancreas becomes inflamed, it releases more lipase into your bloodstream than normal, and that is what your test is showing."*

**Mildly elevated liver enzymes:** *"Your liver enzymes are also slightly elevated. I want to reassure you that this is a very common finding when the pancreas is inflamed, because the pancreas and liver share connected drainage channels. A mild rise like this does not mean your liver is diseased or damaged — it is a secondary effect of the pancreatic inflammation. We will monitor this closely to make sure it settles."*

### Delivering the Diagnosis

Use the SPIKES or equivalent structured communication model:
- **Signpost:** "I'd like to go through your results with you and tell you what we think is going on."
- **Deliver clearly:** "Based on your symptoms and investigations, we believe you have a condition called acute pancreatitis — this means your pancreas has become acutely inflamed."
- **Explain the reasoning:** "The reasons I'm confident about this diagnosis are: your pain is in the upper abdomen and travels to your back, it is worse when you lie flat and better when you lean forward, you have been nauseous and vomiting, and importantly, your lipase — the enzyme produced by your pancreas — is significantly elevated."
- **Acknowledge uncertainty:** "We always consider other possibilities, and I'll share those with you, but based on everything together, pancreatitis is the most likely explanation."

### Addressing Concerns About Alcohol

Approach alcohol as a clinical risk factor, not a moral failing. Use motivational interviewing principles:
- "I'm not here to judge — I just need to understand this so we can give you the best care and reduce the risk of this happening again."
- Explain clearly that recurrent alcohol-related pancreatitis carries a high risk of progression to chronic pancreatitis, endocrine failure (diabetes mellitus), and significantly elevated risk of pancreatic cancer.
- Provide written information and refer to Alcohol and Drug Services before discharge.

### Explaining Differentials

*"When I first assessed you, I was thinking about a number of possible causes for your pain — including problems with your gallbladder, inflammation of your liver, a stomach ulcer, acid reflux, and even in rare cases a heart condition. Now that we have your test results, and putting them together with everything you've told me, I'm confident the diagnosis is pancreatitis."*

## AMC Exam Tips — High-Yield Points

- **Prioritise aetiological history over differential diagnosis in this station type.** When the stem pre-provides investigation results confirming pancreatitis, the examiner is specifically testing your knowledge of the I GET SMASHED mnemonic. Spending the majority of your history on broad abdominal pain differentials will cost you marks.

- **Explore alcohol use directly and non-judgementally.** A vague question about "do you drink?" is insufficient. Ask about quantity, frequency, binge episodes, recent escalation, and dependence symptoms (morning drinking, withdrawal symptoms). This is the most likely aetiology in a 25-year-old male.

- **Gallstones must be specifically asked about.** Do not assume alcohol is the only aetiology. Ask about prior biliary colic, fatty food intolerance, and jaundice. Gallstone pancreatitis is the most common aetiology overall and requires same-admission cholecystectomy to prevent recurrence.

- **Do not over-interpret mildly elevated transaminases as hepatitis.** A hepatocellular injury pattern requires transaminase elevation greater than 5–10 times the upper limit of normal. Mild transaminase elevation (less than 2–3 times normal) in the context of confirmed pancreatitis represents a reactive, secondary process due to shared biliary-pancreatic ductal anatomy, not a primary hepatic disease.

- **Use plain language when explaining investigations.** Saying "your white cell count is elevated at 14 with a left shift suggesting neutrophilia" will immediately disengage a lay patient and lose communication marks. Translate every result into one or two simple sentences.

- **Structure your investigation explanation before delivering the diagnosis.** Explain each abnormal result, then synthesise them into the clinical picture. This logical sequencing demonstrates both communication skill and clinical reasoning.

- **Lean-forward positional relief is a classic clinical pearl — mention it explicitly.** When summarising your reasons for the diagnosis, explicitly state that the positional change (better leaning forward, worse lying flat) is a characteristic feature of retroperitoneal pancreatic inflammation. This demonstrates clinical depth.

- **Acknowledge what is normal in investigations.** Telling the patient "everything else in your tests is normal, which is reassuring" is both good clinical communication and good AMC exam practice. It demonstrates completeness and reduces patient anxiety.

- **Back pain radiation is a high-yield diagnostic feature.** If the history yields pain radiating to the back in a band-like pattern, explicitly call this out as supporting your diagnosis of pancreatitis during your summary to the patient and the examiner.

- **Do not skip ICE under time pressure.** Even if time is short, a brief "Is there anything particular worrying you about this?" will yield marks for the communication domain and often surfaces clinically important concerns (fear of cancer, alcohol stigma) that can be addressed with a focused response.

- **Safety netting must be specific, not generic.** "Come back if you feel worse" is insufficient. Specify the red flags — worsening abdominal pain despite analgesia, fever and rigors, jaundice, inability to take any fluids — and communicate the expected follow-up timeline.

- **If asked about prognosis, frame it honestly and positively.** Mild acute pancreatitis (the majority of cases) carries an excellent prognosis with appropriate management and removal of the causative agent. Communicate that recurrence is largely preventable by addressing the underlying cause.

## Safety Net

Before discharge or at the end of the consultation, the following specific safety net instructions must be communicated clearly and documented:

**Return immediately to the emergency department if:**
- Pain is worsening or becoming uncontrollable despite prescribed analgesia
- Fever develops (temperature above 38°C) or you experience rigors (shaking chills)
- You develop yellowing of the skin or eyes (jaundice)
- You are unable to tolerate any fluids by mouth
- You feel faint, lightheaded, or your condition deteriorates rapidly
- You develop confusion or unusual behaviour

**Expected follow-up:**
- Hospital admission is required for this diagnosis — outpatient management of acute pancreatitis requiring IV fluids and analgesia is not appropriate
- Follow-up with gastroenterology within 4–6 weeks of discharge
- Repeat abdominal ultrasound if gallstones were not definitively excluded during admission
- Fasting lipid profile to assess for hypertriglyceridaemia as an aetiological or contributory factor
- GP review within 1 week of discharge for symptom review, LFT recheck, and blood glucose monitoring

**Lifestyle and behaviour modification:**
- Complete alcohol cessation is strongly advised, particularly if alcohol was identified as the precipitant; recurrent alcohol-related pancreatitis substantially increases the risk of chronic pancreatitis and pancreatic cancer
- Low-fat diet to reduce biliary stimulation and recurrence risk
- Smoking cessation if applicable — smoking is an independent risk factor for chronic pancreatitis and pancreatic malignancy

**Written resources:**
- Provide written information sheet on acute pancreatitis (Gastroenterological Society of Australia patient resources)
- Provide contact details for Alcohol and Drug Services (ADS) or DirectLine (national alcohol and drug helpline: 1800 250 015) if relevant
- Provide emergency department return-to-care instructions in writing
